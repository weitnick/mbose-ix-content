## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction — the idea of the nation

In 1848, the French artist **Frédéric Sorrieu** prepared a series of four prints visualising his dream of a world made up of **"democratic and social Republics"**. In the first print, the peoples of Europe and America — men and women of all ages and classes — march in a long train and offer homage to the **Statue of Liberty**. On the earth in front of them lie the shattered remains of the symbols of **absolutist** institutions. Each nation is identified by its flag and national costume.

During the nineteenth century, **nationalism** emerged as a force that brought sweeping changes in the political and mental world of Europe. The result was the emergence of the **nation-state** in place of the multi-national dynastic empires of Europe.

> **Nation-state:** a state in which the majority of citizens, not only the rulers, come to develop a sense of **common identity and shared history or descent**. This identity was forged through struggles and the actions of leaders and common people.

> **Ernst Renan, "What is a Nation?" (1882):** a nation is the culmination of a long past of endeavours, sacrifice and devotion; it is a large-scale solidarity, and its existence is "a daily plebiscite". A nation is not formed by a common language, race, religion or territory alone.

### The French Revolution and the idea of the nation

The first clear expression of nationalism came with the **French Revolution in 1789**. Political and constitutional changes led to the **transfer of sovereignty** from the monarchy to a body of **French citizens**. The revolution proclaimed that it was the **people** who would constitute the nation and shape its destiny.

**Measures taken by the French revolutionaries to create a sense of collective identity:**

1. The ideas of **la patrie** (the fatherland) and **le citoyen** (the citizen) stressed a **united community enjoying equal rights** under a constitution.
2. A new **French flag, the tricolour**, replaced the former royal standard.
3. The **Estates General** was elected by the body of active citizens and renamed the **National Assembly**.
4. **New hymns** were composed, **oaths** taken and **martyrs commemorated**, all in the name of the nation.
5. A **centralised administrative system** was put in place, formulating **uniform laws** for all citizens within its territory.
6. **Internal customs duties and dues were abolished**, and a **uniform system of weights and measures** was adopted.
7. **Regional dialects were discouraged**, and **French**, as spoken and written in Paris, became the **common language** of the nation.

The revolutionaries also declared that it was the **mission of the French nation to liberate the peoples of Europe from despotism**. Students and members of the educated middle classes in Holland, Belgium, Switzerland and much of Italy set up **Jacobin clubs**, whose activities prepared the way for the French armies.

#### Napoleon and the Civil Code

**Napoleon** destroyed democracy in France (he made himself emperor), but in the administrative field he brought in revolutionary principles to make the system more rational and efficient.

**The Civil Code of 1804 (the Napoleonic Code):**

- did away with all **privileges based on birth**;
- established **equality before the law**;
- secured the **right to property**.

**Other changes in the territories under his control** (Dutch Republic, Switzerland, Italy, Germany):

- He **simplified administrative divisions** and **abolished the feudal system**.
- He **freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues**.
- In towns, **guild restrictions were removed**.
- **Transport and communication systems were improved**.
- A **uniform law, standardised weights and measures and a common national currency** helped the movement and exchange of goods and capital.

Peasants, artisans, workers and new businessmen enjoyed this new freedom. But soon the initial enthusiasm turned to **hostility**: the new administrative arrangements did not go hand in hand with political freedom. **Increased taxation, censorship and forced conscription** into the French armies to conquer the rest of Europe outweighed the advantages.

### The making of nationalism in Europe

In the mid-eighteenth century there were **no nation-states** as we know them. Germany, Italy and Switzerland were divided into **kingdoms, duchies and cantons** whose rulers had their autonomous territories. Eastern and Central Europe was under **autocratic monarchies**. People did not see themselves as sharing a collective identity; they often spoke different languages and belonged to different ethnic groups.

> **Example — the Habsburg Empire (Austria-Hungary):** it included the Alpine regions (Tyrol, Austria, the Sudetenland) and Bohemia, where the aristocracy was mainly German-speaking. It also included the Italian-speaking provinces of **Lombardy and Venetia**. In **Hungary**, half the population spoke **Magyar** and the other half a variety of dialects; in **Galicia**, the aristocracy spoke **Polish**. There were also Bohemians, Slovaks, Slovenes, Croats and Roumans. The only tie binding these groups was **common allegiance to the emperor**.

#### The aristocracy and the new middle class

- The **landed aristocracy** was the dominant class, socially and politically. Its members were united by a **common way of life** across regions: they owned estates and town houses, spoke **French** for diplomacy and in high society, and were connected by marriage. Numerically, however, they were a **small group**.
- The majority of the population were **peasants**: in the west, mostly **tenants and small owners**; in Eastern and Central Europe, the land was cultivated by **serfs**.
- **Industrialisation** began in England in the second half of the 18th century, and in France and parts of the German states in the 19th century. It brought a **new middle class** — **industrialists, businessmen and professionals**.
- It was among the **educated, liberal middle classes** that ideas of **national unity** following the abolition of aristocratic privileges gained popularity.

#### What did liberal nationalism stand for?

The term **liberalism** comes from the Latin root **liber**, meaning **free**. For the new middle classes it stood for **freedom for the individual and equality of all before the law**.

**Politically**, liberalism stood for:

- government by **consent**;
- the end of **autocracy and clerical privileges**;
- a **constitution** and **representative government** through parliament;
- the **inviolability of private property**.

But equality before the law did not mean universal suffrage. In revolutionary France, the right to vote and to get elected was granted **only to property-owning men**. Under the Napoleonic Code, **women** were made subject to the authority of fathers and husbands, and **men without property and all women were excluded** from political rights. Women and non-propertied men organised opposition movements demanding equal political rights.

**Economically**, liberalism stood for:

- **freedom of markets**;
- the **abolition of state-imposed restrictions** on the movement of goods and capital.

In the German regions, there were **39 states**, each with its own currency, weights and measures. A merchant travelling from Hamburg to Nuremberg had to pass through **11 customs barriers** and pay a customs duty of about 5% at each one. Such barriers obstructed trade.

> **Zollverein (1834):** a **customs union** formed at the initiative of **Prussia** and joined by most of the German states. It **abolished tariff barriers** and **reduced the number of currencies from over thirty to two**. The creation of a **network of railways** further stimulated mobility. Economic nationalism strengthened the wider nationalist sentiments.

#### A new conservatism after 1815

After the defeat of **Napoleon in 1815**, European governments were driven by **conservatism**: the belief that **established, traditional institutions** of state and society — the **monarchy, the Church, social hierarchies, property and the family** — should be preserved. Most conservatives did not propose a return to pre-revolutionary society. They realised that **modernisation** (an efficient bureaucracy, a dynamic economy, the abolition of feudalism and serfdom) could in fact **strengthen** traditional institutions such as the monarchy.

**The Treaty of Vienna (1815).** Representatives of the European powers — **Britain, Russia, Prussia and Austria** — who had defeated Napoleon met at **Vienna**. The Congress was hosted by the Austrian Chancellor **Duke Metternich**. Its main aim was to **undo most of the changes** brought about in Europe during the Napoleonic wars.

**Main clauses of the Treaty of Vienna:**

1. The **Bourbon dynasty**, deposed during the French Revolution, was **restored to power** in France.
2. France **lost the territories** it had annexed under Napoleon.
3. A series of states was set up on the **boundaries of France** to prevent future French expansion: the **Kingdom of the Netherlands**, which included **Belgium**, in the north, and **Genoa** added to **Piedmont** in the south.
4. **Prussia** was given important **new territories on its western frontiers**, and **Austria** was given **control of northern Italy**.
5. The **German Confederation of 39 states**, set up by Napoleon, was **left untouched**.
6. In the east, **Russia** was given **part of Poland**, while **Prussia** was given a **portion of Saxony**.

The conservative regimes of 1815 were **autocratic**. They did not tolerate criticism and dissent, and imposed **censorship** on newspapers, books, plays and songs that reflected the ideas of liberty and freedom associated with the French Revolution.

#### The revolutionaries

Fear of repression drove many liberal-nationalists underground. **Secret societies** sprang up in many European states to train revolutionaries and spread their ideas. Being revolutionary at this time meant a commitment to **opposing monarchical forms** set up after the Vienna Congress and to **fighting for liberty and freedom**. Most revolutionaries also saw the creation of **nation-states** as a necessary part of this struggle.

> **Giuseppe Mazzini (born in Genoa, 1807)** became a member of the secret society of the **Carbonari**. At the age of 24 he was sent into exile in 1831 for attempting a revolution in Liguria. He founded two more underground societies: **Young Italy** in **Marseilles** and **Young Europe** in **Berne**, whose members were like-minded young men from Poland, France, Italy and the German states. Mazzini believed that God had intended nations to be the natural units of mankind, so Italy could not continue to be a patchwork of small states and kingdoms; it had to be **forged into a single unified republic**. Following his model, secret societies were set up in Germany, France, Switzerland and Poland. **Metternich** described him as **"the most dangerous enemy of our social order"**.

### The age of revolutions: 1830–1848

As conservative regimes tried to consolidate their power, **liberalism and nationalism** came to be associated with **revolution** in many regions — the Italian and German states, the provinces of the Ottoman Empire, Ireland and Poland. These revolutions were led by **liberal-nationalists** from the **educated middle-class elite** (professors, school teachers, clerks and members of the commercial middle classes).

- **July 1830, France:** the **Bourbon kings**, restored in 1815, were **overthrown** by liberal revolutionaries, who installed a **constitutional monarchy** with **Louis Philippe** at its head. Metternich remarked: **"When France sneezes, the rest of Europe catches cold."** The July Revolution sparked an uprising in **Brussels**, which led to **Belgium breaking away** from the United Kingdom of the Netherlands.
- **The Greek war of independence (from 1821).** Greece had been part of the **Ottoman Empire** since the fifteenth century. The growth of revolutionary nationalism in Europe sparked off a struggle for independence among the Greeks. Nationalists in Greece got support from **Greeks living in exile** and from many **West Europeans** who sympathised with ancient Greek culture. Poets and artists hailed Greece as the **cradle of European civilisation**; the English poet **Lord Byron** organised funds and went to fight in the war, where he died of fever in 1824. Finally, the **Treaty of Constantinople of 1832** recognised Greece as an **independent nation**.

#### The romantic imagination and national feeling

Culture played an important role in creating the idea of the nation: **art, poetry, stories and music** helped express and shape nationalist feelings.

- **Romanticism** was a cultural movement that sought to develop a particular form of **nationalist sentiment**. Romantic artists and poets criticised the glorification of reason and science and focused instead on **emotions, intuition and mystical feelings**. They tried to create a sense of a **shared collective heritage**, a common cultural past, as the basis of a nation.
- The German philosopher **Johann Gottfried Herder** claimed that true German culture was to be discovered among the **common people — das volk**. It was through **folk songs, folk poetry and folk dances** that the true spirit of the nation (volksgeist) was popularised.
- **Language** also played an important role. After **Russian occupation**, the **Polish language** was forced out of schools and **Russian** was imposed everywhere. In **1831**, an armed rebellion against Russian rule took place, which was ultimately crushed. Many members of the **clergy** in Poland used Polish for Church gatherings and religious instruction; the use of Polish came to be seen as a **symbol of the struggle against Russian dominance**.
- **Music:** **Karol Kurpiński** celebrated the national struggle through his **operas and music**, turning folk dances like the **polonaise and mazurka** into nationalist symbols.
- **The Grimm brothers** (Jacob and Wilhelm, from Hanau) collected **folktales** and published them in **1812**. They saw French domination as a threat to German culture and believed that collecting folktales was part of the effort to **oppose French domination and create a German national identity**.

#### Hunger, hardship and popular revolt

The **1830s** were years of great **economic hardship** in Europe:

- an enormous **increase in population**, with more seekers of jobs than employment;
- **migration** from rural areas to overcrowded **urban slums**;
- **small producers** in towns facing stiff competition from cheap **machine-made goods from England**;
- in regions where the aristocracy still enjoyed power, **peasants** struggling under the burden of **feudal dues and obligations**;
- a rise in **food prices** or a year of bad harvest leading to widespread **pauperism** in town and country.

**Paris, 1848:** food shortages and widespread unemployment brought the population of Paris out on the roads. **Barricades** were erected and **Louis Philippe** was forced to flee. A **National Assembly** proclaimed a **Republic**, granted **suffrage to all adult males above 21**, and guaranteed the **right to work**. **National workshops** were set up to provide employment.

**Silesia, 1845:** **weavers** led a revolt against **contractors** who supplied them raw material and gave them orders for finished textiles but **drastically reduced their payments**. The revolt was suppressed by the army.

#### 1848: the revolution of the liberals

Parallel to the revolts of the poor, a revolution led by the **educated middle classes** was under way in 1848. Men and women of the liberal middle classes combined their demands for **constitutionalism** with **national unification**. They demanded the creation of a **nation-state** on parliamentary principles — a **constitution, freedom of the press and freedom of association**.

**The Frankfurt Parliament (German regions):**

1. Large numbers of political associations, whose members were middle-class professionals, businessmen and prosperous artisans, came together in the city of **Frankfurt** and decided to vote for an **all-German National Assembly**.
2. On **18 May 1848**, **831 elected representatives** marched in a festive procession to take their places in the Frankfurt Parliament convened in the **Church of St Paul**.
3. They drafted a **constitution for a German nation** to be headed by a **monarchy subject to a parliament**.
4. When the deputies offered the crown to **Friedrich Wilhelm IV, King of Prussia**, he **rejected it** and joined other monarchs to oppose the elected assembly.
5. The parliament was **dominated by the middle classes**, who resisted the demands of **workers and artisans** and so lost their support. In the end, **troops were called in** and the assembly was **forced to disband**.

**Women** had actively participated in the liberal movement, forming their own political associations and newspapers. Yet they were **denied suffrage** during the election of the assembly; in the Church of St Paul they were admitted **only as observers in the visitors' gallery**.

**Significance of 1848.** Although conservative forces were able to suppress the liberal movements in 1848, they **could not restore the old order**. Monarchs began to realise that cycles of revolution could be ended only by **granting concessions** to the liberal-nationalist revolutionaries. So, in the years after 1848, the autocratic monarchies of Central and Eastern Europe introduced changes: **serfdom and bonded labour were abolished** both in the Habsburg dominions and in Russia, and in **1867** the Habsburg rulers granted **more autonomy to the Hungarians**.

### The making of Germany and Italy

After 1848, nationalism in Europe moved away from its association with democracy and revolution. Nationalist sentiments were often mobilised by **conservatives** to promote state power and achieve political domination over Europe.

![Fig. 1.2 The unification of Germany and of Italy](figures/ss_ch01_fig02_unification.png)

#### Germany — can the army be the architect of a nation?

- Nationalist feelings were widespread among **middle-class Germans**, who in 1848 tried to unite the different regions of the German Confederation into a nation-state governed by an elected parliament. This liberal initiative was **repressed** by the combined forces of the monarchy and the military, supported by the large landowners (**Junkers**) of Prussia.
- From then on, **Prussia** took on the leadership of the movement for national unification. Its chief minister, **Otto von Bismarck**, was the **architect** of this process, carried out with the help of the **Prussian army and bureaucracy**.
- **Three wars over seven years** — with **Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71)** — ended in Prussian victory and completed unification.
- In **January 1871**, the Prussian king **William I** was proclaimed **German Emperor** in a ceremony held at **Versailles** (in the Hall of Mirrors).
- The new state placed strong emphasis on **modernising the currency, banking, and the legal and judicial systems**; Prussian measures and practices often became a model for the rest of Germany.

#### Italy unified

- Like Germany, Italy had a long history of **political fragmentation**. In the middle of the nineteenth century it was divided into **seven states**, of which only one, **Sardinia-Piedmont**, was ruled by an **Italian princely house**.
  - The **north** was under the **Austrian Habsburgs**.
  - The **centre** was ruled by the **Pope**.
  - The **southern regions** were under the domination of the **Bourbon kings of Spain**.
  - Even the **Italian language** had not acquired one common form and still had many regional and local variations.
- During the 1830s, **Mazzini** had sought to put together a coherent programme for a unitary Italian republic and formed **Young Italy**. The failure of revolutionary uprisings in **1831 and 1848** meant that the mantle now fell on **Sardinia-Piedmont** under its ruler **King Victor Emmanuel II** to unify the Italian states through war.
- **Count Camillo de Cavour**, the chief minister who led the movement, was **neither a revolutionary nor a democrat**. Like many other wealthy and educated members of the Italian elite, he **spoke French much better than Italian**.
- Through a **tactful diplomatic alliance with France** engineered by Cavour, Sardinia-Piedmont succeeded in **defeating the Austrian forces in 1859**.
- A large number of **armed volunteers** under **Giuseppe Garibaldi** joined the fray. In **1860**, they marched into **South Italy and the Kingdom of the Two Sicilies** and succeeded in **driving out the Spanish rulers** with the support of the local peasants. Garibaldi's volunteers were called the **Red Shirts**.
- In **1861**, **Victor Emmanuel II** was proclaimed **king of united Italy**. **Rome** joined the kingdom in **1870**.
- Much of the Italian population, among whom rates of illiteracy were very high, remained **unaware of liberal-nationalist ideology**. The peasant masses who supported Garibaldi in southern Italy had never heard of Italia; some believed that "La Talia" was Victor Emmanuel's wife!

#### The strange case of Britain

In Britain, the formation of the nation-state was **not the result of a sudden upheaval or revolution**, but of a **long-drawn-out process**.

1. There was **no British nation** before the eighteenth century. The inhabitants of the British Isles — **English, Welsh, Scots and Irish** — had their own cultural and political traditions.
2. The **English nation** steadily grew in wealth, importance and power and extended its influence over the other nations. The **English parliament**, which had seized power from the monarchy in **1688** (after a long conflict), was the instrument through which a nation-state with England at its centre came to be forged.
3. The **Act of Union (1707)** between England and Scotland resulted in the formation of the **United Kingdom of Great Britain**, meaning in effect that **England was able to impose its influence on Scotland**. Scotland's distinctive culture and political institutions were systematically suppressed: the Catholic clans of the Scottish Highlands were repressed, and they were forbidden to speak their **Gaelic language** or wear their national dress.
4. **Ireland** was deeply divided between **Catholics and Protestants**. The English helped the Protestants establish dominance over a largely Catholic country. A revolt led by **Wolfe Tone and his United Irishmen (1798)** failed, and Ireland was **forcibly incorporated** into the United Kingdom in **1801**.
5. The symbols of the new Britain — the **British flag (Union Jack)**, the **national anthem (God Save Our Noble King)** and the **English language** — were actively promoted, and the older nations survived only as subordinate partners in this union.

#### Visualising the nation *(internal assessment only)*

Artists represented nations as **female figures** (allegories). In France, the nation was christened **Marianne**, a popular Christian name, with characteristics drawn from those of Liberty and the Republic — the **red cap, the tricolour and the cockade**. Her statues were erected in public squares and her image was marked on coins and stamps. **Germania** became the allegory of the German nation, wearing a **crown of oak leaves**, as the German oak stands for **heroism**. *(MBOSE has placed this portion, "Portrayal of a Nation", under internal assessment only.)*

### Nationalism and imperialism

By the last quarter of the nineteenth century, nationalism **no longer retained the idealistic liberal-democratic sentiment** of the first half of the century. It became a **narrow creed with limited ends**. Nationalist groups became increasingly **intolerant** of each other and ever ready to go to war. The major European powers manipulated the nationalist aspirations of subject peoples in Europe to further their own **imperialist aims**.

**The Balkans** — the most serious source of nationalist tension in Europe after 1871:

1. The region comprised modern-day **Romania, Bulgaria, Albania, Greece, Macedonia, Croatia, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Slovenia, Serbia and Montenegro**, whose inhabitants were broadly known as the **Slavs**.
2. A large part of the Balkans was under the control of the **Ottoman Empire**. The spread of the ideas of **romantic nationalism**, together with the **disintegration of the Ottoman Empire**, made the region very explosive.
3. Through the nineteenth century, the Ottoman Empire tried to strengthen itself through **modernisation and internal reforms**, but with very little success.
4. One by one, its European subject nationalities broke away and declared independence. The Balkan peoples based their claims on **nationality** and used **history** to prove that they had once been independent but had subsequently been subjugated by foreign powers.
5. As the different Slavic nationalities struggled to define their identity and independence, the Balkan area became an area of **intense conflict**. The Balkan states were fiercely **jealous of each other**, and each hoped to gain more territory at the expense of the others.
6. The **big powers** — **Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary** — competed for control over the Balkans, keen on countering the hold of the others and extending their own control. This led to a series of wars in the region and finally to the **First World War (1914)**.

Meanwhile, many countries in the world that had been **colonised** by European powers in the nineteenth century began to oppose imperial domination. The anti-imperial movements that developed everywhere were nationalist, in the sense that they struggled to form **independent nation-states**. Thus the idea that societies should be organised into nation-states came to be accepted as **natural and universal**.

### Summary of the chapter

1. The French Revolution (1789) transferred sovereignty to the people and used flags, hymns, uniform laws and a common language to create a national identity; Napoleon's Civil Code (1804) spread equality before the law and the right to property.
2. Liberal nationalism stood for freedom of the individual, constitutional government and free markets (the Zollverein, 1834); conservatism after 1815 (Treaty of Vienna) tried to undo Napoleon's changes.
3. Secret societies (Mazzini's Young Italy) and the revolutions of 1830 and 1848 spread nationalism; culture (Romanticism, folk traditions, language, music) shaped national feeling.
4. Germany was unified by Prussia under Bismarck through wars (1871); Italy by Sardinia-Piedmont under Victor Emmanuel II, Cavour and Garibaldi (1861; Rome 1870); Britain developed through a long process (1688, 1707, 1801).
5. After 1871, nationalism became narrow and aggressive; tensions in the Balkans led to the First World War.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas

- **People make the nation.** Nationhood rests on a shared identity built through symbols, language, history and struggle.
- **Culture builds identity.** Folk songs, stories, language and art helped people imagine their nation.
- **Nationalism can unite or divide.** It inspired freedom movements, but in narrow form it caused rivalry and war.

### 7.2 Real-life connections

- **India's national symbols** — the tricolour, the national anthem and the emblem — play the same uniting role as the French tricolour did in 1789.
- **Language and identity in Meghalaya:** the Khasi and Garo languages, folk tales and songs (such as the stories of U Manik Raitong) keep a shared cultural heritage alive, much as the Grimm brothers' collections did in Germany.
- **Meghalaya's own statehood (1972)** was won through a peaceful, democratic movement (the All Party Hill Leaders' Conference) — a contrast with Germany's unification "by iron and blood".
- **The European Union** today removes customs barriers and uses a common currency, much as the Zollverein did for the German states in 1834.

### 8.1 Textbook questions — solutions

*(The Goyal book's exercises were not available; these are the standard NCERT exercises on this topic.)*

#### Write in brief

**Q1.** Write a note on: (a) Giuseppe Mazzini (b) Count Camillo de Cavour (c) The Greek war of independence (d) The Frankfurt Parliament (e) The role of women in nationalist struggles.
**Answer:**
(a) **Mazzini** — an Italian revolutionary born in Genoa (1807); a member of the Carbonari, exiled in 1831; founded **Young Italy** (Marseilles) and **Young Europe** (Berne); believed nations were the natural units of mankind and Italy must become a united republic; Metternich called him "the most dangerous enemy of our social order".
(b) **Cavour** — chief minister of Sardinia-Piedmont; neither a revolutionary nor a democrat; spoke French better than Italian; built a diplomatic alliance with **France** through which Sardinia-Piedmont **defeated Austria in 1859**, opening the way to Italian unification.
(c) **Greek war of independence** — Greece had been under the **Ottoman Empire** since the 15th century; a struggle for independence began in **1821**, supported by Greeks in exile and by West Europeans who admired ancient Greek culture (Lord Byron died fighting); the **Treaty of Constantinople (1832)** recognised Greece as independent.
(d) **Frankfurt Parliament** — an all-German National Assembly of **831 elected representatives** that met on **18 May 1848** in the **Church of St Paul**; it drafted a constitution for a German nation under a monarchy subject to parliament, but **Friedrich Wilhelm IV of Prussia rejected the crown**; it lost the support of workers and was disbanded by troops.
(e) **Women** — took an active part in liberal and nationalist movements, forming **political associations, newspapers** and taking part in meetings; yet they were **denied the vote** (in Frankfurt they could only watch from the visitors' gallery); they continued to demand equal political rights.

**Q2.** What steps did the French revolutionaries take to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?
**Answer:** (i) They introduced the ideas of **la patrie** and **le citoyen**. (ii) A new **tricolour flag** replaced the royal standard. (iii) The Estates General was elected by active citizens and renamed the **National Assembly**. (iv) **New hymns, oaths and commemoration of martyrs** in the name of the nation. (v) A **centralised administration with uniform laws**; internal customs duties abolished; a uniform system of **weights and measures**. (vi) **French as spoken in Paris** made the common language, discouraging regional dialects.

**Q3.** Who were Marianne and Germania? What was the importance of the way in which they were portrayed? *(internal assessment only)*
**Answer:** **Marianne** was the female allegory of the French nation, shown with the **red cap, the tricolour and the cockade** (symbols of liberty and the republic); **Germania** was the allegory of the German nation, wearing a **crown of oak leaves** (heroism). Such portrayals gave the abstract idea of the nation a **concrete, recognisable form** and appeared on statues, coins and stamps, helping people identify with the nation.

**Q4.** Briefly trace the process of German unification.
**Answer:** (i) Middle-class liberals tried to unite Germany through the **Frankfurt Parliament (1848)**, but it was suppressed by the monarchy, military and Junkers. (ii) **Prussia** then led unification under its chief minister **Otto von Bismarck**, using the **Prussian army and bureaucracy**. (iii) **Three wars over seven years** — with Denmark, Austria and France — ended in Prussian victory. (iv) In **January 1871**, **William I** was proclaimed **German Emperor at Versailles**. (v) The new state modernised its currency, banking, legal and judicial systems.

**Q5.** What changes did Napoleon introduce to make the administrative system more efficient in the territories ruled by him?
**Answer:** (i) The **Civil Code of 1804** — abolished privileges based on birth, established **equality before the law** and secured the **right to property**. (ii) **Simplified administrative divisions**. (iii) **Abolished the feudal system** and freed peasants from serfdom and manorial dues. (iv) Removed **guild restrictions** in towns. (v) Improved **transport and communication**. (vi) Introduced **uniform laws, standardised weights and measures and a common currency**.

#### Discuss

**Q6.** Explain what is meant by the 1848 revolution of the liberals. What were the political, social and economic ideas supported by the liberals?
**Answer:** In **1848**, alongside revolts of the poor, the **educated middle classes** in many parts of Europe led a revolution demanding a **nation-state on parliamentary principles** — a constitution, freedom of the press and of association (e.g. the Frankfurt Parliament).
**Political ideas:** government by consent, end of autocracy and clerical privileges, a constitution and representative government.
**Social ideas:** freedom of the individual, equality before the law, abolition of aristocratic privileges, serfdom and bonded labour.
**Economic ideas:** freedom of markets, abolition of state restrictions on the movement of goods and capital, protection of private property (e.g. the Zollverein).

**Q7.** Choose three examples to show the contribution of culture to the growth of nationalism in Europe.
**Answer:** (i) **Romanticism and folk culture** — Herder said the true German spirit lay among the common people (das volk), expressed in folk songs, poetry and dances. (ii) **Language** — after the Russian occupation of Poland, the use of **Polish** in churches became a symbol of resistance to Russian dominance. (iii) **Music and folktales** — Karol Kurpiński's operas turned the polonaise and mazurka into national symbols, and the **Grimm brothers' folktales** (1812) helped build a German identity against French domination.

**Q8.** Through a focus on any two countries, explain how nations developed over the nineteenth century.
**Answer:** **Germany:** the liberal attempt at unification (Frankfurt, 1848) failed; **Prussia under Bismarck** united the German states through three wars, and **William I** became German Emperor in **1871** — unification from above, by the army and bureaucracy.
**Italy:** divided into seven states; Mazzini's revolutionary attempts failed (1831, 1848); **Sardinia-Piedmont** under **Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour** defeated Austria (1859) with French help; **Garibaldi's Red Shirts** freed the south (1860); Italy was united in **1861** and Rome joined in **1870**.

**Q9.** How was the history of nationalism in Britain unlike the rest of Europe?
**Answer:** In Britain, the nation-state was not formed through a sudden revolution but by a **long-drawn-out process**. The English parliament, which had seized power from the monarchy in **1688**, became the instrument for building a nation centred on **England**. The **Act of Union (1707)** joined England and Scotland, and Scottish culture was suppressed; **Ireland** was forcibly incorporated in **1801** after the failed revolt of 1798. A new British identity was promoted through the **Union Jack, the national anthem and the English language** — the dominance of one ethnic group over the others.

**Q10.** Why did nationalist tensions emerge in the Balkans?
**Answer:** (i) The Balkans had many **ethnic groups (Slavs)** under the weakening **Ottoman Empire**. (ii) **Romantic nationalism** and the **disintegration of the Ottoman Empire** made the region explosive, as subject nationalities broke away. (iii) The Balkan states were **jealous of each other** and wanted more territory. (iv) The **big powers — Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary** — competed for control to further their **imperialist** aims. These rivalries led to wars and finally to the **First World War**.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 marks)

### Short answers (2 marks each)

**Q1.** What is meant by a nation-state?
**Answer:** A state in which most citizens, not only rulers, share a common identity and history or descent.

**Q2.** Mention any two provisions of the Napoleonic Code of 1804.
**Answer:** It abolished privileges based on birth and established equality before the law; it secured the right to property.

**Q3.** What was the Zollverein? State one of its effects.
**Answer:** A customs union of German states (1834) led by Prussia. It abolished tariff barriers and reduced currencies from over thirty to two.

**Q4.** What did liberalism stand for politically?
**Answer:** Government by consent, a constitution and representative government, and an end to autocracy and clerical privileges.

**Q5.** What was the main aim of the Treaty of Vienna?
**Answer:** To undo the changes made during the Napoleonic wars and restore conservative monarchies such as the Bourbons.

**Q6.** Who was Giuseppe Mazzini?
**Answer:** An Italian revolutionary of the Carbonari who founded Young Italy and Young Europe to fight for a united Italian republic.

**Q7.** Why is 1830 important in European history?
**Answer:** The July Revolution overthrew the Bourbons in France and inspired Belgium to break away from the Netherlands.

**Q8.** How did Romanticism contribute to nationalism?
**Answer:** It stressed emotions and a shared cultural past, using folk songs, poetry and dances to express the nation's spirit.

**Q9.** Why was the Frankfurt Parliament a failure?
**Answer:** The Prussian king rejected its crown, and its middle-class members lost the support of workers and artisans.

**Q10.** State two economic hardships faced by Europeans in the 1830s.
**Answer:** Population growth caused unemployment and migration to slums; rising food prices and bad harvests caused widespread poverty.

**Q11.** Who were the Junkers? What role did they play?
**Answer:** The large landowners of Prussia; they supported the monarchy and the military that crushed the liberals in 1848.

**Q12.** How did language become a weapon of resistance in Poland?
**Answer:** When Russian was imposed, the clergy used Polish in churches; speaking Polish became a symbol of resistance.

**Q13.** Name the three wars through which Prussia unified Germany.
**Answer:** Wars with Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71).

**Q14.** What was the role of Garibaldi in Italian unification?
**Answer:** He led the Red Shirts into southern Italy in 1860 and drove out the Spanish rulers of the Two Sicilies.

**Q15.** What was the Act of Union of 1707?
**Answer:** The union of England and Scotland into the United Kingdom of Great Britain, under English dominance.

**Q16.** Why did nationalism after 1871 become a narrow creed?
**Answer:** Nationalist groups became intolerant and ready for war, and big powers used nationalism for imperialist aims.

**Q17.** Did the Treaty of Vienna maintain peace in Europe? Give two reasons.
**Answer:** Largely yes: it restored a balance of power and prevented a major war for about four decades.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (5 marks)

### Long answers (5 marks each)

**Q1.** What steps did the French revolutionaries take to create a sense of collective identity among the French people?
**Answer:** They introduced the ideas of la patrie (fatherland) and le citoyen (citizen).
A new tricolour flag replaced the royal standard.
The Estates General became the elected National Assembly; hymns, oaths and martyrs' commemoration followed.
A centralised administration made uniform laws, abolished internal customs duties and adopted uniform weights and measures.
French as spoken in Paris became the common language.

**Q2.** Describe the main clauses of the Treaty of Vienna of 1815.
**Answer:** The Bourbon dynasty was restored in France.
France lost the territories it had annexed under Napoleon.
Buffer states were set up: the Netherlands (with Belgium) in the north, and Genoa added to Piedmont in the south.
Prussia got western territories and Austria got northern Italy; Russia got part of Poland and Prussia part of Saxony.
The German Confederation of 39 states was left untouched.

**Q3.** Briefly explain the process of unification of Italy.
**Answer:** Italy was divided into seven states; only Sardinia-Piedmont had an Italian ruler.
Mazzini's Young Italy failed in 1831 and 1848.
Victor Emmanuel II and Cavour allied with France and defeated Austria in 1859.
Garibaldi's Red Shirts took southern Italy and the Two Sicilies in 1860.
Victor Emmanuel II became king of united Italy in 1861; Rome joined in 1870.

**Q4.** Describe the process of German unification.
**Answer:** Middle-class liberals tried to unite Germany through the Frankfurt Parliament in 1848.
It was crushed by the monarchy, the army and the Junkers.
Prussia then took the lead under its chief minister Otto von Bismarck.
Three wars with Denmark, Austria and France ended in Prussian victory.
In January 1871, William I was proclaimed German Emperor at Versailles.

**Q5.** What were the political, social and economic ideas of the liberals in 1848?
**Answer:** The educated middle classes demanded a nation-state on parliamentary principles.
Politically: government by consent, a constitution and representative government.
They also wanted freedom of the press and of association.
Socially: equality before the law; an end to aristocratic privileges, serfdom and bonded labour.
Economically: free markets, no state restrictions on goods and capital, and protection of property.

**Q6.** Explain with three examples how culture helped the growth of nationalism in Europe.
**Answer:** Romanticism stressed emotions and a shared past; Herder found the nation's spirit in das volk.
Folk songs, poetry and dances expressed this spirit.
In Poland, using the Polish language against Russian rule became a symbol of resistance.
Karol Kurpiński's operas turned the polonaise and mazurka into national symbols.
The Grimm brothers' folktales (1812) built a German identity against French domination.

**Q7.** How was the formation of the nation-state in Britain different from the rest of Europe?
**Answer:** It came through a long process, not a sudden revolution.
The English parliament, powerful after 1688, built a nation centred on England.
The Act of Union (1707) joined Scotland, whose Gaelic language and dress were suppressed.
Ireland, divided between Catholics and Protestants, was forcibly incorporated in 1801.
The Union Jack, the national anthem and the English language were promoted as British symbols.

**Q8.** Why did the Balkans become a source of nationalist tension after 1871?
**Answer:** The Balkans had many Slav nationalities under the declining Ottoman Empire.
Romantic nationalism made them break away and claim independence, using history to back their claims.
The Balkan states were jealous of each other and wanted more territory.
Russia, Germany, England and Austro-Hungary competed for control to serve their imperialist aims.
These rivalries caused a series of wars and finally the First World War.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (A school flag and anthem)** A new school in Jowai wants its students from different villages to feel like one community. Using the French Revolution as an example, suggest three steps.
**Model answer:** Adopt a common school flag and song, as France adopted the tricolour and hymns; hold shared ceremonies and pledges; and use a common language of instruction alongside respect for local languages, so all feel part of one school community.

**Q2. (Trade without borders)** A merchant had to pay duty at 11 customs barriers between Hamburg and Nuremberg. How did the Zollverein change this, and why did it strengthen nationalism?
**Model answer:** It abolished tariff barriers and reduced currencies to two, so goods moved freely; shared economic interests made Germans feel like one nation.

**Q3. (If Charles Albert had won)** If King Charles Albert of Piedmont had won the Battle of Custozza (1848), how might Italian history have changed?
**Model answer:** Austria's hold on northern Italy might have ended earlier, and Italy might have been united sooner, perhaps under liberal leadership.

**Q4. (Nationalism today)** Give one example from today of healthy national pride and one of narrow nationalism.
**Model answer:** Healthy: celebrating the nation's culture and achievements while respecting others. Narrow: hatred of other nations or communities and readiness for conflict, like the Balkan rivalries before 1914.

**Q5. (Spot the error)** A student writes: "Germany was unified by the Frankfurt Parliament in 1848 under Bismarck." Correct it.
**Model answer:** The Frankfurt Parliament (1848) failed; Germany was unified under Prussia's Bismarck through three wars, completed in 1871.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- 1789: French Revolution — la patrie, le citoyen, tricolour, National Assembly, uniform laws, French language. 1804: Napoleonic Code.
- Liberalism (liber = free): consent, constitution, property, free markets; Zollverein 1834. Conservatism after 1815; Treaty of Vienna (Metternich) undid Napoleon's changes.
- Revolutionaries: Mazzini, Carbonari, Young Italy, Young Europe. 1830 July Revolution; Belgium; Greece (Treaty of Constantinople 1832).
- Culture: Romanticism, Herder's das volk, Polish language, Kurpiński, Grimm brothers. 1845 Silesian weavers; 1848 Paris; Frankfurt Parliament (18 May 1848, 831 deputies).
- Germany: Bismarck, three wars (Denmark, Austria, France), William I Emperor at Versailles 1871. Italy: Cavour, Garibaldi (Red Shirts), Victor Emmanuel II king 1861, Rome 1870. Britain: 1688, 1707, 1801.
- After 1871: narrow nationalism; the Balkans (Slavs, Ottoman decline, big-power rivalry) → First World War.

| Liberals gave votes to all | Only property-owning men could vote. |
