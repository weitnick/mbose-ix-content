## 1. Complete Lesson

### Sectors of economic activities

There are many activities undertaken by people that are directed at producing goods or services. These activities can be grouped into **three sectors** on the basis of the **nature of the activity**.

![Fig. 1.1 Ways of classifying the economy](figures/ss_ch18_fig01_classify.png)

1. **Primary sector.** When we produce a good by **exploiting natural resources**, it is an activity of the primary sector. Cotton is a crop grown on a farm with the help of natural factors like soil, rain and sunlight. **Agriculture, dairy, fishing, forestry and mining** are primary activities. Since most of the natural products we get are from agriculture, dairy, fishing and forestry, this sector is also called **agriculture and related sector**.
2. **Secondary sector.** It covers activities in which **natural products are changed into other forms through ways of manufacturing** that we associate with industrial activity. It is the next step after primary. The product is not produced by nature but has to be **made**, and therefore some process of manufacturing is essential — in a factory, a workshop or at home. Using cotton fibre from the plant, we **spin yarn and weave cloth**; using sugarcane, we **make sugar or gur**. Since this sector gradually became associated with the different kinds of industries that came up, it is also called the **industrial sector**.
3. **Tertiary sector.** These are activities that **help in the development of the primary and secondary sectors**. These activities **by themselves do not produce a good**, but they are an **aid or a support** for the production process. Goods produced need to be **transported** by trucks or trains and sold in shops; they may need to be **stored** in godowns; we may need to talk to others over the **telephone** or send letters (communication) or borrow money from **banks** (banking) to help production and trade. **Transport, storage, communication, banking and trade** are examples. Since these activities generate **services** rather than goods, the tertiary sector is also called the **service sector**. It also includes some essential services that may not directly help in the production of goods — **teachers, doctors, washermen, barbers, cobblers, lawyers** and people who do administrative and accounting work. In recent times, certain new services based on **information technology**, such as internet cafés, ATM booths, call centres and software companies, have become important.

#### The sectors depend on each other

The three sectors are **interdependent**. Cotton is grown on a farm (primary), spun and woven into cloth in a mill (secondary), and then transported and sold in shops (tertiary). The primary sector **supplies raw materials** to the secondary sector; the secondary sector **processes them into finished goods**; the tertiary sector **transports, stores, finances and sells** them. If farmers refuse to sell sugarcane to a sugar mill, the mill will shut down; if there is a transport strike, farmers cannot sell their produce.

![Fig. 1.2 How the three sectors depend on each other](figures/ss_ch18_fig02_chain.png)

### Comparing the three sectors: GDP

The various production activities in the primary, secondary and tertiary sectors produce a very large number of goods and services. Also, the three sectors have a large number of people working in them to produce these goods and services. To compare the sectors, we look at the **value** of goods and services produced in each.

- **Not every good (or service) that is produced and sold needs to be counted.** It makes sense only to include the **final goods and services**. For example, a farmer sells wheat to a flour mill for ₹8 per kg; the mill grinds it and sells flour to a biscuit company for ₹10 per kg; the biscuit company sells four packets of biscuits for ₹60. Biscuits are the **final goods** — goods that reach the consumers. Wheat and flour are **intermediate goods** — used up in producing the final good. The value of final goods **already includes** the value of all the intermediate goods. **Counting the value of the intermediate goods separately would lead to double counting.**
- The value of final goods and services produced in each sector during a particular year provides the total production of the sector for that year. The **sum of production in the three sectors** gives what is called the **Gross Domestic Product (GDP)** of a country.

> **GDP** is the **value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a particular year**. GDP shows **how big the economy is**. In India, the task of measuring GDP is undertaken by a **central government ministry**, with the help of various government departments of all the States and Union Territories, who collect information relating to the total volume of goods and services and their prices.

### Historical change in sectors

- In the **initial stages of development**, the **primary sector** was the most important sector of economic activity. As the methods of farming changed and the agriculture sector began to prosper, it produced much more food than before. Many people could now take up other activities — there were increasing numbers of craftspersons and traders.
- Over a long time (more than a hundred years), and especially because new methods of manufacturing were introduced, factories came up and started expanding. Those people who had earlier worked on farms now began to work in factories in large numbers. Gradually, the **secondary sector** became the most important in total production and employment.
- In the past 100 years, there has been a further shift from secondary to **tertiary sector** in developed countries. The service sector has become the most important in terms of total production. Most of the working people are also employed in the service sector. This is the general pattern observed for developed countries.

### Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors in India

**Rising importance of the tertiary sector.** Over the past decades, production in all three sectors has increased, but it has increased the most in the tertiary sector. The **tertiary sector has emerged as the largest producing sector in India**, replacing the primary sector — it now produces **more than half of India's GDP**.

Why is the tertiary sector becoming so important?

1. **Basic services:** in any country, several services such as **hospitals, educational institutions, post and telegraph services, police stations, courts, village administrative offices, municipal corporations, defence, transport, banks and insurance companies** are required. These can be considered basic services. In a developing country, the government has to take responsibility for the provision of these services.
2. **Development of agriculture and industry** leads to the development of services such as **transport, trade and storage**. The greater the development of the primary and secondary sectors, the more would be the demand for such services.
3. **Rising incomes:** as income levels rise, certain sections of people start demanding many more services like **eating out, tourism, shopping, private hospitals, private schools and professional training**.
4. **Information technology:** over the past decades, certain new services such as those based on **information and communication technology** have become important and essential — **software development, online banking and IT-enabled services**. The production of these services has been rising rapidly.

However, **not all of the service sector is growing equally well**. Service sector in India employs many different kinds of people. At one end, there are a limited number of services that employ **highly skilled and educated workers**. At the other end, there are a very large number of workers engaged in services such as **small shopkeepers, repair persons, transport persons**, etc. These people barely manage to earn a living, and yet they perform these services because no alternative opportunities for work are available to them.

### Where are most of the people employed?

- Although the share of the primary sector in GDP has fallen, the **primary sector continues to be the largest employer** — around **half of India's workers** are still in the primary sector, mainly agriculture, while it produces only a **small share of GDP**.
- Why didn't a similar shift out of the primary sector happen in the case of employment? It is because **not enough jobs were created in the secondary and tertiary sectors**. Even though industrial output and the production of services increased, employment in these sectors did not increase enough.

#### Underemployment (disguised unemployment)

- There are **more people in agriculture than is necessary**. So, even if you move a few people out, production will not be affected.
- **Example:** Laxmi's family of five works on a small plot of land of about two hectares. Even if two of them move to work elsewhere, the **production will not fall**. In this case, the five people are underemployed — they are working, but **all are made to work less than their potential**.
- This kind of underemployment is **hidden**, in contrast to someone who does not have a job and is clearly visible as unemployed. It is also called **disguised unemployment**.
- Underemployment can also happen in other sectors — for example, there are thousands of **casual workers in the service sector in urban areas** (painters, plumbers, repair persons) who search for daily employment and may not find work every day.

### How to create more employment?

1. **Irrigation:** Laxmi's family can get a **loan to construct a well** and irrigate the land; a **dam or canal** built by the government can irrigate many farms. Farmers can then grow **two or three crops** a year, which creates more work in agriculture and lets some people move to other jobs.
2. **Rural roads and transport:** better roads and transport help farmers reach markets; this creates jobs in **transport and trade**.
3. **Storage and credit:** setting up **godowns or cold storage**, and providing **cheap loans** to farmers through banks, help them get better prices and invest in production.
4. **Industries and services in semi-rural areas:** identify, promote and locate industries and services where a large number of people may be employed — for example, a **dal mill, cold storage, honey collection, vegetable and fruit processing**, and **tourism, regional crafts and IT**.
5. **Education and health:** a study by the former Planning Commission estimated that nearly **20 lakh jobs** can be created in the **education sector** alone; similarly, improving **health** would create jobs for doctors, nurses and health workers.
6. **Guaranteed employment — MGNREGA:** the central government made a law implementing the **Right to Work**, called the **Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005 (MGNREGA 2005)**. Under it, all those who are able to, and are in need of, work in rural areas are **guaranteed 100 days of employment in a year** by the government. **If the government fails to provide employment, it will give unemployment allowances** to the people. The types of work that would help increase the production from land are given preference. The scheme was launched in **February 2006** and now covers **the whole country**.

### Organised and unorganised sectors

Another way of classifying economic activities is on the basis of **how people are employed** — the **conditions of employment**.

![Fig. 1.3 Organised and unorganised sectors](figures/ss_ch18_fig03_organised.png)

1. **Organised sector:** it covers those enterprises or places of work where the **terms of employment are regular** and therefore people have **assured work**. They are **registered by the government** and have to follow its rules and regulations, which are given in various laws such as the **Factories Act, Minimum Wages Act, Payment of Gratuity Act, Shops and Establishments Act**, etc. It is called organised because it has some **formal processes and procedures**.
   - Workers in the organised sector enjoy **security of employment**. They are expected to work only a **fixed number of hours**; if they work more, they have to be **paid overtime** by the employer.
   - They also get several other benefits from the employers: **paid leave, payment during holidays, provident fund, gratuity**, etc. They are supposed to get **medical benefits** and, under the laws, the factory manager has to ensure facilities like drinking water and a safe working environment. When they retire, many of these workers get **pensions**.
2. **Unorganised sector:** it is characterised by **small and scattered units which are largely outside the control of the government**. There are rules and regulations, but these are **not followed**.
   - Jobs here are **low-paid and often not regular**. There is **no provision for overtime, paid leave, holidays, leave due to sickness**, etc.
   - **Employment is not secure** — people can be asked to leave without any reason. When there is less work, such as during some seasons, some people may be asked to leave.
   - A lot also depends on the whims of the employer. This sector includes a large number of people who are employed on their own, doing small jobs such as **selling on the street or doing repair work**. Similarly, farmers work on their own and hire labourers as and when they require.

**How to protect workers in the unorganised sector?**

- The organised sector offers jobs that are the most sought-after. But employment opportunities in the organised sector have been expanding very slowly. Many workers have been forced to enter the unorganised sector, which has low wages and no security.
- In **rural areas**, the unorganised sector mostly comprises **landless agricultural labourers, small and marginal farmers, sharecroppers and artisans** (weavers, blacksmiths, carpenters and goldsmiths). Nearly 80 per cent of rural households in India are in the small and marginal farmer category. These farmers need to be supported through adequate facilities for **timely delivery of seeds, agricultural inputs, credit, storage facilities and marketing outlets**.
- In **urban areas**, the unorganised sector comprises mainly **workers in small-scale industry, casual workers in construction, trade and transport**, and those who work as **street vendors, head-load workers, garment makers, rag pickers**, etc. **Small-scale industry** also needs the government's support for procuring raw material and marketing of output. Casual workers in both rural and urban areas need to be protected.
- We also find that the majority of workers from **Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and backward communities** find themselves in the unorganised sector. Besides getting the irregular and low-paid work, these workers also face **social discrimination**.
- The government can **fix minimum wages** and **working hours**, support **small-scale industries** with cheap loans, and give **social security** to protect these workers.

### Public and private sectors

Another way of classifying economic activities into sectors could be on the basis of **who owns assets and is responsible for the delivery of services**.

1. **Public sector:** the **government owns most of the assets** and provides all the services. Examples: **Indian Railways, the post office**, government hospitals and schools.
2. **Private sector:** the **ownership of assets and delivery of services is in the hands of private individuals or companies**. Examples: companies like **Tata Iron and Steel Company Limited (TISCO)** or **Reliance Industries Limited (RIL)**, private hospitals.

**Purpose.** Activities in the private sector are guided by the **motive to earn profits**. To get such services, we have to pay money to these individuals and companies. The purpose of the public sector is **not just to earn profits**. Governments raise money through **taxes and other ways** to meet expenses on the services rendered by them.

**Why is the public sector important?**

1. There are several things needed by society as a whole, which the private sector will **not provide at a reasonable cost** — for example, **roads, bridges, railways, harbours, generating electricity, providing irrigation through dams**. These need **huge spending**, which is beyond the capacity of the private sector, and it is not possible to collect money from thousands of people who use these facilities. So the government has to undertake such heavy spending and ensure these facilities are available for everyone.
2. Some activities the government has to **support** to encourage the private sector — for example, selling **electricity at the cost of generation** may raise costs for small-scale industries, so the government **subsidises** electricity.
3. The government buys wheat and rice from farmers at a **fair price** through the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**, stores it and sells it at a **lower price** to consumers through **ration shops**; it bears part of the cost.
4. There are a large number of activities which are the **primary responsibility of the government** — **health and education** facilities for all, running proper schools and providing quality education, and reducing malnutrition and infant mortality, especially in backward regions.

> **Public hospital or private hospital?** A government hospital provides **free or low-cost treatment**, especially for the poor, and focuses on **public welfare**; a private hospital may offer advanced facilities but charges **high fees** and aims to earn **profit**. Both are needed, but the public sector ensures that **essential services reach everyone**.

### Summary of the chapter

1. On the basis of activity, the economy has primary (from nature), secondary (manufacturing) and tertiary (services) sectors, which depend on one another.
2. GDP is the value of final goods and services produced in a country in a year; intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting.
3. In India, the tertiary sector now produces the largest share of GDP, but the primary sector still employs the most people, with much underemployment.
4. More jobs can be created through irrigation, roads, storage, credit, rural industries, education, health and MGNREGA (100 days of guaranteed work).
5. Organised and unorganised sectors differ in conditions of work; public and private sectors differ in ownership and purpose.

### 8.1 Textbook questions — solutions

*(The Goyal book's exercises were not available; these are the standard NCERT exercises on this topic.)*

**Q1.** Fill in the blanks: Employment in the service sector ……… increased to the same extent as production. *(has / has not)*
**Answer:** **has not**.

**Q2.** Workers in the ……… sector do not produce goods. *(tertiary / agricultural)*
**Answer:** **tertiary**.

**Q3.** Most of the workers in the ……… sector enjoy job security. *(organised / unorganised)*
**Answer:** **organised**.

**Q4.** The sectors are classified into public and private sector on the basis of: (a) employment conditions (b) the nature of economic activity (c) ownership of enterprises (d) number of workers employed.
**Answer:** **(c)** ownership of enterprises.

**Q5.** Production of a commodity, mostly through the natural process, is an activity in the ……… sector. (a) primary (b) secondary (c) tertiary (d) information technology
**Answer:** **(a)** primary.

**Q6.** GDP is the total value of ……… produced during a particular year. (a) all goods and services (b) all final goods and services (c) all intermediate goods and services (d) all intermediate and final goods and services
**Answer:** **(b)** all final goods and services.

**Q7.** Explain the difference between primary, secondary and tertiary sectors using examples.
**Answer:** The **primary** sector produces goods by **using natural resources** — e.g. growing cotton, fishing, mining. The **secondary** sector **changes natural products into other forms by manufacturing** — e.g. making cloth from cotton, sugar from sugarcane. The **tertiary** sector **provides services** that support the other two — e.g. transport, banking, trade, teaching.

**Q8.** "The tertiary sector is not playing any significant role in the development of the Indian economy." Do you agree? Give reasons.
**Answer:** **No.** (i) The tertiary sector has become the **largest producing sector**, contributing more than half of GDP. (ii) It provides **basic services** like health, education, banking and transport. (iii) It supports the growth of **agriculture and industry**. (iv) **IT services** earn foreign exchange and employ educated workers. However, many service workers earn little, so its benefits are uneven.

**Q9.** What is disguised unemployment? Explain with an example each from urban and rural areas.
**Answer:** **Disguised unemployment** is when more people are working than are needed, so each works below potential, and removing some does not reduce output. **Rural:** five members of a family work on a small plot where three are enough. **Urban:** casual workers such as painters and plumbers who wait for daily work and find it only on some days.

**Q10.** Distinguish between open unemployment and disguised unemployment.
**Answer:** **Open unemployment:** people willing and able to work have **no job at all**; it is visible (common in industry and among the educated). **Disguised unemployment:** people **appear to be employed** but are not fully productive; their removal does not reduce output (common in agriculture).

**Q11.** Explain the objective of implementing the MGNREGA 2005.
**Answer:** To give **100 days of guaranteed wage employment** a year to every rural household whose adults volunteer for unskilled work, to provide **income security**, to create **useful assets** (such as works that increase production from land), and to give an **unemployment allowance** if work is not provided.

**Q12.** How can more employment be created in rural areas?
**Answer:** (i) Loans for **irrigation** and building dams and canals so that farmers can grow more crops. (ii) **Rural roads and transport**. (iii) **Storage** and **cheap credit**. (iv) **Small industries** in semi-rural areas (dal mill, cold storage, fruit processing). (v) **Education, health and tourism**; and **MGNREGA**.

**Q13.** Distinguish between organised and unorganised sectors.
**Answer:** **Organised:** registered by the government, follows laws, offers **regular and secure jobs** with fixed hours, overtime pay, paid leave, provident fund and medical benefits. **Unorganised:** small, scattered units **outside government control**; jobs are **low-paid, irregular and insecure**, with no paid leave or benefits.

**Q14.** How do public and private sectors differ?
**Answer:** In the **public sector**, the **government owns** assets and provides services, aiming at **public welfare** (Indian Railways). In the **private sector**, **individuals or companies** own assets and aim to **earn profit** (TISCO, RIL).

**Guidebook C39.** (i) It provides **essential services** — education, health, electricity, water, transport, post. (ii) It builds **infrastructure** — roads, railways, dams, power. (iii) It undertakes **large, long-term investments** private firms avoid (railways, steel). (iv) It creates **jobs** and reduces **regional imbalance**. (v) It ensures **social justice** through affordable services (FCI ration shops). Examples: **Indian Railways, BHEL, public hospitals**.

**Guidebook Case D17.** (a) **GDP** is the value of **final goods and services produced within a country in a year**; e.g. **rice production** (primary) and **textile manufacturing** (secondary), also transport and banking (tertiary). **Guidebook Case D18.** (a) (i) **Public** hospital aims at **welfare**, private at **profit**. (ii) Public is **free/low-cost**; private charges **high fees**. ## 11. Long-Answer Questions (5 marks)

### Long answers (5 marks each)

**Q1.** Explain the three sectors of the economy with examples, and show how they depend on each other.
**Answer:** Primary: goods from natural resources — farming, fishing, mining.
Secondary: manufacturing — cloth from cotton, sugar from cane.
Tertiary: services — transport, banking, trade, teaching.
The primary sector supplies raw materials to the secondary.
The tertiary sector transports, stores, finances and sells their goods.

**Q2.** Why has the tertiary sector become so important in India?
**Answer:** Basic services — hospitals, schools, banks, transport — are needed everywhere.
Growth of agriculture and industry raises demand for transport, trade and storage.
Rising incomes increase demand for eating out, tourism and private schools.
IT-based services like software and online banking have grown rapidly.
It now produces more than half of GDP, though many service workers earn little.

**Q3.** What is underemployment? How can more employment be created?
**Answer:** Underemployment: more people work than needed (Laxmi's family).
Irrigation through loans, wells and dams lets farmers grow more crops.
Rural roads, storage and cheap credit create jobs in trade and transport.
Small industries and services in semi-rural areas offer new work.
Education, health, tourism and MGNREGA (100 days) add jobs.

**Q4.** Distinguish between organised and unorganised sectors. How can unorganised workers be protected?
**Answer:** Organised: registered, follows laws; secure jobs, fixed hours, benefits.
Unorganised: small, scattered, outside control; low, irregular pay, no benefits.
Rural unorganised workers need seeds, credit, storage and markets.
Urban workers need support for small industries and protection for casual work.
Government should fix minimum wages and hours and give social security.

**Q5.** Why is the public sector important for the development of a country?
**Answer:** It provides essential services — education, health, power, water, transport.
It builds infrastructure — roads, railways, dams.
It invests where private firms will not, such as railways and steel.
It creates jobs and reduces regional imbalance.
It ensures social justice through cheap food (FCI) and services.

**Q6.** What is GDP? Why are only final goods counted? Explain with an example.
**Answer:** GDP is the value of final goods and services produced in a country in a year.
A farmer sells wheat to a flour mill; the mill sells flour to a biscuit maker.
Biscuits are final goods; wheat and flour are intermediate goods.
The biscuits' value already includes the wheat and flour.
Counting intermediate goods again would cause double counting.

**Q7.** Explain the historical change in the importance of the three sectors.
**Answer:** Early on, the primary sector was most important.
Better farming produced surplus food, freeing people for crafts and trade.
New manufacturing methods made the secondary sector most important.
In developed countries, the tertiary sector later became the largest.
In India, services lead GDP, but agriculture still employs the most.

**Q8.** Explain how irrigation, small industries, cheap loans and skills can reduce unemployment.
**Answer:** Irrigation lets farmers grow two or three crops, creating more farm work.
Cheap loans help farmers invest in wells, seeds and machines.
Small industries and services in semi-rural areas give alternative jobs.
Skill training, education and health create jobs for skilled workers.
MGNREGA guarantees wage work, reducing seasonal unemployment.

**Answer:** So poor people get essential health care they cannot afford privately.

### Case 3 — Kamal in the rice mill

> Kamal works in a rice mill. His job is low paid and often not regular. There is no provision for paid leave, and he can be asked to leave at any time.

**(i)** In which sector does Kamal work by conditions of employment?
**Answer:** The unorganised sector.

**(ii)** Is a rice mill in the primary, secondary or tertiary sector?
**Answer:** Secondary (processing paddy into rice).

**(iii)** Name one benefit Kamal does not get.
**Answer:** Paid leave (or provident fund, job security).

**(iv)** Suggest one way the government can protect him.
**Answer:** Enforce minimum wages (or social security).

### Case 4 — MGNREGA

> The central government made a law implementing the Right to Work. All those who are able to, and are in need of, work in rural areas are guaranteed 100 days of employment in a year by the government.

**(i)** Name the law.
**Answer:** MGNREGA, 2005.

**(ii)** How many days of work does it guarantee?
**Answer:** 100 days a year.

**(iii)** What if the government cannot give work?
**Answer:** It pays an unemployment allowance.

**(iv)** Which kind of work gets preference?
**Answer:** Work that increases production from land.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Primary (nature: agriculture, dairy, fishing, forestry, mining; "agriculture and related"), secondary (manufacturing; "industrial"), tertiary (services: transport, storage, communication, banking, trade, teachers, IT; "service"). Interdependent.
- GDP = value of final goods and services in a year; intermediate goods excluded (double counting); measured by a central ministry.
- History: primary → secondary → tertiary. India: tertiary largest in GDP (basic services, agri/industry growth, rising incomes, IT); uneven growth.
- Employment: primary still largest employer; not enough jobs elsewhere; disguised unemployment (Laxmi's family; urban casual workers).
- More jobs: irrigation, roads, storage, credit, semi-rural industries, education (20 lakh jobs), health, tourism; MGNREGA 2005 (Feb 2006) — 100 days, unemployment allowance, land-productive work.
- Organised (registered, laws, security, benefits) vs unorganised (small, scattered, insecure); protect via minimum wages, credit, social security; SC/ST and backward communities largely unorganised.
- Public (government; welfare; Railways, post) vs private (companies; profit; TISCO, RIL); public needed for infrastructure, subsidies, FCI, health and education.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Agriculture produces most of India's GDP | Services produce the most; agriculture employs the most. |
| GDP counts all goods sold | Only final goods and services. |
| MGNREGA gives 200 days of work | It gives 100 days. |
| Organised/unorganised is by ownership | It is by conditions of employment. |
| Mining is secondary | Mining is primary. |
| Public sector aims at profit | It aims at public welfare. |
