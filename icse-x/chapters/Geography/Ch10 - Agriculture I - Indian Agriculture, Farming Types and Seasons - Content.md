## 1. Complete Lesson

### Indian agriculture — the backbone of the economy

India is an **agricultural country**. About **two-thirds of its people live in villages**, and **nearly half of its workforce** depends on agriculture and allied activities (livestock, fishing, forestry). Agriculture contributes about **one-sixth** of the country's income.

**Importance of agriculture**
1. **Employment** — the **largest source of employment and livelihood**.
2. **Food** — it feeds India's **huge population**; food security depends on it.
3. **Raw materials for agro-based industries** — **cotton** (textiles), **jute**, **sugarcane** (sugar), **oilseeds** (vegetable oil), **tea, coffee**, tobacco.
4. **Exports and foreign exchange** — tea, coffee, spices, rice, cotton, cashew, fruits.
5. **National income** — a big share of the GDP.
6. **Market for industrial goods** — farmers buy **fertilisers, pesticides, tractors, pumps** and consumer goods.
7. **Livestock** — fodder and crop residue support cattle; **railways and roads** earn by carrying farm produce.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2024, Q9(i))* Why is agriculture considered an important sector of the Indian economy? (Two points)
**Solution:** Any two: it is the **major source of employment**; it **provides food** to the large Indian population; the **surplus is exported** to earn **foreign exchange**; it **provides raw materials to agro-based industries**; it **contributes to national income**; it is a **market for industrial products**; it helps **livestock rearing**; railways earn by moving agricultural products.

### Problems of Indian agriculture

1. **Dependence on the monsoon** — rainfall is **erratic, unevenly distributed and unreliable**; most farms are **rain-fed** and lack irrigation.
2. **Small and fragmented land holdings** — land divided among heirs into **tiny, scattered plots**; machines cannot be used; farming becomes **less profitable**.
3. **Poverty and debt** — poor farmers cannot buy **HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides** or irrigation; lack of **easy credit** keeps them in the hands of **moneylenders**.
4. **Illiteracy and traditional methods** — reluctance to use **modern scientific methods**; **old tools** (wooden plough).
5. **Poor quality seeds**, **pests and diseases**.
6. **Soil erosion and loss of fertility** — **pressure of population** on land degrades the soil.
7. **Lack of storage and marketing** — produce rots; **middlemen** take the profit; poor transport.
8. **Primitive land tenure** — **exploitation** of tenants and landless labourers.
9. **Subsistence farming** — little surplus to sell.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q9(i))* Despite being the backbone of the Indian economy, our agricultural sector faces several problems. Mention any two such problems.
**Solution:** Any two: **too much dependence on rainfall**, which is erratic, unevenly distributed and unreliable; **illiteracy** makes farmers reluctant to use modern methods; **poverty** does not allow them to use HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides and irrigation; **fragmentation of land holdings** makes farming less profitable; **lack of easy finance and credit** keeps them debt-ridden in the hands of moneylenders; **pressure of population on land** degrades soil quality and reduces yields; the **primitive land tenure system** exploits poor farmers.

### Government measures — reforms for agriculture

The government has taken many steps to raise production and help farmers ("benefits" of planned agriculture):

| Area | Measures |
|---|---|
| **Technology** | The **Green Revolution** — **HYV seeds**, chemical **fertilisers**, **pesticides**; **agricultural universities** and research (ICAR) |
| **Water** | New **irrigation projects** (multipurpose dams, canals, tube wells); drip and sprinkler subsidies |
| **Land** | **Land reforms** — abolition of zamindari, ceiling on holdings, **consolidation of holdings** (joining scattered plots) |
| **Credit** | **Rural banks**, **cooperative societies**, **NABARD**, **Kisan Credit Cards** — cheap and easy loans |
| **Prices and risk** | **Minimum Support Price (MSP)** (the Agricultural Prices Commission was set up in 1965), **crop insurance** |
| **Subsidies** | **Fertilisers and pesticides at subsidised rates**; cheap / free **electricity and diesel** for pumps |
| **Awareness** | **Mass communication** — radio, TV and mobile advisories for farmers; **Soil Health Cards** |
| **Marketing** | **Regulated markets**, **cold storage**, warehouses, online markets |

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q9(i))* Mention any two agricultural reforms done by the government for the betterment of the agricultural sector.
**Solution:** Any two: the **Green Revolution** / use of **HYV seeds**; **development of irrigation** facilities; **rural banks and cooperatives** so that farmers get **loans** easily / **credit facilities**; **mass communication** to raise awareness; **distribution of fertilisers and pesticides at subsidised rates**.

### The Green Revolution

**Meaning** — the **Green Revolution** is the **sudden, large increase in food-grain production** (especially **wheat** and **rice**) brought about from the **mid-1960s** by using a **package of modern inputs** — **high-yielding variety (HYV) seeds**, **assured irrigation**, **chemical fertilisers**, **pesticides**, **farm machinery** and **cheap credit**.

**Background** — In the early 1960s India suffered **food shortages** and depended on **imported wheat**. Dwarf high-yielding wheat developed by **Norman Borlaug** in Mexico was introduced and adapted by Indian scientists led by **M. S. Swaminathan**; the first big gains came in **Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh**.

![Fig. 10.3 The Green Revolution — the package of inputs and its impact](figures/g_ch10_fig03_green_revolution.png)

**Impact of the first Green Revolution**

*Positive*
1. **Bumper production** of food crops — especially **wheat**, later **rice**.
2. **Self-sufficiency in food grains**; **food scarcity removed**; **imports reduced**, saving **foreign exchange**; buffer stocks built up.
3. **Higher rural incomes**; more **employment** in farming, transport and trade.
4. **Stimulated industrial growth** — demand for **fertilisers, pesticides, tractors, pumps**; agro-industries grew.
5. **Multiple cropping** and modern methods spread.

*Negative*
1. **Regional inequality** — gains were mainly in **Punjab, Haryana, western UP**; dry and eastern regions lagged.
2. **Crop inequality** — **wheat and rice** gained; **pulses, millets and oilseeds** were neglected.
3. **Rich farmers gained more** than small farmers.
4. **Environmental damage** — overuse of **fertilisers and pesticides** harmed the soil and water; **waterlogging and salinity**; **falling groundwater table** from over-pumping.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q9(ii))* The Green Revolution marked a transformation in Indian agriculture. (a) Explain how the Green Revolution has been particularly significant for the Indian economy. (b) State two key inputs whose widespread use helped usher in the Green Revolution.
**Solution:** (a) Any one: **bumper production** of food crops / **self-sufficiency in food grains**, removed **food scarcity**; it **stimulated industrial growth**; **raised rural income**; **reduced imports** and saved **foreign exchange**. (b) Any two: **irrigation**; **high-yielding variety seeds**; **pesticides**; **fertilisers**; **farm machinery**.

### Types of farming in India

#### 1. Subsistence farming

**Subsistence farming** is farming in which the farmer grows crops **mainly to feed the family**, with **little or no surplus** for sale.
- **Small holdings**; **family labour**; **simple tools**; little use of fertilisers or HYV seeds.
- Mostly **food crops** (rice, wheat, millets, pulses); **low yields**.

It is of two kinds:
- **Primitive subsistence (shifting) farming** — a patch of **forest is cleared by cutting and burning** ("**slash and burn**"); crops (maize, millets, vegetables) are grown for a few years until the **soil loses fertility**; then the farmer **moves to a new patch**. It is called **jhum** in the **North-East** (Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland), **podu** in Odisha and Andhra Pradesh, **bewar / dahiya** in Madhya Pradesh. **Disadvantages:** **deforestation**, **soil erosion**, **loss of fertility**, **low yields**, wastage of land and forest.
- **Intensive subsistence farming** — on **small plots in densely populated areas**, using much labour (and sometimes fertilisers and irrigation) to get the most food from little land — e.g. **rice** in the Ganga delta, Kerala.

#### 2. Commercial farming

**Commercial farming** is farming in which crops are grown **for sale in the market** to earn **profit**.
- **Large farms**; **hired labour** and **machines**; **heavy use of modern inputs** — HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides, irrigation.
- Often **cash crops** (cotton, sugarcane, tea, coffee) or food grains for the market — e.g. **wheat in Punjab and Haryana**.

| Subsistence farming | Commercial farming |
|---|---|
| Grown mainly for the **farmer's own family** | Grown **for sale** in the market, for **profit** |
| **Small** holdings | **Large** farms |
| **Family labour**, simple tools | **Hired labour**, **machines** |
| Little use of **modern inputs**; low yield | **HYV seeds, fertilisers, irrigation**; high yield |
| Mostly **food crops** | Often **cash crops** |
| Little or no **surplus** | Large **surplus** |

**Why are some crops called cash crops?** They are grown **specifically to sell for profit** in the market (often as **raw materials for industry** or **exports**) rather than for the farmer's own consumption — e.g. cotton, jute, sugarcane, tea.

#### 3. Intensive and extensive farming

| Intensive farming | Extensive farming |
|---|---|
| **Small farms** | **Large farms** |
| Much **labour and capital per unit of land** | **Machines**, less labour; **capital-intensive** |
| **High yield per hectare** | **Low yield per hectare** but **large total output** |
| **Many crops** a year — **multiple cropping**, crop rotation | **One or two crops** |
| **Manure, fertilisers, good seeds**, irrigation | Less input per hectare |
| In **densely populated** areas (Ganga plains, Kerala, West Bengal) | In **sparsely populated** areas with plenty of land |

#### 4. Plantation farming

**Plantation farming** is the growing of a **single cash crop** (such as **tea, coffee, rubber**, coconut, banana) on **large estates** for **sale and export**.
- **Huge capital**, **scientific methods**, a large **labour force** (skilled and unskilled).
- **Processing** (tea factories, rubber sheets) is often done **on the estate**.
- Good **transport** to markets and ports is essential.
- Examples: **tea** in **Assam** and the **Darjeeling hills (West Bengal)**; **coffee** in **Karnataka**; **rubber** in **Kerala**.

![Fig. 10.4 Where the farming types of this chapter are found (generalised)](figures/g_ch10_fig04_farming_types_map.png)

**Tea is grown on a large scale by plantation farming.**

#### 5. Mixed farming

**Mixed farming** is the **growing of crops along with the rearing of livestock** (dairy cattle, poultry, sheep; also **bee-keeping**) **on the same farm**. *(Do not confuse it with **mixed cropping**, which is growing two or more crops together in the same field.)*

![Fig. 10.2 Mixed farming — crops and livestock support each other](figures/g_ch10_fig02_mixed_farming.png)

**Advantages (benefits) of mixed farming**
1. **Low wastage** — **crop residue** is used as **animal fodder**, and **animal dung** is used as **manure**.
2. **Less risk / security** — if the **crops fail** (drought), the farmer still earns from **livestock** (milk, eggs, meat).
3. **Higher and more steady income** — **more than one source of income**.
4. **Saves expenditure** on fertilisers and fodder; keeps the soil fertile.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q9(iv))* [A photograph of a farm: cattle grazing on a fenced pasture beside rows of maize, with farm sheds.] (a) Identify the type of farming. (b) State one way in which it provides economic benefit to the farmer. (c) Give a reason why some crops are considered cash crops.
**Solution:** (a) **Mixed farming**. (b) Any one: it provides **more than one source of income**; it **saves expenditure**, as **animal waste is used as manure** and **crop residue as fodder**; **animal products substitute income** in case of **crop failure**, reducing risk. (c) Cash crops are grown **specifically to sell for profit** in the market rather than for the farmer's own consumption.

#### Other terms

- **Dry farming** — farming in **low-rainfall areas without irrigation**, growing **drought-resistant crops** (millets, pulses) and conserving moisture.
- **Wet farming** — farming in areas of **heavy rain or assured irrigation** (rice, sugarcane, jute).
- **Crop rotation** — growing **different crops in succession** on the same field (e.g. a cereal followed by a **pulse**) to **keep the soil fertile**.
- **Multiple cropping** — growing **two or more crops a year** on the same field.

### Agricultural (cropping) seasons

India has **three cropping seasons**, decided mainly by the **monsoon**.

| Season | Sowing | Harvesting | Crops |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kharif** (summer / monsoon crop) | **June–July**, with the onset of the south-west monsoon (May–June in some areas) | **September–October** | **Rice, maize, jowar, bajra, ragi, cotton, jute, groundnut, soybean**, arhar, moong, urad |
| **Rabi** (winter crop) | **October–December** | **March–April** (February–March in some areas) | **Wheat, barley, gram, peas, mustard**, rapeseed, linseed |
| **Zayad** (short summer season) | **March** | **June** | **Watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables, fodder** crops — mostly irrigated |

![Fig. 10.1 India's three cropping seasons](figures/g_ch10_fig01_crop_seasons.png)

- **Rabi** crops grow on **residual soil moisture** and **winter rain from western disturbances** (useful for wheat in the north-west).
- **Zayad** is the **intermediate season between rabi and kharif**.
- **Sugarcane** is a **year-long (annual)** crop; **cotton** is a **kharif** crop because it needs **high temperatures (21–30 °C)** and about **200 frost-free days** during its growth.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2025, Q9(iv))* Explain: (a) mixed farming (b) zayad (c) transplantation.
**Solution:** (a) **Growing crops along with rearing of livestock**. (b) The **intermediate cropping season between rabi and kharif**. (c) **Growing rice (or another crop) in a nursery** and then **transplanting the saplings into the main field**.

### Methods of sowing (introduction)

| Method | How | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Broadcasting** | **Scattering seeds by hand** over the field | Rice in some areas, wheat, millets |
| **Drilling** | Dropping seeds **in rows** with a **seed drill** | Wheat, gram |
| **Dibbling** | Putting seeds in **holes** made at intervals | Maize, cotton |
| **Transplantation** | Raising **seedlings in a nursery** and then **planting them out** in the main (puddled) field | **Rice**; also some vegetables |

Transplantation of rice gives a **surer, healthier crop**, **economical use of water**, **more production**, **less wastage of seed**, **easier weeding and harvesting** (Chapter 11).

### Summary of the chapter

- **Importance** — jobs (nearly half the workforce), food, raw materials, exports, national income, market for industry, railways.
- **Problems** — monsoon dependence, small/fragmented holdings, poverty and debt, illiteracy, poor seeds, pests, soil erosion, poor storage and marketing, middlemen, land tenure.
- **Measures** — Green Revolution, irrigation, land reforms and consolidation, credit (rural banks, cooperatives, NABARD, KCC), MSP, insurance, subsidies, research, mass communication.
- **Green Revolution** — mid-1960s; HYV seeds + irrigation + fertilisers + pesticides + machines + credit; wheat and rice; self-sufficiency, incomes, industry; but regional and crop inequality, environmental damage.
- **Farming types** — **subsistence** (family, small, food crops; shifting/jhum, intensive subsistence) vs **commercial** (sale, large, modern inputs, cash crops); **intensive** (small farms, high yield per hectare, dense population) vs **extensive** (large, mechanised, low yield per hectare); **plantation** (one cash crop, estates — tea, coffee, rubber); **mixed** (crops + livestock — fodder, manure, security).
- **Seasons** — **kharif** (June–July to Sept–Oct: rice, cotton, jute, bajra, groundnut), **rabi** (Oct–Dec to Mar–Apr: wheat, gram, mustard), **zayad** (Mar–Jun: melons, vegetables, fodder).

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Why farming still matters
Even as industry and services grow, the farm feeds the nation, employs the most people and supplies the mills. A good monsoon still lifts the whole economy.

### Section 2 — The farmer's troubles in one line
**Rain, land, money, knowledge, market** — the rain is unreliable, the land is small, money is scarce, modern knowledge is lacking, and the market is controlled by middlemen.

### Section 3 — A revolution in a bag of seed
HYV seeds gave huge yields **only** with water, fertilisers and pesticides — so the revolution came first where irrigation already existed: Punjab, Haryana and western UP.

### Section 4 — Pairs to compare
**Subsistence–commercial** (who eats it?), **intensive–extensive** (how much input per hectare?), **plantation** (one crop on an estate), **mixed** (crops + animals).

### Section 5 — Seasons follow the monsoon
**Kharif** rides the monsoon; **rabi** uses winter moisture; **zayad** fills the hot gap with irrigation.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 M. S. Swaminathan (1925–2023)
The agricultural scientist known as the **father of India's Green Revolution**. He adapted high-yielding wheat varieties to Indian conditions and championed their spread in the 1960s; later he worked for sustainable, "evergreen" farming. He was the first recipient of the World Food Prize (1987).

### 6.2 Norman Borlaug (1914–2009)
The American scientist who bred the dwarf, high-yielding wheat in Mexico that India used in the Green Revolution. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Farming is the base** — jobs, food and industry all start in the field.
- **Inputs make the difference** — water, seed and credit decide yields more than soil alone.
- **Balance matters** — the Green Revolution fed the nation but taught the cost of overusing water and chemicals.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya and the North-East:** jhum is still practised on hill slopes; shorter jhum cycles reduce fertility, while terraces, horticulture and mixed farming offer alternatives.
- **Your plate:** rice from a kharif harvest, wheat chapati from a rabi harvest, and a slice of watermelon from the zayad season.
- **Dairy cooperatives:** many farmers earn steady daily income from milk while growing crops — mixed farming in practice.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (2 marks) Distinguish between mixed farming and mixed cropping.
**Answer:** Mixed farming — crops and livestock together on one farm; mixed cropping — two or more crops grown together in the same field.

**Q2.** (3 marks) Name the type of farming: (a) one crop on a large estate for export (b) small plots with heavy labour in a crowded delta (c) clearing a forest patch by burning, then moving on.
**Answer:** (a) Plantation farming. (b) Intensive (subsistence) farming. (c) Shifting cultivation (jhum).

**Q3.** (3 marks) Give two positive and one negative impact of the Green Revolution.
**Answer:** Positive — self-sufficiency in food grains; higher rural incomes (also industrial growth). Negative — regional inequality (or soil and water damage, neglect of pulses).

**Q4.** (2 marks) Name one crop each of the kharif, rabi and zayad seasons.
**Answer:** Kharif — rice; rabi — wheat; zayad — watermelon.

**Q5.** (2 marks) What is consolidation of land holdings? Why is it useful?
**Answer:** Joining a farmer's scattered plots into one compact block; it saves time and allows better irrigation and machines.

**Q6.** (3 marks) Why did the Green Revolution succeed first in Punjab and Haryana? (Three reasons)
**Answer:** Assured irrigation (canals and tube wells); fertile alluvial soil; enterprising farmers with larger holdings and access to credit and power.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part II parts (2 marks each)

**Q1.** Why is agriculture important to the Indian economy? (Two points)
**Answer:** It is the largest source of employment and feeds the population.
It supplies raw materials to industry and earns foreign exchange.

**Q2.** Mention two problems faced by Indian farmers.
**Answer:** Dependence on an erratic monsoon.
Small, fragmented holdings (also debt, illiteracy, middlemen).

**Q3.** Differentiate between subsistence and commercial farming.
**Answer:** Subsistence — for the family; small farms, simple tools, low yield.
Commercial — for sale; large farms, modern inputs, high yield.

**Q4.** Differentiate between intensive and extensive farming.
**Answer:** Intensive — small farms, much labour, high yield per hectare, dense population.
Extensive — large mechanised farms, low yield per hectare, sparse population.

**Q5.** What is mixed farming? Give one advantage.
**Answer:** Growing crops and rearing livestock on the same farm.
Crop residue feeds animals and dung manures fields; income if crops fail.

**Q6.** Name two agricultural reforms of the government.
**Answer:** Easy credit through rural banks and cooperatives.
Subsidised fertilisers and HYV seeds (also irrigation, MSP).

**Q7.** State one difference between rabi and kharif seasons, with a crop of each.
**Answer:** Rabi — winter crop, sown Oct–Nov, harvested Feb–Apr (wheat).
Kharif — summer crop, sown May–July, harvested Sept–Oct (rice).

**Q8.** (a) Name the farming of slash and burn. (b) One disadvantage.
**Answer:** (a) Shifting cultivation (jhum).
(b) Deforestation and soil erosion.

**Q9.** Explain the significance of the Green Revolution and name two inputs.
**Answer:** Self-sufficiency in food grains; removed food scarcity.
HYV seeds and irrigation (also fertilisers, pesticides).

**Q10.** Why are some crops called cash crops? Give an example.
**Answer:** They are grown to sell for profit.
Cotton (also jute, sugarcane, tea).

**Q11.** Name two steps taken by the government to boost production.
**Answer:** New irrigation projects.
Consolidation of holdings (also MSP, agricultural universities).

**Q12.** Name the two main seasons and one crop of each.
**Answer:** Rabi — wheat.
Kharif — rice.

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Explain: (a) mixed farming (b) zayad (c) transplantation.
**Answer:** (a) Crops plus livestock on the same farm.
(b) The season between rabi and kharif.
(c) Seedlings raised in a nursery and planted in the field.

**Q14.** Mixed-farming picture: (a) identify (b) one economic benefit (c) why some crops are cash crops.
**Answer:** (a) Mixed farming.
(b) More than one source of income; saves on fodder and manure.
(c) Grown for sale and profit.

**Q15.** (a) Why is agriculture important? (b) Name the two main seasons. (c) What is mixed farming?
**Answer:** (a) Jobs, raw materials, exports, food.
(b) Rabi and kharif.
(c) Crops with an allied occupation like dairying.

**Q16.** Give three problems of Indian agriculture.
**Answer:** Erratic monsoon.
Small, fragmented holdings.
Poverty, debt and lack of credit.

**Q17.** State three measures to improve agriculture.
**Answer:** HYV seeds and fertilisers at subsidised rates.
Irrigation projects.
Credit, MSP and crop insurance.

**Q18.** Give two positive effects and one negative effect of the Green Revolution.
**Answer:** Bumper food-grain output; self-sufficiency.
Higher rural incomes; industrial growth.
Negative — regional inequality (or soil and water damage).

**Q19.** Name the type of farming: (a) a tea estate (b) a tribal farmer clearing forest by burning (c) a dairy farm that also grows wheat.
**Answer:** (a) Plantation farming.
(b) Shifting cultivation.

**Q20.** Give the sowing and harvesting months and two crops of: (a) kharif (b) rabi (c) zayad.
**Answer:** (a) June–July to Sept–Oct; rice, cotton.
(b) Oct–Dec to Mar–Apr; wheat, mustard.
(c) March to June; watermelon, cucumber.

**Q21.** State three features of plantation farming.
**Answer:** A single cash crop on a large estate.
Heavy capital and a large labour force.
Processing on the estate, mainly for export.

**Q22.** Give three advantages of mixed farming.
**Answer:** Less waste — residue as fodder, dung as manure.
Security if crops fail.
Steadier, higher income.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Advising a family farm)** A family in Bihar owns three small, scattered plots and depends on rain. Suggest three changes that could raise its income.

**Q3. (Hill farming)** A village in Meghalaya practises jhum with a three-year cycle. Explain one problem and one better practice.
**Model answer:** Short cycles do not let the forest and soil recover, causing erosion and low yields; terrace farming with horticulture or mixed farming is better.

**Q4. (The Green Revolution debate)** Your friend says the Green Revolution was "only good". Give one point to agree and one to disagree.
**Model answer:** Agree — it made India self-sufficient in food grains. Disagree — it caused regional inequality and damaged soil and groundwater.

**Q5. (Choosing a season)** A farmer has irrigation and wants a quick crop in April–May. Which season, and which crops?
**Model answer:** Zayad — watermelon, cucumber, vegetables or fodder.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — Two farmers

> Farmer A in Punjab has 20 hectares, a tractor, a tube well and HYV wheat, and sells the whole crop to the government. Farmer B in a hill village grows maize and millets on one hectare with family labour and eats most of it.

**(i)** Name A's type of farming.
**Answer:** Commercial farming.

**(ii)** Name B's type of farming.
**Answer:** Subsistence farming.

**(iii)** Name the price at which A sells to the government.
**Answer:** The Minimum Support Price.

**(iv)** Name the movement that brought HYV wheat to Punjab.
**Answer:** The Green Revolution.

### Case 2 — A year on the farm

> A farmer sows rice in July and harvests it in October, sows wheat in November and harvests it in April, and grows watermelon in May with canal water.

**(i)** Name the season of rice.
**Answer:** Kharif.

**(ii)** Name the season of wheat.
**Answer:** Rabi.

**(iii)** Name the season of watermelon.
**Answer:** Zayad.

**(iv)** What is growing three crops a year on one field called?
**Answer:** Multiple cropping.

### Case 3 — A farm with a dairy

> A farmer in Gujarat keeps ten cows. Their dung goes into the fields, and maize stalks feed the cows. In a drought year the crop failed, but milk sales kept the family going.

**(i)** Name this type of farming.
**Answer:** Mixed farming.

**(ii)** How is dung used?
**Answer:** As manure.

**(iii)** How is crop residue used?
**Answer:** As fodder.

**(iv)** What advantage does the drought year show?
**Answer:** Security — income from livestock when crops fail.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Importance: jobs (nearly half), food, raw materials, exports, national income, market for industry, railways, fodder.
- Problems: erratic monsoon, small/fragmented holdings, poverty and debt, illiteracy, old methods, poor seeds, pests, soil erosion, poor storage and marketing, middlemen, land tenure.
- Measures: Green Revolution (HYV), irrigation, land reforms and consolidation, credit (rural banks, cooperatives, NABARD, KCC), MSP, crop insurance, subsidies, universities and research, mass communication.
- Green Revolution: mid-1960s; HYV + irrigation + fertilisers + pesticides + machines + credit; wheat, rice; Punjab, Haryana, western UP; self-sufficiency, incomes, industry; regional/crop inequality, soil and water damage.
- Farming: subsistence vs commercial; shifting (jhum, podu) and intensive subsistence; intensive vs extensive; plantation (tea, coffee, rubber); mixed (crops + animals; fodder, manure, security).
- Seasons: kharif (Jun–Jul → Sep–Oct; rice, cotton, jute, bajra, groundnut, soybean); rabi (Oct–Dec → Mar–Apr; wheat, gram, mustard, barley); zayad (Mar–Jun; melons, vegetables, fodder).
- Sowing: broadcasting, drilling, dibbling, transplantation.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Mixed farming = growing many crops | Mixed farming = crops + livestock. |
| "Summer crop and winter crop" as season names | Write kharif and rabi (and zayad). |
| Importance of agriculture without economic links | Give jobs, food, raw materials, exports. |
| Extensive farming gives high yield per hectare | Intensive does; extensive gives large total output. |
| Plantation = any big farm | One cash crop on an estate, with processing, for sale. |
| Zayad = kharif | Zayad is between rabi and kharif. |
| Listing only advantages of the Green Revolution | Include its problems when asked for impact. |
