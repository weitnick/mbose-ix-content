## 1. Complete Lesson

### Energy — the power behind development

- **Energy** is needed for **industry, agriculture, transport, homes and services**. The amount of energy a country uses is a measure of its **development**.
- Energy is obtained from **fuels** (coal, petroleum, natural gas, wood, dung) and from **natural forces** (falling water, sunlight, wind, tides, the earth's heat) and **nuclear fuels** (uranium, thorium).

**Conventional and non-conventional sources**

| Conventional sources | Non-conventional (alternative) sources |
|---|---|
| Used for a **long time**, on a large scale | **New** sources, developed recently |
| **Coal, petroleum, natural gas** (fossil fuels), **hydel power**, firewood | **Solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, nuclear, biogas** |
| Fossil fuels are **non-renewable / exhaustible** | Mostly **renewable / inexhaustible** |
| Fossil fuels **pollute** — smoke, carbon dioxide, ash | Mostly **clean, eco-friendly** |
| Large, centralised power stations | Can be **small and local** (village biogas, rooftop solar) |

> Hydel power is **conventional** (in use for a long time) but **renewable**. Nuclear power is **non-conventional** but uses **exhaustible** minerals.

**Why are conventional sources not eco-friendly?** Burning coal and oil **pollutes the air**; they release **carbon dioxide** (global warming) and **poisonous gases**; **mining** causes large-scale pollution and **ecological imbalance**; there is **dust pollution**; and they are **non-renewable** — they may be **exhausted** soon.

**Why are alternative sources becoming essential?**
1. Fossil fuels are **exhaustible** and their **prices keep rising**.
2. They cause **pollution** and **global warming**.
3. India's **energy demand is growing** rapidly.
4. India **imports most of its petroleum**, spending huge foreign exchange.
5. Alternative sources can bring electricity to **remote villages**.

### 1. Coal

Coal is a **fossil fuel** formed from **plants buried millions of years ago** and changed by **heat and pressure**. It is India's **most important source of commercial energy** — most of India's electricity comes from **coal-based thermal power stations**.

**Types of coal**

| Type | Carbon | Features | Where / use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anthracite** | 80–95 % | **Hardest**, best quality; **highest calorific (heat) value**; burns with little smoke | Small amounts in Jammu & Kashmir |
| **Bituminous** | 60–80 % | **Most widely used**; **coking coal** for iron and steel; called **industrial coal** | **Gondwana coalfields** (Jharkhand, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, West Bengal); **thermal power** |
| **Lignite** | 40–55 % | **Brown coal**; high moisture; low quality | **Neyveli** (Tamil Nadu), Rajasthan, Gujarat; thermal power |
| **Peat** | below 50 % | **First stage** of coal formation; much moisture; smoky; low heat | Marshy areas; little use |

![Fig. 9.1 Types of coal — carbon content and heat value](figures/g_ch09_fig01_coal_types.png)

**Why is bituminous coal most widely used in thermal power?** It is **abundantly found in India** and has a **high carbon content**, giving a **high calorific value**.

**Distribution of coal**

*A. Gondwana coal* (about 250 million years old; nearly all of India's reserves; good quality, mostly bituminous) — found in the **river valleys of the peninsula**:

| Valley | Coalfields | State |
|---|---|---|
| **Damodar valley** | **Jharia** (largest coking-coal field), **Raniganj** (**oldest coalfield** — mining began in 1774), Bokaro, Giridih, Karanpura | Jharkhand, West Bengal |
| **Mahanadi valley** | **Talcher**, Ib valley | Odisha |
| **Son valley** | **Singrauli** | Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh |
| **Hasdeo / Mand valleys** | **Korba** | Chhattisgarh |
| **Godavari valley** | **Singareni** | Telangana |
| **Wardha valley** | **Chandrapur**, Wardha | Maharashtra |

*B. Tertiary coal* (younger, about 55 million years old; **lower quality, high sulphur**) — found in **Assam** (**Makum**, Nazira), **Meghalaya** (Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills), **Arunachal Pradesh** (Namchik–Namphuk), **Nagaland**, and **Jammu & Kashmir** (Kalakot). The **lignite** of **Neyveli**, Rajasthan and Gujarat is also of this younger age.

**Leading states** — **Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand** produce most of India's coal; **Odisha** has led production in recent years, while **Jharkhand** has the **largest reserves**. **West Bengal, Madhya Pradesh, Telangana and Maharashtra** follow.

| Advantages of coal | Disadvantages of coal |
|---|---|
| **Large reserves** in India; easily available | **Non-renewable** — will be exhausted |
| **Relatively cheap**; high calorific value (bituminous) | **Pollutes** — smoke, carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, **fly ash** |
| Fuel for **thermal power**; **coking coal** for iron and steel | **Bulky** — **heavy transport cost** |
| Raw material for **chemicals** (coal tar, dyes, fertilisers) | Mining **hazards and diseases**; **land degradation** |
| Supports **many industries** and jobs | Indian coal has **high ash content**; problem of **ash disposal** |

**Why is coal the most preferred source of energy in Indian industries?** It has a **high calorific value** and is **easily available** in large quantities.

### 2. Petroleum (mineral oil)

**Petroleum** (crude oil) is a **fossil fuel** formed from the remains of **tiny sea organisms** buried in **sedimentary rocks**. It is found trapped in **folds (anticlines) and faults** of rocks, often with **natural gas** above it. It is refined into **petrol, diesel, kerosene, LPG, lubricants, wax** and **bitumen**.

**Why is petroleum called "liquid gold"?** It is a **versatile** mineral — it **generates power**; it is the **fuel for vehicles** and factories; it is the **raw material** for **plastics, synthetic fibres, fertilisers, tarpaulin, wax**; its **by-products** (kerosene, LPG) are very useful; **not even the smallest part of the crude oil goes waste**; and it has a **high economic value**.

**Distribution of oil fields**

| Region | Oil fields |
|---|---|
| **Assam** (oldest) | **Digboi** (oil found 1889 — India's oldest), **Naharkatiya**, **Moran**, Hugrijan, Rudrasagar |
| **Gujarat — onshore** (on land) | **Ankleshwar**, **Kalol**, **Lunej**, **Kosamba**, **Mehsana**, Nawagam |
| **Offshore — Arabian Sea** (in the sea) | **Mumbai High** (the **largest producing oil field** of India, about 160 km off Mumbai, found in 1974), **Bassein** (mainly gas), **Aliabet** (in the **Gulf of Khambhat / Cambay**) |
| **Eastern coast** | **Krishna–Godavari basin** and **Cauvery basin** (onshore and offshore) |
| **Rajasthan** | **Barmer** — Mangala, Bhagyam, Aishwarya |

![Fig. 9.5 Coalfields and oil and gas fields of India](figures/g_ch09_fig05_coal_oil_map.png)

**Oil refineries** — Digboi, Guwahati, Bongaigaon, Numaligarh (Assam); **Barauni** (**Bihar**); Haldia (West Bengal); Paradip (Odisha); Visakhapatnam; Chennai; Kochi; Mangaluru; Mumbai (two); **Koyali** and **Jamnagar** (Gujarat); Mathura (Uttar Pradesh); Panipat (Haryana); Bathinda (Punjab); Bina (Madhya Pradesh).

**Why are oil refineries located close to oilfields or near ports?** Crude oil is **bulky**; refining near the **oilfields** saves transport (it comes by **pipeline**). India **imports** most of its crude oil by sea, so many refineries are near **ports** (Mumbai, Jamnagar, Kochi, Chennai, Paradip). Others are in **market areas** connected by pipelines (Mathura, Panipat).

| Advantages of petroleum | Disadvantages of petroleum |
|---|---|
| **Versatile** — fuel and raw material; "liquid gold" | **Non-renewable**; limited reserves in India |
| **Easy to transport** by pipelines and tankers | **Pollution** — smoke, carbon dioxide, **oil spills** at sea |
| High energy; runs vehicles, aircraft, generators | **Highly inflammable** — fire hazard |
| Many useful **by-products** (petrochemicals) | India **imports** most of its needs — costly; prices fluctuate |

### 3. Natural gas

**Natural gas** is found **with petroleum** or **alone**, in sedimentary rocks. It is used as **fuel for power plants**, **CNG** for vehicles, **PNG / LPG** for cooking, and as a **raw material for fertilisers (urea) and petrochemicals**.

**Areas** — **Mumbai High and Bassein** (offshore), the **Krishna–Godavari basin**, **Gujarat** (Ankleshwar, Kalol), **Assam**, **Tripura**, **Rajasthan** (Jaisalmer). Gas is carried across the country by **pipelines** such as the **Hazira–Vijaipur–Jagdishpur (HVJ) pipeline**.

**Advantages of natural gas over coal**
1. **Cleaner** — **less polluting**, emits **fewer greenhouse gases**, leaves **no ash**; eco-friendly.
2. **Easy to transport** through **pipelines**.
3. **Cheaper** and more **efficient** (high heat value).
4. Also a **raw material** for **fertilisers** and **petrochemicals**.

**Though a fossil fuel, why is natural gas so popular in homes and industries?** It is **cheaper** and **cleaner (less polluting)**, producing **fewer greenhouse gases**.

**Disadvantages** — it is **non-renewable**; **highly inflammable and explosive** (leaks cause accidents); it needs **costly pipelines** and storage; it still releases **carbon dioxide** when burnt.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2018, Q7(b))* (i) Mention one advantage of the use of natural gas over coal or petroleum. (ii) Name one offshore oil field of India.
**Solution:** (i) Natural gas creates **less pollution** (as CNG or LPG) compared with coal or petroleum. (ii) Any one: **Mumbai High / Aliabet / Bassein** / the basins of the Godavari, Krishna, Cauvery.

### 4. Hydel power (hydroelectricity)

**Hydel power** is electricity produced by **fast-flowing or falling water** turning **turbines** that drive **generators**. Water is stored behind a **dam** in a **reservoir** and released through pipes (**penstocks**) to the turbines.

![Fig. 9.2 How a hydel (hydroelectric) power station works](figures/g_ch09_fig02_hydel_station.png)

**Conditions** — a **perennial supply of water**, **steep slopes or waterfalls** (a good "head"), a **suitable dam site** with hard rock, and nearby **demand**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Renewable**, inexhaustible — water is not used up | **High initial cost** and long construction time |
| **Clean** — no smoke or ash | **Displacement** of people; **submergence** of forests, farms and villages |
| **Cheaper in the long run** — **no raw material** to buy, **low maintenance and recurring cost** | **Silting** of reservoirs |
| Part of **multipurpose projects** — irrigation, flood control, fishing | Output falls in **dry years**; sites only in hilly areas; earthquake risk |

**Multipurpose river valley projects** — a single project on a river serves **many purposes**: **irrigation, hydel power, flood control, drinking and industrial water, fishing, navigation, tourism, soil conservation and afforestation**. Jawaharlal Nehru called the dams the "**temples of modern India**".

![Fig. 9.4 Benefits of a multipurpose river valley project](figures/g_ch09_fig04_multipurpose.png)

#### The Bhakra Nangal Project

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **River** | **Sutlej** |
| **Location** | **Bhakra dam** in **Bilaspur district, Himachal Pradesh**; **Nangal dam** about 13 km downstream in **Punjab** |
| **Joint venture of** | **Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan** (with Himachal Pradesh) |
| **Reservoir** | **Gobind Sagar** |
| **Dam** | One of the **highest straight gravity dams** in the world (about 226 m); completed in **1963** |

**Benefits**
1. **Irrigation** — the **Bhakra canal system** irrigates vast areas of **Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan**; it helped the **Green Revolution** (wheat and rice).
2. **Hydel power** — power houses supply electricity to **Punjab, Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Rajasthan, Delhi** (and Chandigarh) for **industries, tube wells and homes**.
3. **Flood control** in the Sutlej basin.
4. **Drinking water** and water for **industries** (e.g. the fertiliser plant at **Nangal**).
5. **Fishing** in Gobind Sagar, **tourism**, afforestation.

**Problems it solved** — **agricultural**: irrigation for the dry lands of Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan, and flood control; **industrial**: **cheap hydel power** for industries.

#### The Hirakud Project

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **River** | **Mahanadi** |
| **Location** | Near **Sambalpur**, **Odisha** |
| **Dam** | One of the **longest earthen-and-masonry dams in the world** (about 26 km with its dykes); completed in **1957** |
| **Power houses** | **Burla** and **Chiplima** |

**Benefits**
1. **Flood control** — the main aim: it protects the **Mahanadi delta** from devastating floods.
2. **Irrigation** for both **kharif and rabi** crops in western Odisha (Sambalpur, Bargarh, Bolangir).
3. **Hydel power** for **industries** (aluminium smelting at Hirakud, steel at **Rourkela**, cement, paper) and homes.
4. **Inland waterways** on its canals; **fishing (pisciculture)** in the reservoir.
5. **Drinking water**, **soil conservation**, **eradication of malaria** in the area, and **tourism** (a popular dam site).

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2024, Q8(iii))* (a) Mention two ways in which the Hirakud dam has helped the people of Odisha. (b) Across which river is the Hirakud dam built?
**Solution:** (a) Any two: it **controls floods**; **generates power**; **supplies water for irrigation**; its **canals serve as inland waterways**; the dam site is a **popular tourist destination**; **pisciculture** in the lake; **drinking water**; **eradication of malaria**; **prevents soil erosion**. (b) **Mahanadi**.

### 5. Non-conventional (alternative) sources

#### Solar energy

**Solar energy** is energy from the **sun's rays**, turned into **electricity** by **photovoltaic (solar) cells** or used directly as **heat** (solar cookers, water heaters, dryers).

**Why does India stand at an advantage?** India is a **tropical country** (between about 8° N and 37° N, crossed by the **Tropic of Cancer**); it receives **strong sunshine** for most of the year — about **300 sunny days**. **Solar energy has the most widespread potential** of all non-conventional sources in India.

**Areas** — **Rajasthan** (**Bhadla Solar Park**, Jodhpur — one of the largest in the world), **Gujarat** (Charanka), **Karnataka** (Pavagada), **Andhra Pradesh** (Kurnool), **Madhya Pradesh** (Rewa), **Tamil Nadu** (Kamuthi); rooftop panels everywhere.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Clean, inexhaustible, renewable** | **High initial cost** of panels and batteries |
| **Reduces pressure on fossil fuels** | Not available **at night**; less on **cloudy / monsoon** days |
| **Cheaper in the long run**; low maintenance | Large solar parks need **much land** |
| Good for **remote villages**; **government subsidies** | Storage of power is costly |

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q8(iv))* [A photograph of rows of solar panels.] (a) Identify the type of energy being harnessed. (b) Why is this form of energy preferred over fossil fuels? (c) Why does India stand at an advantage in the production of this type of energy?
**Solution:** (a) **Solar energy**. (b) Any one: a **clean source** of energy; **inexhaustible**; **reduces pressure on fossil fuels**; **cheaper in the long run**. (c) India has a **tropical location** and receives **more or less strong rays of the sun** for a **large part of the year**.

#### Wind energy

**Wind energy** is produced by **wind turbines (windmills)** whose blades are turned by strong, steady winds, driving generators.

**Areas** — **Tamil Nadu** (**Muppandal** near Kanyakumari — one of the largest wind-farm clusters in India; Aralvaimozhi pass), **Gujarat** (Kutch, Lamba), **Maharashtra** (Satara), **Rajasthan** (Jaisalmer), Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh. Coastal areas and mountain passes have strong winds.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Clean, renewable**, inexhaustible | Wind is **not constant** — supply varies |
| **Cheap to run** after installation; low cost per unit | **Noise**; risk to **birds**; spoils the landscape |
| Windmills on **farmland** give farmers **extra income** (rent) and **cheap power for tube wells and pumps** | Needs **large open areas** with strong winds |

#### Tidal energy

**Tidal energy** is produced by the **natural rise and fall of ocean waters** (tides). Water entering and leaving a **bay or creek** through a **barrage** turns turbines. India's **vast coastline** gives it potential (also for **wind**).

**Areas with potential** — the **Gulf of Khambhat (Cambay)** and the **Gulf of Kachchh (Kutch)** in Gujarat, and the **Sundarbans** (Durgaduani creek) in West Bengal.

**Advantages** — **renewable**, **predictable** (tides are regular), clean. **Disadvantages** — **very costly** to build; **few suitable sites** with a large tidal range; may harm **marine life**.

#### Geothermal energy

**Geothermal energy** is the **heat of the earth's interior**, tapped from **hot springs** and hot rocks to produce steam and electricity.

**Areas** — **Puga valley** (Ladakh), **Manikaran** (Himachal Pradesh), **Tattapani** (Chhattisgarh); other hot springs in the Himalayas and western India.

**Advantages** — **eco-friendly**, **renewable, inexhaustible**, **sustainable**; available **day and night**; needs little land.
**Why is it not popular in India?** It is **confined to a few areas**; it is **expensive to develop**; **less energy is generated**; and it can cause some **air and water pollution** (gases and minerals in the steam).

#### Nuclear (atomic) energy

**Nuclear energy** is produced by **splitting (fission) of atoms** of radioactive minerals — **uranium, thorium** (also plutonium, beryllium, zirconium; **monazite sand** of the Kerala coast contains thorium) — releasing enormous heat to make steam and electricity.

**Nuclear power stations** — **Tarapur** (Maharashtra — the first, 1969), **Rawatbhata** (near Kota, Rajasthan), **Kalpakkam** (Tamil Nadu), **Narora** (Uttar Pradesh), **Kakrapar** (Gujarat), **Kaiga** (Karnataka), **Kudankulam** (Tamil Nadu — the largest). Uranium is mined at **Jaduguda** (Jharkhand).

**Why is nuclear energy considered the energy of the future?** **Fossil fuels are running out**, while a **small amount of nuclear fuel gives enormous power**; India has **large thorium reserves**; it gives **continuous, large-scale** power and **no smoke or carbon dioxide**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Huge power from a little fuel** | **Radioactive waste** — very hard to dispose of safely |
| **No smoke / greenhouse gases** | Risk of **accidents** and **radiation leaks** |
| Steady, round-the-clock supply; large thorium reserves | **Very high cost**; much uranium is imported; security concerns |

#### Biogas

**Biogas** (**gobar gas**) is a gas — mainly **methane** — formed by the **decomposition of animal dung, vegetable and kitchen waste in the absence of air** inside a **biogas plant (digester)**. It is used for **cooking and lighting**.

![Fig. 9.3 A biogas (gobar gas) plant](figures/g_ch09_fig03_biogas_plant.png)

**Why can biogas be produced in every rural household?** Every rural household produces **organic kitchen waste and animal (cattle) waste**, which can be converted into biogas.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Raw material easily available** and **cheap**; no fuel to buy | Needs cattle and **daily feeding** of the plant |
| The **sludge (slurry)** left behind is a **rich manure / bio-fertiliser** | Gas output **falls in cold winters** |
| **Clean, non-polluting** — no smoke; **keeps the village clean**, improves **hygiene** | Some **initial cost** of the plant |
| **Reduces waste**; saves **firewood** (protects forests); **renewable** | Suitable only on a **small scale** |

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2025, Q8(ii))* (a) How is biogas formed? (b) Mention any one advantage of biogas over other non-conventional energy resources.
**Solution:** (a) It is the gas obtained by the **decomposition of vegetative and animal waste**. (b) Any one: the **raw material is easily available**; it is **cheap**; **waste is reduced**; it gives **extra income**; **farm waste is recycled**; the **sludge is used as manure**; it **keeps the village clean**.

![Fig. 9.6 Hydel projects, nuclear power stations and non-conventional energy sites](figures/g_ch09_fig06_power_map.png)

### Advantages of non-conventional sources over conventional sources

1. They are **renewable and inexhaustible**.
2. They are **eco-friendly** — little or no pollution.
3. They can **produce large amounts of power** for domestic, industrial and other uses, and can be set up **locally**.
4. They **reduce dependence on fossil fuels** and **imports**.

### All sources at a glance

| Source | Type | Key area | One advantage | One disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coal | Conventional, fossil | Jharia, Raniganj, Talcher, Korba | Large reserves, high heat | Pollution, exhaustible |
| Petroleum | Conventional, fossil | Mumbai High, Digboi, Ankleshwar | Versatile ("liquid gold") | Pollution; imported |
| Natural gas | Conventional, fossil | Bassein, KG basin | Cleaner, piped | Inflammable |
| Hydel | Conventional, renewable | Bhakra Nangal, Hirakud | Cheap in long run, clean | Displacement, high cost |
| Solar | Non-conventional | Bhadla (Rajasthan) | Inexhaustible, clean | Not at night; costly |
| Wind | Non-conventional | Muppandal (TN) | Clean; farmer income | Wind not steady |
| Tidal | Non-conventional | Gulf of Khambhat, Kutch | Predictable | Costly; few sites |
| Geothermal | Non-conventional | Puga valley, Manikaran | Eco-friendly | Few areas; costly |
| Nuclear | Non-conventional | Tarapur, Kudankulam | Huge power, no smoke | Radioactive waste |
| Biogas | Non-conventional | Villages everywhere | Cheap; manure | Small scale |

### Summary of the chapter

- **Conventional** (coal, oil, gas, hydel, wood) vs **non-conventional** (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, nuclear, biogas); fossil fuels exhaustible and polluting.
- **Coal** — anthracite (highest heat), **bituminous** (industrial/coking, most used), lignite (brown, Neyveli), peat; **Gondwana** (Damodar — **Jharia**, **Raniganj** oldest; Talcher; Singrauli; Korba; Singareni; Chandrapur) and **tertiary** (Assam — Makum; Meghalaya); Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand lead.
- **Petroleum** — "liquid gold"; **Digboi** (oldest), Ankleshwar, Kalol (onshore Gujarat); **Mumbai High** (largest, offshore), Bassein, **Aliabet** (Gulf of Khambhat); refineries near oilfields, ports, markets (**Barauni** in Bihar).
- **Natural gas** — cleaner, cheaper, piped; fertilisers; inflammable.
- **Hydel** — renewable, clean, cheap in the long run; costly to build, displacement. **Bhakra Nangal** — **Sutlej**, HP/Punjab, Gobind Sagar; Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan; irrigation, power, floods. **Hirakud** — **Mahanadi**, Odisha; floods, irrigation, power (Burla), waterways, fishing, malaria control.
- **Solar** — tropical, 300 sunny days, most potential; Bhadla. **Wind** — Muppandal; farmer income. **Tidal** — Gulf of Khambhat/Kutch, Sundarbans. **Geothermal** — Puga, Manikaran; few areas, costly. **Nuclear** — uranium, thorium; Tarapur, Kudankulam; energy of the future; waste. **Biogas** — dung + waste → methane; manure; clean village.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Old sunlight
Coal, oil and gas are stored sunshine — the energy of ancient plants and sea life, locked in rocks for millions of years. Once burnt, they are gone.

### Section 2 — Read the coal by its carbon
More carbon means more heat: peat → lignite → bituminous → anthracite. **Bituminous** is India's workhorse; **anthracite** is the best but scarce.

### Section 3 — Onshore or offshore?
**On land**: Digboi, Ankleshwar, Kalol. **In the sea**: Mumbai High, Bassein, Aliabet. Many questions turn on this one word.

### Section 4 — Two dams to remember
**Bhakra Nangal** tamed the **Sutlej** for the **north-western plains** (irrigation and power — the Green Revolution). **Hirakud** tamed the **Mahanadi** for **Odisha** (flood control first).

### Section 5 — Energy that does not run out
Sun, wind, tides, the earth's heat and farm waste never run out — but each needs the right place: sunny Rajasthan, windy Kanyakumari, the tidal gulfs of Gujarat, hot springs of the Himalayas, cattle-rich villages.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Homi Jehangir Bhabha (1909–1966)
The physicist who founded India's atomic energy programme. He planned a **three-stage nuclear programme** to use India's **large thorium reserves**. The Bhabha Atomic Research Centre (BARC), Trombay, is named after him.

### 6.2 Jawaharlal Nehru and the "temples of modern India"
Speaking at **Bhakra Nangal** while the project was being built, Prime Minister Nehru called such dams the "temples of modern India", expressing the hope that irrigation and power would transform the country. The dam was dedicated to the nation in 1963.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Energy drives development** — industry, farming and homes all depend on it.
- **From burning to harvesting** — India is moving from coal and oil towards sun, wind and water.
- **Every source has a cost** — even clean sources affect land, rivers or wildlife.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya:** hydel projects on the Umiam (Barapani) and other rivers; tertiary coal of the Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills.
- **Your rooftop:** a few solar panels can run lights and fans and earn credit by feeding the grid.
- **Villages:** a biogas plant turns cattle dung into cooking gas and manure — cleaner kitchens, healthier families.

#### Exercise 9A — Conventional sources

**Q1.** Name the four types of coal in order of quality.
**Answer:** Anthracite, bituminous, lignite, peat.

**Q2.** Name the oldest coalfield and the largest coking-coal field of India.
**Answer:** Raniganj; Jharia.

**Answer:** It is versatile and highly valuable — fuel, raw material and many by-products; nothing goes waste.

**Q4.** Give two advantages of natural gas over coal.
**Answer:** It is cleaner (less pollution, no ash) and easily transported by pipelines.

**Answer:** Flood control in the Mahanadi delta; irrigation (also power, fishing).

**Q7.** Why is hydel power cheaper in the long run?
**Answer:** No raw material is bought; maintenance and running costs are low.

#### Exercise 9C — Non-conventional sources

**Q8.** Why is India suited to solar energy?
**Answer:** Its tropical location gives strong sunshine on about 300 days a year.

**Q9.** Name two minerals used for nuclear power.
**Answer:** Uranium and thorium.

**Q10.** Give one use of the slurry from a biogas plant.
**Answer:** As rich manure.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Planning power for a state)** A hilly state with fast rivers, many hot springs and heavy cloud cover wants clean power. Which source should it develop first, and why?
**Model answer:** Hydel power — fast perennial rivers and steep slopes suit it; solar would suffer from clouds, and geothermal is costly and small.

**Q2. (A farmer's choice)** A farmer in Kutch is offered rent to allow wind turbines on part of the land. Should the farmer agree? Give two reasons.
**Model answer:** Yes — it gives steady extra income, and cheap power may be available for tube wells; the rest of the land can still be farmed.

**Q3. (A smoky kitchen)** A rural family cooks with wood and dung cakes and has three cows. Suggest a better fuel and two benefits.
**Model answer:** A biogas plant — clean smoke-free cooking gas, and slurry as manure; it also saves trees.

**Q4. (Reading a picture)** A photograph shows a dam with spillway gates on the Mahanadi near Sambalpur. Name the project and two benefits for Odisha.
**Model answer:** Hirakud — flood control in the delta and irrigation (also power, fishing).

**Q5. (City air)** Why did many Indian cities switch buses and autorickshaws to CNG?
**Model answer:** Natural gas burns cleaner than diesel or petrol, cutting smoke and harmful gases.

## 13. Assertion–Reason Questions

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) A is true, but R is false.
- (b) A is false, but R is true.
- (c) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (d) Both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A.

**Q1.**

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — A dam in the north-west

> A tall dam on a Himalayan river in Himachal Pradesh holds back a lake called Gobind Sagar. Canals from it reach the dry fields of three states, and power lines carry its electricity to towns and tube wells.

**(i)** Name the project.
**Answer:** Bhakra Nangal.

**(ii)** Name the river.
**Answer:** Sutlej.

**(iii)** Name two states it irrigates.
**Answer:** Punjab and Haryana (also Rajasthan).

**(iv)** Name one other benefit.
**Answer:** Hydel power (also flood control).

### Case 2 — Fuel from the cowshed

> A family in a village fills a pit with cow dung and water. A dome above it collects a gas for their stove, and the waste that flows out goes to their fields.

**(i)** Name the gas.
**Answer:** Biogas (mainly methane).

**(ii)** Why does the gas form?
**Answer:** The dung decays without air.

**(iii)** How is the waste used?
**Answer:** As manure.

**(iv)** Give one environmental benefit.
**Answer:** No smoke; saves firewood (protects forests).

### Case 3 — Oil from the sea

> A helicopter flies 160 km out to sea from Mumbai to a platform where crude oil and gas are pumped from under the sea bed.

**(i)** Name this oil field.
**Answer:** Mumbai High.

**(ii)** Is it onshore or offshore?
**Answer:** Offshore.

**(iii)** Name another field producing mainly gas nearby.
**Answer:** Bassein.

**(iv)** Why is petroleum called liquid gold?
**Answer:** It is versatile and highly valuable; nothing goes waste.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Conventional (coal, oil, gas, hydel, wood) vs non-conventional (solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, nuclear, biogas).
- Coal: anthracite (best) > bituminous (industrial, coking, thermal power) > lignite (brown; Neyveli) > peat. Gondwana: Jharia (largest coking), Raniganj (oldest), Bokaro, Talcher, Singrauli, Korba, Singareni, Chandrapur. Tertiary: Makum (Assam), Meghalaya. Leaders: Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand.
- Petroleum: liquid gold; Digboi (oldest), Naharkatiya; Ankleshwar, Kalol (onshore Gujarat); Mumbai High (largest, offshore), Bassein, Aliabet (Gulf of Khambhat); KG basin; Barmer. Refineries near oilfields, ports, markets — Barauni (Bihar).
- Natural gas: cleaner, cheaper, piped; fertilisers; inflammable.
- Hydel: renewable, clean, cheap in the long run; costly, displacement. Bhakra Nangal — Sutlej, HP/Punjab, Gobind Sagar; Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan; irrigation, power, floods. Hirakud — Mahanadi, Odisha; floods, irrigation, power, waterways, fishing, malaria control.
- Solar (most potential; Bhadla), wind (Muppandal; farm income), tidal (Khambhat, Kutch), geothermal (Puga, Manikaran; few areas, costly), nuclear (uranium, thorium; Tarapur first, Kudankulam largest; waste), biogas (dung → methane; manure; clean).

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Naming coal for nuclear energy | Name uranium or thorium. |
| Naming an onshore field when asked for offshore | Offshore: Mumbai High, Bassein, Aliabet. |
| Solar or wind for "vast coastline" | Tidal energy (wind also accepted). |
| Hirakud on the Godavari / Bhakra on the Beas | Hirakud — Mahanadi; Bhakra Nangal — Sutlej. |
| Missing "northern" in a hydel question | Northern project — Bhakra Nangal. |
| Giving the use of coal when asked why it is preferred | Give high calorific value, easy availability. |
| Types of iron ore given for types of coal | Coal: anthracite, bituminous, lignite, peat. |
| "Geothermal is unpopular because it pollutes" only | Lead with: few areas, expensive, little energy. |
