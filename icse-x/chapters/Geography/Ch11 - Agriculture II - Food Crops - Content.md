## 1. Complete Lesson

### Food crops of India

**Food crops** are grown mainly to feed people. India's chief food crops are the **cereals** — **rice, wheat** and the **millets** (jowar, bajra, ragi; maize is grouped with them as a coarse cereal) — and the **pulses**. India is the **second-largest producer of rice and wheat** in the world and the **largest producer and consumer of pulses and millets**.

![Fig. 11.2 Climatic needs of the food crops (approximate ranges)](figures/g_ch11_fig02_food_crop_climate.png)

### 1. Rice

**Importance** — rice is the **staple food of most Indians**, especially in the **east, south and north-east**; it occupies the **largest area** under any crop in India. India is also the **world's largest exporter** of rice.

**Season** — mainly a **kharif** crop (sown with the monsoon in June–July, harvested in October–December). Where water is available, **two or three crops** are grown a year — in **West Bengal** they are called **aus** (autumn), **aman** (winter, the main crop) and **boro** (summer).

**Climatic conditions**
- **Temperature: 20–32 °C** (about 24 °C on average) — high temperature throughout the growing season.
- **Rainfall: 150–300 cm**; where rainfall is **less than 100 cm**, rice needs **irrigation** (Punjab, Haryana, Tamil Nadu).
- **Standing water** (flooded fields) during most of its growth.
- A **dry, sunny spell at harvest** helps the grain ripen.

**Soil** — **fertile, clayey or loamy alluvial soil**, with a **sub-soil of clay** that does **not let water drain away**; **deltaic alluvium** is ideal. Rice also grows on red and black soils with irrigation.

**Other conditions**
- **Flat, level land** — so that **water can stand evenly** in the field (in hilly areas, **terraces** make level steps).
- **Plenty of cheap labour** — for **transplanting** seedlings into puddled fields, **weeding**, and **harvesting with sickles**; most of the work is still done by hand.

**Methods of cultivation**
| Method | How |
|---|---|
| **Broadcasting** | Seeds **scattered by hand**; easy but low yield, uneven plants |
| **Drilling** | Seeds sown in **rows** by a seed drill |
| **Dibbling** | Seeds put in **holes** at intervals |
| **Transplantation** | **Seedlings raised in a nursery**, then **planted by hand** in the **puddled main field** — the **usual and best method** in India |
| **Japanese method** | An improved transplantation method — **HYV seeds**, raised **nursery beds**, transplanting **in rows**, **heavy manuring** and careful weeding — gives the **highest yields** |

![Fig. 11.1 Growing rice by transplantation](figures/g_ch11_fig01_rice_transplantation.png)

**Advantages of transplantation** — a **surer, healthier crop**; **economical use of water** (the nursery is small); **more production**; **less wastage of seeds**; **weeds are removed** easily; **easier harvesting** (plants in rows).

**After the harvest** — **threshing** (separating grain from the stalks), **winnowing** (removing chaff), and **husking / milling** (removing the husk — paddy becomes rice); some rice is **parboiled**.

**Distribution** — **West Bengal** (the **largest producer**), **Uttar Pradesh**, **Punjab**, **Andhra Pradesh**, **Telangana**, **Odisha**, **Chhattisgarh**, **Tamil Nadu**, **Bihar**, **Assam**.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2018, Q8(b))* With reference to rice cultivation: (i) Why does the cultivation of rice require a lot of manual labour? (ii) Mention two geographical conditions which suit the cultivation of rice.
**Solution:** (i) Rice has to be **transplanted in puddled fields** for a better crop, and it has to be **harvested carefully by sickle** — all this requires a lot of labour. (ii) Any two: **temperature 20–32 °C** (16–32 °C); **rainfall 150–300 cm**; **alluvial soil with a sub-soil of clay**.

### 2. Wheat

**Importance** — wheat is the **second most important food crop** and the **staple food of north and north-west India**. It was the chief crop of the **Green Revolution**.

**Season** — a **rabi** crop: **sown in October–November**, **harvested in March–April**.

**Climatic conditions**
- **Temperature: cool and moist** at sowing and growth — about **10–15 °C**; **warm and sunny (20–25 °C)** at **ripening**. (Ideal range about **10–20 °C** in the growing season.)
- **Rainfall: 50–100 cm**; the **winter rain from western disturbances** in north-west India is very helpful; elsewhere **irrigation** is used.
- **Bright sunshine** at ripening and harvest.
- **Frost** at flowering and **hailstorms** damage the crop.

**Soil** — **well-drained, fertile loamy (alluvial) soil**; also the **black soil** of the Deccan (Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra).

**Methods of cultivation** — the field is ploughed and levelled; seeds are sown by **drilling** (seed drill) or **broadcasting**; HYV seeds with **fertilisers** and **4–6 irrigations** (canals, tube wells); in Punjab and Haryana it is harvested and threshed with **combine harvesters**.

**Distribution** — **Uttar Pradesh** (the **largest producer**), **Madhya Pradesh**, **Punjab**, **Haryana**, **Rajasthan**, **Bihar**, **Gujarat**. The **Punjab–Haryana–western UP** belt has the **highest yields**.

**Why is wheat cultivation confined to the northern part of India?** Wheat is a **temperate crop**; only **north India** has **cool winters (10–20 °C)**; the north-west also gets **winter rain from western disturbances** and **50–100 cm** of rain, with **canal and tube-well irrigation**.

**Why is wheat not grown extensively in West Bengal and the extreme south?** Their **winters are too warm and humid**, without the **cool, dry growing season** wheat needs; heavy rain and high humidity cause **diseases (rust)**; and **rice** suits these areas better.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2019, Q8(d)(i))* Give a reason: cultivation of wheat is confined to the northern part of India.
**Solution:** The **ideal temperature of 10–20 °C** for wheat is available only in **north India** in winter; north India gets **winter rain** that suits its growth; **50–100 cm** of rain in north-west India; wheat is a **temperate crop**, and India is warm, so a cool climate is found only in the north in winter.

### 3. Millets (coarse grains)

**Millets** are **coarse grains** — **jowar** (sorghum), **bajra** (pearl millet) and **ragi** (finger millet). They are called **dry crops** because they grow in **dry areas with low rainfall (50–100 cm)**, **without irrigation**, on **poorer soils**, and are **drought- and heat-resistant** with a **short growing season**.

**Why are millets becoming popular food crops again?** They are **nutritious** (rich in fibre, minerals — ragi is rich in **calcium and iron**), a **cheaper option** for those who cannot afford rice and wheat, their **residue is good fodder**, they need **little water**, and the **government promotes them** ("Shree Anna"; 2023 — International Year of Millets).

| Millet | Season | Temperature | Rainfall | Soil | Leading states |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Jowar** | Mainly **kharif** (also rabi in Maharashtra, Karnataka) | **27–32 °C** | **50–100 cm** | **Black soil**, alluvial, red | **Maharashtra** (largest), Karnataka, Rajasthan, MP, Tamil Nadu |
| **Bajra** | **Kharif** | **25–35 °C** (hot, dry) | **40–60 cm** | **Sandy**, light and shallow black soils | **Rajasthan** (largest), UP, Haryana, Gujarat, Maharashtra |
| **Ragi** | Kharif | 20–30 °C | 50–100 cm | **Red**, black, sandy, loamy | **Karnataka** (largest), Tamil Nadu, Uttarakhand, Maharashtra |

**Soil requirement (general)** — **sandy alluvium**, **black** or **red** soils; millets grow on **poor, light soils** where other cereals fail.

**Uses** — **food** for poorer people in dry regions (roti, porridge); **fodder** for cattle; **poultry feed**; increasingly in **health foods**.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2019, Q8(b))* (i) Name two varieties of millet grown in India. (ii) What is the soil requirement for growing millet?
**Solution:** (i) Any two: **jowar, bajra, ragi**. (ii) Any one: **sandy alluvium / black / red** soil.

### 4. Pulses

**Pulses** are **leguminous crops** — **gram (chana)**, **tur (arhar)**, **urad**, **moong**, **masoor (lentil)**, **peas**, **rajma**, **lobia**. India is the **largest producer and consumer** of pulses (it also imports some).

**Importance**
1. The **main source of protein**, especially for **vegetarians** — dal is part of the daily diet.
2. **Leguminous** — **root nodules** with bacteria **fix atmospheric nitrogen** in the soil, **restoring soil fertility**; so pulses are **good rotation crops** (grown between cereal crops).
3. Need **little water** and grow in **dry areas**.
4. Plants and husk are good **cattle feed**.

![Fig. 11.3 Why pulses are good rotation crops](figures/g_ch11_fig03_pulses_nitrogen.png)

**Season** — **both**: **rabi pulses** — **gram, masoor, peas**; **kharif pulses** — **tur (arhar), urad, moong**.

**Climatic conditions** — **temperature 20–27 °C**; **rainfall 50–75 cm** — they are **dry crops**; **frost** and heavy rain are harmful.

**Soil** — a **variety of soils** — **loamy**, **black**, **red** and **light sandy loam**; **well-drained** soil.

**Distribution** — **Madhya Pradesh** (the **largest producer**), **Rajasthan**, **Maharashtra**, **Uttar Pradesh**, **Karnataka**, **Andhra Pradesh**. **Madhya Pradesh** leads in **gram**.

![Fig. 11.4 Leading states for rice, wheat, millets and pulses](figures/g_ch11_fig04_food_crops_map.png)

**Why are pulses a popular food crop?** They are **rich in protein** and **cheap**; part of the **daily diet** (dal) of most Indians; many varieties; easy to store.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2018, Q8(c)(iii))* Give a reason: the growing of pulses is important in India.
**Solution:** Pulses are a **source of proteins**, particularly for **vegetarians**; they are **good rotation crops**; being **leguminous**, they have a **nitrogen-fixing quality** in the soil; they are used as **cattle feed** (any one).

### A note on maize and soybean

- **Maize** is a **coarse cereal** used as **food and fodder** and in **starch and poultry feed**; mainly a **kharif** crop (21–27 °C, 50–100 cm, well-drained alluvial or red loams); grown in Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Telangana.
- **Soybean** is an **oilseed** (Chapter 12), but it is also a **protein-rich food**. Its cultivation is **gaining popularity** because it has a **high protein (about 40 %) and oil (about 20 %) content**, it is used for **oil, food products and cattle feed**, it fetches a **good price**, and as a **legume it fixes nitrogen**. **Madhya Pradesh** (the "Soya State") leads.

### Problems of food-crop farming and their remedies

| Problem | Effect | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Erratic monsoon** | Rice and millets fail in drought years | **Irrigation**, drought-resistant varieties, rainwater harvesting |
| **Low yields** of rice in the east | Less food from more land | **HYV seeds**, fertilisers, the **Japanese method**, better water control |
| **Over-use of water** for rice in Punjab–Haryana | **Falling water table** | **Crop diversification** (pulses, maize), drip irrigation, delayed paddy transplanting |
| **Soil exhaustion** from continuous cereals | Falling fertility | **Crop rotation with pulses**, organic manure, Soil Health Cards |
| **Neglect of pulses and millets** after the Green Revolution | Protein shortage; imports of pulses | **MSP for pulses and millets**, better seeds, "Shree Anna" campaign |
| **Pests and diseases** (rust of wheat, blast of rice) | Crop losses | Resistant varieties, timely spraying, integrated pest management |
| **Poor storage** | Grain spoils | **Warehouses**, silos, cold chains |

### Food security — storing and sharing the harvest

**Food security** means that **everyone has enough nutritious food at all times**. India achieves it through:
1. **Procurement** — the **Food Corporation of India (FCI)** buys wheat and rice from farmers at the **Minimum Support Price**.
2. **Buffer stocks** — grain is **stored in warehouses** to meet shortages in bad years.
3. **Public Distribution System (PDS)** — grain is sold at **low prices through ration (fair-price) shops**; under the **National Food Security Act (2013)** most families get subsidised grain.
4. **Mid-day meals** in schools and nutrition schemes for children and mothers.

Millets and pulses are now being added to the PDS and school meals to **improve nutrition**.

### Summary of the chapter

- **Rice** — staple; kharif (aus, aman, boro in WB); **20–32 °C, 150–300 cm** (irrigation below 100 cm); standing water; **clayey alluvium with clay sub-soil**; **flat land**, much labour; **transplantation** (Japanese method best); **West Bengal**, UP, Punjab, AP, Odisha.
- **Wheat** — rabi; **10–20 °C** (cool growth, warm sunny ripening); **50–100 cm**; western disturbances; **loamy alluvium**, black soil; drilling, irrigation; **Uttar Pradesh**, MP, Punjab, Haryana; confined to the north (cool winters).
- **Millets** — **dry crops**; jowar (black soil; **Maharashtra**), bajra (sandy; hot, dry; **Rajasthan**), ragi (red; **Karnataka**); nutritious, cheap, fodder; Year of Millets 2023.
- **Pulses** — protein; **leguminous — fix nitrogen**, rotation crops; rabi (gram, masoor) and kharif (tur, urad, moong); 20–27 °C, 50–75 cm; varied soils; **Madhya Pradesh**, Rajasthan, Maharashtra.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Water decides rice and wheat
Rice is a **thirsty, hot-weather** crop of the rainy east and south; wheat is a **cool-weather** crop of the north-west winters. Where the two meet (UP, Punjab), farmers grow **rice in kharif and wheat in rabi** on the same field.

### Section 2 — Why so many hands for rice?
Seedlings are raised, pulled and **transplanted one by one** into muddy fields; weeds are pulled by hand; the crop is cut with **sickles**. Cheap, plentiful labour in the densely populated east makes this possible.

### Section 3 — Hardy grains of dry lands
Millets survive heat and little rain on poor soils, so they feed the people of dry regions and their cattle. They are now valued again as **"nutri-cereals"**.

### Section 4 — The soil's friend
Pulses carry **nitrogen-fixing bacteria** in their root nodules, so a field after a pulse crop is richer — the reason they are grown in **rotation** with cereals.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Climate maps the menu** — rice in the wet east and south, wheat in the cool north-west, millets in the dry interior.
- **Labour and land shape methods** — transplanting in crowded deltas, machines on large Punjab farms.
- **Pulses protect soil and health** — protein for people, nitrogen for fields.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **North-East India:** rice is grown on terraces and in valleys; jhum fields grow upland rice, maize and millets together.
- **Your lunch box:** rice or roti (wheat), dal (pulses) and, increasingly, ragi or jowar snacks.
- **School gardens:** a crop of beans or peas before a vegetable crop enriches the soil naturally.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (2 marks) Name the three rice crops of West Bengal and the main one.
**Answer:** Aus, aman and boro; aman (winter rice) is the main crop.

**Q2.** (3 marks) State the season, rainfall and leading state of wheat.
**Answer:** Rabi; 50–100 cm (with western-disturbance rain or irrigation); Uttar Pradesh.

**Q3.** (3 marks) Name the leading state of: (a) bajra (b) ragi (c) pulses.
**Answer:** (a) Rajasthan. (b) Karnataka. (c) Madhya Pradesh.

**Q4.** (2 marks) Give two disadvantages of the broadcasting method for rice.
**Answer:** Seeds are wasted and plants grow unevenly; yields are low and weeding is difficult.

**Q5.** (3 marks) Give reasons: (a) rice is grown on terraces in hilly areas (b) wheat is harvested in March–April (c) millets are grown in Rajasthan.
**Answer:** (a) Terraces give level steps that hold standing water. (b) It is sown in winter and ripens in the warm, sunny spring. (c) Rainfall is low and soils are sandy; millets are drought-resistant.

**Q6.** (2 marks) Why is rice grown in Punjab though rainfall is low?
**Answer:** Assured irrigation from canals and tube wells, fertile soil and HYV seeds.

**Q7.** (3 marks) Compare rice and wheat in season, rainfall and soil.
**Answer:** Rice — kharif, 150–300 cm, clayey alluvium; wheat — rabi, 50–100 cm, loamy alluvium (also black soil).

**Q8.** (2 marks) Name two uses of millets other than food grain.
**Answer:** Fodder for cattle; poultry feed (also health foods).

**Q9.** (3 marks) Name the food crop: (a) needs standing water and clayey soil (b) cool winter crop helped by western disturbances (c) fixes nitrogen.
**Answer:** (a) Rice. (b) Wheat. (c) Pulses.

**Q12.** (2 marks) What is the Public Distribution System?

**Q13.** (2 marks) How does the Food Corporation of India help farmers and consumers?
**Answer:** It buys grain from farmers at the MSP and stores buffer stocks for distribution through ration shops.

**Answer:** They are rich in protein and cheap.
They form part of the daily diet (dal).

**Q8.** Why are millets becoming popular food crops?
**Answer:** They are nutritious and cheaper than rice and wheat.
Their residue is good fodder; the government promotes them.

**Q9.** State the climatic conditions of bajra.
**Answer:** Hot and dry, 25–35 °C.
Low rainfall, 40–60 cm.

**Q10.** Why is wheat not grown widely in West Bengal?
**Answer:** Winters are too warm and humid.
Heavy rain suits rice better; humidity causes disease.

**Q11.** Name the leading producers of jowar and ragi.
**Answer:** Jowar — Maharashtra.
Ragi — Karnataka.

**Q12.** State the soil and rainfall needed for pulses.
**Answer:** Loamy, black or red, well-drained soil.
50–75 cm of rain.

**Q19.** (a) Largest rice state (b) two conditions for rice.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Planning a crop)** A farmer in the Ganga delta has clayey soil, 180 cm of rain and plenty of family labour. Suggest the main crop and the method.
**Model answer:** Rice by transplantation — heavy rain, water-holding clay and labour suit it.

**Q2. (A dry village)** A village in western Rajasthan gets 45 cm of rain on sandy soil. Which food crop should it grow and why?
**Model answer:** Bajra — a drought-resistant millet suited to hot, dry conditions and sandy soil.

**Q3. (Restoring soil)** A Punjab farmer's soil has lost fertility after years of rice and wheat. Suggest a crop change.
**Model answer:** Rotate with a pulse such as moong or gram — its root nodules fix nitrogen.

**Q4. (A healthy lunch)** Suggest a meal using a cereal, a millet and a pulse, and one benefit of each.
**Model answer:** Rice (energy), ragi roti (calcium, iron) and dal (protein).

**Q5. (Moving south)** Why would a wheat farmer from Punjab struggle to grow wheat in Kerala?
**Model answer:** Kerala has no cool, dry winter; it is warm and humid with heavy rain, which suits rice, not wheat.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

### Case 1 — A delta village

> In July, a family in the Ganga delta pulls young green seedlings from a small bed and plants them by hand in a flooded field. In November they cut the crop with sickles.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Rice: staple of east/south; kharif (aus, aman, boro); 20–32 °C; 150–300 cm (irrigation below 100 cm); standing water; clayey alluvium, clay sub-soil; flat land; much labour; transplantation (Japanese method); West Bengal, UP, Punjab, AP, Odisha.
- Wheat: rabi; 10–20 °C growth, 20–25 °C ripening; 50–100 cm; western disturbances; loamy alluvium, black soil; drilling, irrigation, combines; UP, MP, Punjab, Haryana; confined to cool north.
- Millets: dry crops; jowar (27–32 °C, 50–100 cm, black soil, Maharashtra), bajra (25–35 °C, 40–60 cm, sandy, Rajasthan), ragi (red soil, Karnataka); nutritious, cheap, fodder.
- Pulses: protein; legumes fix nitrogen — rotation crops; rabi (gram, masoor, peas), kharif (tur, urad, moong); 20–27 °C, 50–75 cm; loamy/black/red soils; Madhya Pradesh.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| A single temperature value | Give the range — rice 20–32 °C; wheat 10–20 °C. |
| "Hot and wet" without figures | Give rainfall in cm — rice 150–300 cm. |
| Wheat as a kharif crop | Wheat is rabi; rice is kharif. |
| Millets need "less water" only | Say drought- and heat-resistant, grown without irrigation. |
| Punjab as the largest rice producer | West Bengal is the largest. |
| Pulses only "give protein" in a rotation question | Say they fix nitrogen and restore fertility. |
