## 1. Complete Lesson

### Manufacturing and its importance

**Manufacturing** is the **processing of raw materials into more valuable finished goods** with the help of machines — cane into sugar, cotton into cloth, iron ore into steel. It is a **secondary activity**.

**Importance of industries for India**
1. Make India **self-reliant** — goods for daily use and **defence equipment** are made at home.
2. Provide **employment** to millions.
3. **Raise the standard of living** and **national income**.
4. **Earn foreign exchange** through exports and help the **balance of trade**.
5. **Modernise agriculture** — supply fertilisers, machines, pumps — and give farmers a **market** for raw materials.
6. **Counter regional imbalances** by developing backward areas.

**Why are not all areas suitable for large-scale industry?** A large industry needs **raw materials, power, transport, water, capital, labour and markets** together, and level land. **Hilly, forested, desert or remote areas** lack many of these — poor transport, little power, few workers and small markets — so industries concentrate in favoured regions.

### Classification of industries

![Fig. 13.1 Classification of industries](figures/g_ch13_fig01_classification.png)

**1. On the basis of size (capital and labour)**

| Large-scale industries | Small-scale industries |
|---|---|
| **Huge capital investment** | **Less capital** |
| **Large number of workers** | **Fewer workers** |
| **Large area** of land; big factories | Small units, often in homes or small sheds |
| **Mass production** of goods | Goods produced in **smaller quantities** |
| e.g. **cotton mills, iron and steel, sugar mills** | e.g. **bicycle parts, soap, leather goods, food processing** |

**Cottage industries** are the smallest — run by **families at home** with **simple tools and local raw materials** (handloom, pottery, basketry, carpets).

**Why are small-scale and cottage industries significant?** They **provide employment** to skilled and unskilled labour, need **little capital** and **built-up area**, use **local raw materials** with **low transport cost**, meet **local needs**, **contribute to national income and exports**, and **keep traditional skills alive** from one generation to the next.

**2. On the basis of raw material**

| Type | Raw material from | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Agro-based** | **Agriculture** | **Cotton textile, sugar, jute, silk**, vegetable oil, food processing, tea, coffee, rubber |
| **Mineral-based** | **Minerals** | **Iron and steel, cement, aluminium, petrochemicals**, machine tools |
| **Forest-based** | **Forests** | **Paper, furniture**, matchboxes, lac, gum, resin, sports goods, medicines |
| **Marine-based** | Sea | Fish processing, sea salt |
| **Animal-based** | Animals | Leather, wool, dairy |

**3. On the basis of ownership**

| Sector | Owned and managed by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Public sector** | The **Government** (central or state) | **Bhilai, Rourkela, Visakhapatnam** steel plants; BHEL |
| **Private sector** | **Individuals or companies** | **Tata Steel**, Reliance, Bajaj |
| **Joint sector** | **Government and private** companies together | Oil India Ltd. (earlier), Maruti (at first) |
| **Cooperative sector** | A **group of individuals** (often the suppliers of raw material) who **own and operate the industry collectively** | **Sugar mills of Maharashtra**, **Amul** (milk) |

**4. On the basis of role / weight**

- **Basic (key) industries** — supply **materials or machines to other industries** — e.g. **iron and steel** (Chapter 14).
- **Consumer industries** — make goods **used directly by people** — sugar, soap, cloth, electronics.
- **Heavy industries** use heavy raw materials and make heavy goods (steel); **light industries** make light goods (watches, electronics).

### Agro-based industries

Agro-based industries use **farm produce** as raw material. They provide **rural employment**, give farmers a **market**, and are spread across the country.

#### 1. The sugar industry

**Importance** — the **second-largest agro-based industry** of India; India is one of the **two largest sugar producers** in the world (with Brazil) and the largest consumer. Sugar mills give jobs to lakhs of workers and a market to crores of cane farmers.

**Location — close to the cane fields.** Sugar mills are located **near the sugarcane-growing areas** because:
1. Sugarcane is **bulky** — only about a tenth of its weight becomes sugar, so carrying it far is costly.
2. It is **perishable** — it **starts losing its sucrose (sugar content) soon after it is cut**, so it **must be crushed within about 24 hours** (at most 48 hours).
3. Short distances **reduce transport cost**.

**Why is work in sugar mills seasonal?** Cane is **harvested only in one season** (roughly November–April) and must be crushed at once; the mills **run only during the crushing season** and stay idle for the rest of the year.

**Distribution**
- **North India** — **Uttar Pradesh** (the **largest number of sugar mills**: Meerut, Muzaffarnagar, Saharanpur, Gorakhpur, Lakhimpur Kheri), **Bihar**, **Haryana**, **Punjab**.
- **South India** — **Maharashtra** (Kolhapur, Pune, Satara, Sangli, Ahmednagar, Solapur), **Karnataka** (Belagavi, Mandya), **Tamil Nadu** (Coimbatore), **Andhra Pradesh, Telangana**.

![Fig. 13.5 The sugar industry: leading states and mill centres](figures/g_ch13_fig05_sugar_map.png)

**Why is the sugar industry shifting to (gaining importance in) peninsular India?**
1. **Favourable climate** — tropical, frost-free, no loo — gives cane with **higher sucrose content and yield**.
2. **Longer crushing season** (about 7–8 months against about 4 in the north).
3. **Fertile black soil** and **irrigation**.
4. **Large, modern, well-managed cooperative mills** located **near the plantations**; scientific farming; capital from the sugar cooperatives.

**Though Uttar Pradesh has the largest number of mills, Maharashtra often produces more sugar** — for the same reasons: **richer cane (higher sucrose)**, **higher yield per hectare**, a **longer crushing season**, and **large, efficient cooperative mills**.

**Why does the sugar industry continue to be a major industry of Uttar Pradesh?** UP is still the **largest producer of sugarcane**, the chief raw material.

**By-products of the sugar industry**

| By-product | What it is | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Bagasse** | The **crushed, rejected cane fibre** after juice is extracted | **Fuel in sugar mills**; **paper, cardboard**, fibre board |
| **Molasses** | The thick syrup left after sugar crystallises | **Alcohol (ethanol)**, yeast, cattle feed |
| **Press mud** | The mud left after juice is cleaned | **Wax** → **shoe polish, carbon paper**; **manure** |

**Problems of the sugar industry**
1. **Seasonal** nature; **short crushing season** in the north.
2. **Poor-quality cane** with low sucrose and low yields in the north.
3. **Old, obsolete machinery**; small, uneconomic mills.
4. **Power cuts**; **transport bottlenecks**.
5. Cane **diverted to gur and khandsari**; irregular raw-material supply.
6. **Under-use of by-products**.
7. **Cane price fixed by the government** while sugar prices fluctuate; **strikes and lockouts**.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2019, Q9(c))* With reference to sugar industries: (i) Why should these industries be located close to the sugarcane growing areas? (ii) Name two by-products. (iii) Mention one leading sugar-producing state in North India and one in South India.
**Solution:** (i) Any one: **sugarcane starts losing its sucrose** after it is cut; to **reduce transport cost**; it **must be crushed within 48 hours**; sugarcane is **perishable**. (ii) Any two: **molasses, bagasse, press mud**. (iii) North — **Uttar Pradesh** / Bihar / Haryana / Punjab; South — **Maharashtra** / Karnataka / Tamil Nadu / Andhra Pradesh / Telangana.

#### 2. The cotton textile industry

**Importance** — the **oldest and one of the largest** industries of India. The first successful modern cotton mill was set up in **Mumbai in 1854**.
1. Gives **employment** to millions — mills, powerlooms, **handlooms**, cotton farmers.
2. Earns valuable **foreign exchange** (cloth, yarn, garments).
3. Helps **decentralisation** of industry — mills spread across the country.
4. Meets the **high domestic demand** for cotton clothes in a **tropical** country.

![Fig. 13.4 Stages of the cotton textile industry](figures/g_ch13_fig04_cotton_textile.png)

**Why is the cotton textile industry widely distributed in India?** Raw cotton is **light and does not lose weight** in manufacture, so it can be carried anywhere; there is a **market for cloth everywhere**; **power** (grid electricity) and **cheap labour** are widely available; and the **rail and road network** links all regions.

**Major centres**
- **Maharashtra** — **Mumbai** ("Cottonopolis of India"), Solapur, Nagpur, Pune, Ichalkaranji.
- **Gujarat** — **Ahmedabad** ("Manchester of India"), **Surat**, Vadodara, Rajkot.
- **Tamil Nadu** — **Coimbatore** ("Manchester of South India"), Madurai, Tiruppur (knitwear), Salem.
- **Uttar Pradesh** — **Kanpur** ("Manchester of North India").
- **West Bengal** — **Kolkata** and the Hooghly belt (Howrah, Serampore).
- Also **Indore** (MP), **Ludhiana** (Punjab), **Delhi**, Bengaluru.

**Why has Mumbai become an important centre?**
1. **Raw cotton** from the nearby **black-soil region of the Deccan** (Maharashtra, Gujarat).
2. **Port** — to **import machinery** and long-staple cotton and to **export cloth**.
3. **Humid coastal climate** — the thread **does not break** during spinning.
4. **Cheap hydel power** from the Western Ghats (Tata hydro-electric projects).
5. **Capital, banking and credit** facilities; **skilled and cheap labour**; **rail and road network**; large market; soft water.

**Why is Ahmedabad important?** **Raw cotton** from the cotton belt of Gujarat; **humid climate**; **cheap labour**; **cheap power**; **port** (Kandla) and **transport**; **capital** from local merchants; a **large market**; soft water; government support.

**Why is Kolkata important though no cotton is grown nearby?** A **humid climate**; the **Kolkata port** (import of cotton, export of cloth); **coal from Raniganj and Jharia** for power; a **large market** in eastern India; **cheap, skilled labour**; **soft water from the Hooghly**; **capital**; a good **transport network**.

**Problems of the cotton textile industry**
1. An agro-based industry — **fluctuation in the supply of raw cotton** (depends on the monsoon); short-staple cotton.
2. **Obsolete machinery** and **outdated methods**; **low productivity of labour**.
3. **Sick mills** — many old mills are closed.
4. **Shortage of power**.
5. **Competition** from **synthetic fibres** and from **other countries** (China, Bangladesh, Egypt).

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q10(iv))* (a) State two locational advantages that have helped Mumbai to emerge as an important centre of cotton textiles. (b) Mention two types of silk produced in India.
**Solution:** (a) Any two: a **steady supply of raw cotton** from the nearby **black-soil region of the Deccan**; a **good transport network** to bring raw materials and distribute finished goods; a **humid climate** that favours spinning; **cheap hydel power**; **excellent financial and credit** facilities; **abundant cheap labour**. (b) Any two: **mulberry, eri, tussar, muga**.

#### 3. The silk industry

**Sericulture** is the **rearing of silkworms for the production of silk threads**. The silkworm feeds on leaves, spins a **cocoon** of a single long thread, and the cocoons are **boiled and reeled** to get raw silk, which is then woven.

![Fig. 13.3 From silkworm to silk](figures/g_ch13_fig03_sericulture.png)

India is the **second-largest producer of silk** in the world (after China) and its largest consumer. It is the only country that produces **all four kinds of silk**:

| Silk | Silkworm feeds on | Main areas |
|---|---|---|
| **Mulberry** (most of India's silk) | **Mulberry** leaves (*Bombyx mori* worm) | **Karnataka** (leading), Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal, Jammu & Kashmir |
| **Tasar (tussar)** — non-mulberry | Leaves of **arjun, asan, sal** trees in forests | **Jharkhand**, **Chhattisgarh**, **Madhya Pradesh**, Odisha, Bihar (Bhagalpur) |
| **Eri** — non-mulberry | **Castor** leaves | **Assam**, Meghalaya and the North-East |
| **Muga** — non-mulberry, **golden** | Som and soalu trees | **Assam** only |

**Why is Karnataka the leading silk state?**
1. A **moderate climate** suits **mulberry** and lets **silkworms (Bombyx mori) be reared throughout the year**.
2. **Soft, fresh, alkali-free water** for reeling.
3. **Traditional skill** — plenty of **skilled labour**, including women.
4. **Modern scientific technology**, **licensed seed nurseries**, research institutes.
5. **Government support and subsidies**; a **wide market**.

**Why is Mysore famous for silk?** **Mulberry** grows well around it; a **moderate climate** for rearing silkworms all year; a long **tradition** of royal patronage; the state's **silk-weaving factory** and the famous **Mysore silk sarees**.

**Other centres** — **Bengaluru, Ramanagara** (a large cocoon market), Kolar (Karnataka); **Kanchipuram** (TN); **Varanasi** (UP — brocades); **Murshidabad** (West Bengal); **Bhagalpur** (Bihar — tasar); **Sualkuchi** (Assam — muga and pat silk).

![Fig. 13.6 Cotton textile and silk centres](figures/g_ch13_fig06_textiles_map.png)

**Problems of the silk industry (why it is losing its market)**
1. **Competition from cheaper synthetic fibres** (nylon, polyester).
2. Silk is **expensive** — a **luxury good**, so its market is **small**.
3. **Competition from other countries** (especially China) — Indian silk is **costlier abroad**.
4. **Outdated looms**, poor **testing and grading**, designs not suited to **modern taste**, and a shortage of **skilled labour** in some areas.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2025, Q10(ii))* "Karnataka is the leading state for silk production in India." Give two reasons.
**Solution:** Any two: a **suitable climate for sericulture / mulberry trees**; a **wide market**; **silkworms reared all through the year** (Bombyx mori); **fresh, soft or alkali-free water**; **modern scientific technology**; **skilled labour**; **licensed nurseries**; **traditional skill**; sericulture provides **employment to women**; **government subsidy**.

### The three agro-based industries compared

| Feature | Sugar | Cotton textile | Silk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Raw material | Sugarcane (bulky, perishable) | Raw cotton (light) | Cocoons (mulberry, tasar, eri, muga) |
| Location | **Near cane fields** | **Widely spread**; near ports and markets | Near mulberry areas; traditional centres |
| Leading areas | UP (most mills), Maharashtra | Mumbai, Ahmedabad, Coimbatore, Kanpur, Kolkata | Karnataka (Mysore, Bengaluru) |
| Key terms | Bagasse, molasses, press mud; crushing season | Spinning, weaving; humid climate | Sericulture, reeling |
| Main problems | Seasonal; old mills; poor cane | Old machinery; sick mills; synthetics | Costly; synthetics; foreign competition |

### Summary of the chapter

- **Manufacturing** turns raw materials into finished goods; it gives self-reliance, jobs, income, foreign exchange and balanced development; needs raw materials, power, labour, market, transport, capital and water.
- **Classification** — size (**large, small, cottage**); raw material (**agro, mineral, forest**); ownership (**public, private, joint, cooperative**); role (**basic, consumer**).
- **Sugar** — near cane fields (bulky, loses sucrose — crush within 24 hours); **seasonal**; UP (most mills), **Maharashtra** (rich cane, long season, cooperatives); shift south; by-products **bagasse (fuel), molasses (alcohol), press mud (wax, shoe polish)**; problems.
- **Cotton textile** — oldest; Mumbai 1854; jobs, exports; widely spread; **Mumbai** (Deccan cotton, port, humid climate, hydel power, capital), **Ahmedabad**, **Kolkata** (port, humidity, coal, market), Coimbatore, Kanpur; problems — raw-material swings, old machines, sick mills, synthetics.
- **Silk** — **sericulture** = rearing silkworms; mulberry (Karnataka), tasar (Jharkhand, MP, Chhattisgarh), eri (Assam), muga (Assam); Karnataka — climate, soft water, skill, technology, support; problems — costly, synthetics, foreign competition.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Weight decides location
Industries using **heavy or perishable** raw materials (sugarcane) stay **near the source**; industries using **light, durable** raw materials (cotton) can go to **ports and markets**.

### Section 2 — Who owns it?
Government → **public sector** (Bhilai); companies → **private** (Tata Steel); both → **joint**; a group of farmers or users → **cooperative** (Maharashtra sugar mills, Amul).

### Section 3 — North versus south in sugar
The north has **more mills** but the south has **richer cane, longer seasons and better-run cooperatives** — so production is shifting south.

### Section 4 — Humidity spins cotton
Moist air stops cotton threads from snapping — one reason the first mills grew in **humid coastal Mumbai**.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Cowasjee Nanabhoy Davar (died 1873)
A Parsi businessman who set up the **Bombay Spinning and Weaving Company** in **1854**, the first successful modern cotton mill in India — the start of Mumbai's rise as a textile city.

### 6.2 Vitthalrao Vikhe Patil (1897–1980)
He founded Asia's first cooperative sugar factory at **Pravaranagar** (Maharashtra) in 1950 — the model for the cooperative sugar mills that made Maharashtra a leading sugar state.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Industry multiplies value** — a tonne of cane, cotton or cocoons becomes far more valuable as sugar, cloth or silk.
- **Location follows advantage** — raw material for sugar, ports and humidity for cotton, climate and skill for silk.
- **Cooperation works** — farmer-owned mills transformed sugar in Maharashtra.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya and Assam:** eri silk is woven at home in Ri-Bhoi and across the North-East; muga silk of Sualkuchi has a GI tag.
- **Your wardrobe:** cotton shirts from Tiruppur or Ahmedabad, a silk saree from Mysore or Kanchipuram.
- **Clean energy at the mill:** sugar mills burn bagasse to make their own power and sell surplus electricity to the grid.
