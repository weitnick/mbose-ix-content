## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*The Last Lesson* is one of the most famous stories of the French writer **Alphonse Daudet**. It is set in **Alsace**, a region on the border between France and Germany, in **1871**, just after France was defeated by **Prussia** in the **Franco-Prussian War**. The victors took over the French regions of **Alsace and Lorraine** and ordered that only **German** be taught in their schools.

The story is told by **Franz**, a young schoolboy who has never cared much for his French lessons. One morning he arrives late, expecting to be scolded — and instead discovers that this is the **last French lesson** that his teacher, **M. Hamel**, will ever give. In a single morning Franz realises how precious his **language** is, how much he has wasted, and what it means to lose one's **freedom and identity**.

The story's message is universal: **love your language**, and never put off learning till tomorrow — because tomorrow may never come.

### About the author

**Alphonse Daudet** (1840–1897) was a French novelist and short-story writer, born in Nîmes in the south of France. He is remembered for his warm, gently humorous and touching tales, especially *Letters from My Windmill* (1869) and the comic adventures of *Tartarin of Tarascon*. *The Last Lesson* (*La Dernière Classe*) was published in **1873** in his collection *Monday Tales* (*Contes du lundi*), stories written in the shadow of France's defeat in the war of 1870–71.

### Historical background

| Point | Detail |
|---|---|
| the war | the **Franco-Prussian War** (1870–1871) |
| the winner | **Prussia**, which led the newly united **German** states |
| the loss | France had to hand over **Alsace** and part of **Lorraine** |
| the order | only **German** to be taught in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine |
| "Berlin" | the capital of Prussia (later of Germany), from where the order came |
| the story's time | the first days of German rule in an Alsatian village |

### The story, retold

#### 1. A late start and a temptation

Franz is **very late** for school that morning and dreads being **scolded**, especially as M. Hamel has said he will question the class on **participles** — and Franz does not know the first thing about them. For a moment he thinks of **running away** and spending the day outdoors. It is a warm, bright day; birds are chirping at the edge of the woods; in the open field behind the sawmill, **Prussian soldiers are drilling**. All this tempts him far more than the rule for participles. But he resists and **hurries off to school**.

#### 2. The town hall

As he passes the **town hall**, Franz sees a **crowd in front of the bulletin board**. For the last **two years**, all the bad news has come from that board — the **lost battles**, the **draft** (orders for men to join the army), the orders of the commanding officer. He wonders, without stopping, what has happened now.

**Wachter the blacksmith**, who is there reading the bulletin with his apprentice, calls after him not to hurry so — he will reach school in plenty of time. Franz thinks he is **making fun** of him and runs on, arriving at school out of breath.

#### 3. A strange silence

Usually, when school begins, there is a **great bustle** that can be heard out in the street — desks opening and closing, lessons repeated loudly in unison, and the teacher's great **ruler** rapping on the table. Franz had counted on this noise to slip into his seat **unnoticed**.

But today everything is **quiet, as still as a Sunday morning**. Through the window he sees his classmates already **in their places** and M. Hamel walking up and down with his terrible iron ruler under his arm. Franz must open the door and walk in before everyone. He blushes and trembles. *(2027 specimen Q5(iii): everyone was quiet; the students were all seated; the usual bustle was missing.)*

To his surprise, **M. Hamel does not scold** him. He says, very kindly, that Franz should go to his place quickly; they were beginning without him.

#### 4. The teacher's best clothes and the village elders

Once seated, Franz notices that M. Hamel is wearing his **beautiful green coat**, his **frilled shirt** and his **little black silk cap, all embroidered** — clothes he wears only on **inspection** and **prize days**. The whole school seems strange and **solemn**.

Most surprising of all, the **back benches**, usually empty, are filled with **village people**, sitting as quietly as the children: **old Hauser** in his three-cornered hat, the **former mayor**, the **former postmaster**, and several others. Everyone looks sad. Old Hauser has brought an **old primer** (first reading book), thumbed at the edges, and holds it open on his knees with his great spectacles lying across the pages. *(2027 specimen Q5(ii).)*

#### 5. "My children, this is the last lesson I shall give you"

M. Hamel mounts his chair and, in the same grave and gentle tone, says:

> "My children, this is the last lesson I shall give you. The order has come from Berlin to teach only German in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine. The new master comes tomorrow. This is your last French lesson. I want you to be very attentive."

"**What a thunder-clap these words were to me!**" Franz says. Now he understands what was on the bulletin board at the town hall. *(2027 specimen Q5 extract.)*

#### 6. Franz's regret

**His last French lesson** — and he hardly knows how to write! He will never learn any more. He is sorry for the time he has wasted: lessons missed while he was **seeking birds' eggs** or **sliding on the Saar** (the river). His books — the **grammar** and the **history of the saints** — which only a moment ago seemed such a nuisance, now seem like **old friends** he cannot bear to give up. And the thought that M. Hamel is going away makes him forget the ruler and how **cranky** the teacher had been. *(2027 specimen Q5(iv): his biggest regret is not having learnt French when he had the chance.)*

He now understands why the old men of the village are sitting at the back: they too are **sorry they had not gone to school more**. It is their way of **thanking** M. Hamel for his **forty years** of faithful service, and of showing **respect** for the country that is theirs no more.

#### 7. The participles — and the blame

Franz hears his name called: it is his turn to recite the rule for **participles**. He would give anything to say it right through, loud and clear, without a mistake — but he gets mixed up at the first words and stands there, holding on to his desk, his heart beating, not daring to look up.

M. Hamel does not scold him. He says that Franz must feel bad enough already. **Every day** they have all told themselves: *Bah! I have plenty of time; I'll learn it tomorrow* — and now see where it has led. The people "out there" will have the right to say: you pretend to be French, and yet you can neither speak nor write your own language! But Franz is **not the worst**; **everyone** has something to blame himself for:

- the **parents**, who were not anxious to have their children learn, and preferred to put them to work on a farm or at the mills to earn a little more money;
- **M. Hamel himself**, who had sometimes sent the children to **water his flowers** instead of learning their lessons, and had given them a holiday when he wanted to go **fishing**.

#### 8. "The key to their prison"

Then M. Hamel speaks about the **French language**. He calls it **the most beautiful language in the world** — the clearest and most logical. The people of Alsace must **guard it** among themselves and **never forget it**:

> "… because when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison."

Then he opens a grammar and reads the lesson. Franz is amazed at how well he **understands** it. Everything M. Hamel says seems so easy. Franz thinks he had never listened so carefully, and M. Hamel had never explained everything with so much **patience** — as if, before going away, the poor man wanted to give them **all he knew** and put it all into their heads at one stroke.

#### 9. "France, Alsace, France, Alsace"

Next comes **writing**. M. Hamel has prepared new **copies** for them, written in a beautiful round hand: **"France, Alsace, France, Alsace."** Hung from the rods at the top of the desks, they look like **little flags** floating everywhere in the room.

Everyone works hard, and the room is so quiet that one hears only the **scratching of pens** on paper. Some **beetles** fly in, but nobody pays any attention — not even the littlest ones, who are working hard at their **fish-hooks** (the curved strokes children practise in early writing). On the roof the **pigeons coo** very softly, and Franz thinks: **Will they make them sing in German, even the pigeons?**

#### 10. Forty years in one place

Whenever Franz looks up, he sees M. Hamel sitting **motionless** in his chair, gazing first at one thing, then another, as if he wanted to fix in his mind just how everything looked in that little schoolroom. For **forty years** he has been there in the same place, with his **garden** outside the window and his class in front of him. Only the desks and benches have been worn smooth; the **walnut trees** in the garden are taller, and the **hop-vine** that he planted himself twines about the windows to the roof. It must break his heart to leave it all — and Franz can hear his **sister** moving about in the room upstairs, **packing their trunks**. They must leave the country the next day.

#### 11. Ba, be, bi, bo, bu

Still M. Hamel has the courage to hear every lesson to the very last. After the writing comes a lesson in **history**, and then the **babies** chant their **ba, be, bi, bo, bu**. Down at the back of the room, **old Hauser** puts on his spectacles and, holding his primer in both hands, spells the letters with them. His voice **trembles with emotion**. It is so funny to hear him that everyone wants to **laugh and cry**. Franz will remember that last lesson always.

#### 12. "Vive La France!"

All at once the **church clock strikes twelve**, then the **Angelus** rings. At the same moment the **trumpets of the Prussians**, returning from drill, sound under the windows.

M. Hamel stands up, **very pale**, in his chair. He has never looked so tall to Franz. "My friends," he says, "I — I —" But **something chokes him**; he cannot go on. He turns to the blackboard, takes a piece of chalk and, bearing on it with all his might, writes as large as he can:

> **"Vive La France!"** ("Long live France!")

Then he stops, leans his head against the wall and, **without a word**, makes a gesture with his hand: **School is dismissed — you may go.**

### Conclusion: what the story teaches

- **Love and respect your mother tongue**; it is part of your identity and freedom.
- **Do not put things off** till tomorrow — "Bah! I have plenty of time" — opportunities can vanish.
- **Everyone shares responsibility** for neglect: children, parents and teachers.
- **Language can resist oppression**: as long as people hold on to it, "it is as if they had the key to their prison".
- Teachers deserve **respect and gratitude**, as the villagers show for M. Hamel's forty years of service.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The morning and the temptation

- **What happens:** Franz is late, fears a scolding about participles, is tempted to spend the day outdoors, but hurries to school.
- **Points to note:**
  - **Participles** — a grammar topic; Franz has not learnt the rule.
  - The **warm, bright day**, the **birds** and the **Prussian soldiers drilling** behind the sawmill: nature tempts him; the soldiers remind us of the occupation.
  - He **resists** — a small sign of conscience.

### Section 2 — The bulletin board

- **What happens:** a crowd reads the bulletin board at the town hall; Wachter calls after Franz.
- **Points to note:**
  - The board has brought only **bad news for two years** — lost battles, the draft, the commanding officer's orders.
  - **Wachter's** remark that Franz will be in time seems mocking; really he knows school will never be the same. **Dramatic irony**: Franz does not yet know what the board says.

### Section 3 — The unusual silence

- **What happens:** school is silent; everyone is seated; Franz must walk in before them all; M. Hamel speaks kindly.
- **Points to note:**
  - **What was different (2027 specimen Q5(iii)):** everyone was quiet; the students were all seated; the usual bustle (desks banging, lessons repeated aloud, the ruler rapping) was missing.
  - "as still as a Sunday morning" — a **simile** showing unusual solemnity.
  - M. Hamel's **kindness** instead of anger is the first hint that something serious has happened.

### Section 4 — Signs of a special day

- **What happens:** Franz notices M. Hamel's best clothes and the villagers on the back benches.
- **Points to note:**
  - The **green coat, frilled shirt and embroidered black silk cap** are worn only on **inspection and prize days** — today is a day of great importance.
  - **Back benches (2027 specimen Q5(ii)):** the **elderly village folk** — old Hauser, the former mayor, the former postmaster and others.
  - **Their feelings (any two):** deep **respect** for M. Hamel's service; **shame** at not having valued their language; **regret** at having neglected education.

### Section 5 — The announcement

- **What happens:** M. Hamel announces the last French lesson.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The order has come from Berlin to teach only German in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine.**" — the conquerors impose their language.
  - "**What a thunder-clap these words were to me!**" — a **metaphor** for sudden shock; Franz is stunned. *(2027 specimen Q5(v))*
  - "**Oh, the wretches; that was what they had put up at the town-hall!**" — Franz now understands the bulletin board; "wretches" shows his anger at the Prussians.

### Section 6 — Regret and blame

- **What happens:** Franz regrets his wasted time; he fails at the participles; M. Hamel shares the blame among all.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - **"Bah! I've plenty of time. I'll learn it tomorrow."** — the habit of **procrastination** that M. Hamel blames for their loss.
  - Franz's books now seem like **old friends** — personification showing his change of heart.
  - **Everyone is to blame:** Franz (birds' eggs, sliding on the Saar); the **parents** (sending children to farms and mills for money); **M. Hamel** (sending pupils to water his flowers; holidays for fishing). Franz's biggest regret: **not having learnt French** when he had the chance. *(2027 specimen Q5(iv))*

### Section 7 — Language as the key

- **What happens:** M. Hamel praises French and explains why it must be kept alive; the grammar lesson.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**the most beautiful language in the world — the clearest, the most logical**" — his love and pride.
  - "**when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison**" — **metaphor**: language preserves identity and the hope of freedom. *(2027 specimen Q5(v): one lesson — language is the key to freedom for an enslaved people.)*
  - Franz **understands everything** — he had never listened so carefully; M. Hamel had never explained so patiently.

### Section 8 — The writing lesson and the pigeons

- **What happens:** the copies "France, Alsace"; the silence; the beetles and the pigeons.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - **"France, Alsace, France, Alsace"** like **little flags** — a symbol of patriotism and of the bond between Alsace and France.
  - The **scratching of pens** and the unnoticed **beetles** — total concentration.
  - "**Will they make them sing in German, even the pigeons?**" — a child's innocent but bitter question; it mocks the absurdity of forcing a language on people.

### Section 9 — M. Hamel's farewell

- **What happens:** M. Hamel gazes at his schoolroom; history and the babies' lesson; old Hauser spells with them; the clock, the Angelus and the Prussian trumpets; "Vive La France!"
- **Key lines explained:**
  - **Forty years** in the same place; walnut trees grown taller; the **hop-vine** he planted — his whole life is in this room.
  - His **sister packing** upstairs — they must leave the country the next day.
  - **Old Hauser's trembling voice** — "we all wanted to laugh and cry" — tragicomedy.
  - "**My friends, I — I —**" — choked with emotion.
  - "**Vive La France!**" — his final act of **patriotism** and defiance, written with all his might.
  - The silent **gesture** of dismissal — dignity in grief.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- After the Franco-Prussian War, Alsace and part of Lorraine remained under German rule until the end of the **First World War** (1918), when they returned to France.
- The region has long been **bilingual**; many Alsatians spoke a German dialect at home. Daudet's story is a patriotic French view written soon after the defeat.
- **Linguistic chauvinism** (forcing one language on others) has happened in many countries; India's Constitution protects linguistic diversity through its Eighth Schedule and the rights of linguistic minorities.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Language and identity:** a people's language is its identity and its "key" to freedom.
- **Patriotism:** love of country expressed through love of its language.
- **Procrastination and regret:** "I'll learn it tomorrow" leads to loss.
- **Shared responsibility:** children, parents and teachers all neglected education.
- **The cost of war and conquest:** defeat takes away not only land but language and culture.
- **Respect for teachers:** the village honours M. Hamel's forty years of service.

### 7.1 Irony and symbols

| Item | Meaning |
|---|---|
| the bulletin board | the voice of the conquerors; two years of bad news |
| Wachter's "plenty of time" | dramatic irony — there is no more time for French |
| M. Hamel's best clothes | the solemnity of the last day |
| the empty back benches now full | the whole village's regret and respect |
| "France, Alsace" like little flags | patriotism; Alsace's bond with France |
| the pigeons | nature cannot be forced to change its language — the absurdity of the order |
| the Prussian trumpets at noon | the end of the French lesson and the arrival of foreign rule |
| "Vive La France!" | defiance, love of country, hope |

### 7.2 Then and now: linguistic chauvinism

| In the story | In the world |
|---|---|
| German forced on French speakers | many countries have suppressed minority languages |
| "the key to their prison" | language keeps a culture alive under foreign rule |
| villagers regret neglecting their language | people today may neglect their mother tongue in favour of another |
| M. Hamel's pride in French | pride in one's language without contempt for others |

### 7.3 Significance of the title

"The Last Lesson" refers to the **last French lesson** in M. Hamel's school, but it also means the **last and most important lesson** he teaches: to love one's language, to value learning, and never to put it off. For Franz, it is also the **first lesson he truly learns**.

**(i) Who was M. Hamel? Which one of his character traits as a teacher do you find most appealing?** [3]
M. Hamel was the **teacher at the village school** in Alsace, who **taught French** (for forty years). *(Personal response — any one trait with a reason.)* **Model:** his **patience and dedication** — on the last day he explained everything so patiently that Franz understood it all, and he heard every lesson to the very end, even though his heart was breaking.

**(ii) Who else was present in the classroom on that day besides M. Hamel and the students? What feelings do you think these persons experienced as they sat in the classroom?** [3]
The **elderly village folk** — old Hauser, the former mayor, the former postmaster and others. Their feelings (any two): **deep respect** for M. Hamel's forty years of service to the village; **shame** at not having given enough importance to their language; **regret** at having neglected education.

**(iii) What felt different to Franz when he arrived late for school that morning?** [3]
**Everyone was quiet** — as still as a Sunday morning; the **students were all seated** in their places; the **usual bustle** — desks opening and closing, lessons repeated aloud in unison, the teacher's ruler rapping on the table — **was missing**. (Also: M. Hamel did not scold him, and was wearing his best clothes.)

**(iv) What is Franz's biggest regret about school? What aspects of his character do you relate to?** [3]
His biggest regret is **not having learnt French when he had the chance** — now he would **never learn any more**. *(Personal response — any two aspects.)* **Model:** like many students, he was a **typical boy** who found lessons **boring** and was **inattentive**, preferring to play outdoors (seeking birds' eggs, sliding on the Saar) and putting off study till "tomorrow".

**(v) What feeling does the expression 'thunder-clap' suggest? Use the expression in a sentence of your own with the same meaning as it appears in the passage. Mention one important lesson M. Hamel taught his students during his 'last French lesson'. Give a reason for your choice.** [4]
'Thunder-clap' suggests **shock** — Franz was **stunned**. **Sentence (model):** *The news that our school would close at the end of the term came as a thunder-clap to the whole town.* **One lesson (any one):** to **love one's language**; the **importance of knowing one's mother tongue**; **patriotism**; **language is the key to freedom** for an enslaved people. **Reason (model, for the last):** as long as the people of Alsace hold on to French, the conquerors cannot take away their identity; their language keeps alive their memory of France and their hope of freedom.

**2025 Q5.**

**(i) What does 'that' refer to? What other 'bad news' had the villagers received from the bulletin board?** [3]
'That' is **the order that only German was to be taught** in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine. For two years the bulletin board had brought all their bad news: **the lost battles**, **the draft** (calling men to the army) and **the orders of the commanding officer**.

**(ii) How does the narrator describe the daily bustle at the start of a typical school day?** [3]
There was a great noise that could be heard out in the street: **desks being opened and closed**, **lessons repeated aloud in unison**, the pupils covering their ears to learn better, and **the teacher's great iron ruler rapping** on the table.

**(iii) What was different on that morning when he arrived late for school?** [3]
Any three: everything was **still and quiet**, like a Sunday morning; his **classmates were already seated**; M. Hamel was walking up and down **but did not scold him**; M. Hamel wore his **Sunday best** — the beautiful green coat, frilled shirt and little black silk cap, worn only on inspection and prize days; the **village elders** (the former mayor, the former postmaster and others) sat **on the back benches**; there was an air of **sadness and solemnity**.

**(iv) What thoughts filled the narrator's head when he heard the announcement?** [3]
Franz felt **sorry for not learning his lessons**, for **seeking birds' eggs** and **sliding on the Saar** instead of going to school. The books that had seemed a nuisance and so heavy to carry — his **grammar and the history of the saints** — were now **old friends** he could not bear to give up. He was **sad that M. Hamel was going** away, and that now he would never learn any more French.

**(v) What does M. Hamel urge his listeners never to forget? What did he write on the board at the end? What does he hope to inspire?** [4]
He urges them to **guard their mother tongue, French**, and never forget it, because **when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison**. At the end he writes, as large as he can, "**Vive la France!**" (Long live France!). He hopes to inspire **love for their language** and **patriotism** for their country.

**2025 Improvement Q1(xvi).** **(a) not learnt his participles.** M. Hamel had said he would question them on participles, and Franz did not know the first word about them.

**2025 Improvement Q5.**

**(i) Why does Franz call M. Hamel 'poor man'? Why was this to be their 'last lesson'?** [3]
Because M. Hamel must **leave the school and the village after forty years** of faithful service; his life's work is ending. It was the last lesson because **an order had come from Berlin** that **only German was to be taught** in the schools of Alsace and Lorraine; the new master would come the next day.

**(ii) Describe the Sunday clothes M. Hamel wore.** [3]
His **beautiful green coat**, his **frilled shirt** and his **little black silk cap, all embroidered** — clothes he wore only on **inspection and prize days**.

**(iii) Why were the old men of the village seated at the back?** [3]
To **thank M. Hamel for his forty years of faithful service** and to **show their respect for the country that was theirs no more**. They also seemed **sorry they had not gone to school more** — old Hauser had brought his old primer to read along.

**(iv) Why does M. Hamel blame himself? Whom else does he blame?** [3]
He admits he had often **sent pupils to water his flowers** instead of learning their lessons, and **given them a holiday when he wanted to go fishing**. He also blames **the parents**, who were not anxious to have their children learn and **put them to work on a farm or at the mills** to earn a little more money — and the Alsatian habit of **putting off learning till tomorrow**.

**(v) How does M. Hamel bring out the importance of one's language? What does this reveal of his character?** [4]
He says **French is the most beautiful language in the world — the clearest, the most logical** — and that they must **guard it among themselves and never forget it**, because **when a people are enslaved, as long as they hold fast to their language it is as if they had the key to their prison**. He teaches his last lesson with great patience and ends by writing "**Vive la France!**" on the board. It reveals him as **deeply patriotic**, **devoted to his duty** as a teacher, and **honest** enough to admit his own faults.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> "I won't scold you, little Franz; you must feel bad enough. See how it is! Every day we have said to ourselves, 'Bah! I've plenty of time. I'll learn it tomorrow.' And now you see where we've come out."

**(i)** What had Franz just failed to do? How did he feel? [3]
**Answer:** He had failed to **recite the rule for participles**; he got mixed up at the first words and stood **holding on to his desk**, his heart beating, **ashamed** and not daring to look up.

**(ii)** What does M. Hamel mean by "you see where we've come out"? [3]
**Answer:** By always **putting off learning** till tomorrow, they have arrived at a day when there is **no tomorrow** for French — the language is being taken from them, and they cannot even speak or write it properly.

**(iii)** What will "those fellows out there" have the right to say? [3]
**Answer:** That the people of Alsace **pretend to be French**, yet can **neither speak nor write their own language** — a humiliating taunt from the conquerors.

**(iv)** Whom else does M. Hamel blame? [3]
**Answer:** The **parents**, who preferred to put their children to work on **farms and in mills** to earn more money; and **himself**, for sending the children to **water his flowers** and giving them a holiday when he wanted to go **fishing**.

**(v)** What do you learn from M. Hamel's words? Give a reason. [4]
**Answer:** *(Personal response.)* **Model:** never **procrastinate** — what we put off may become impossible; and **responsibility is shared**: M. Hamel does not put all the blame on a child but honestly admits his own faults. This makes his lesson more powerful, because he practises the honesty he teaches.

**Extract B.**
> Then, from one thing to another, M. Hamel went on to talk of the French language, saying that it was the most beautiful language in the world — the clearest, the most logical; that we must guard it among us and never forget it.

**(i)** Why does M. Hamel speak so warmly of French on this day? [3]
**Answer:** It is the **last French lesson**: an order from **Berlin** says only **German** may be taught in Alsace and Lorraine from tomorrow; he wants his pupils to **love and keep** their language.

**(ii)** What reason does he give for guarding the language? [3]
**Answer:** When a people are **enslaved**, as long as they **hold fast to their language**, it is as if they had **the key to their prison** — their identity and hope of freedom survive.

**(iii)** How does Franz respond to the grammar lesson that follows? [3]
**Answer:** He is amazed at how well he **understands** it; everything seems easy; he had **never listened so carefully**, and M. Hamel had never explained with **so much patience**.

**(iv)** Describe the writing lesson. [3]
**Answer:** M. Hamel had prepared new **copies** in a beautiful round hand — "**France, Alsace, France, Alsace**" — which, hanging from the desk-rods, looked like **little flags**; everyone worked silently, with only the **scratching of pens**.

**(v)** What does Franz wonder about the pigeons, and what does it show? [4]
**Answer:** He wonders whether they will make **even the pigeons sing in German**. It shows a child's **innocent but bitter** humour: the order to change a people's language is so **unnatural and absurd** that it seems the conquerors would like to change even the birds; it also shows Franz's new **love for French** and anger at the occupation.

### 8.3 Writing practice linked to the story (Paper 1 skills)

**Task A (argumentative).** "Every student should study their mother tongue at school." Express your views for or against.

**Plan:** stand → identity and culture (M. Hamel's "key to their prison") → thinking and communicating with family and community → the value of other languages too (balance) → conclusion.

**Task B (narrative).** Write about the last day of something you valued — a school, a teacher, a house, a tradition.

**Tips:** begin with an ordinary detail that turns out to be different; show the change through small observations (clothes, silence, faces); include one moment of strong feeling; end with a single image or gesture, as Daudet ends with "Vive La France!" and the silent wave of a hand.
