## 1. Complete Lesson

### What Question 1 asks

Question 1 of Paper 1 gives **five topics**, and you write on **one** of them in **300–350 words**. The five topics always cover the same five types:

| Type | What it asks | Specimen examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Short story (narrative) | an original story — often with a given title, given opening words, or given characters | 2024: *A Narrow Escape*; 2025: a woman, her strange neighbour and a police officer; 2027: begin with "He was the funniest boy I had ever met…" |
| 2. Personal account / experience | a first-person account of an experience and what you learnt | 2024: forgiving someone when it was hard; 2025: a group project; 2027: punished for something you had not done |
| 3. Argument (for or against) | your views on a statement, taking a clear side | 2024: "Competition hampers the overall development of students"; 2025: "Peer pressure is a force for good"; 2027: "Too many cooks spoil the broth" |
| 4. Description | a place, scene or event described through the senses | 2024: a stroll in a beautiful garden; 2025: the locality in which you live |
| 5. Picture composition | a story, description or account suggested by a photograph | every year: "there must be a clear connection between the picture and your composition" |

*(2027 specimen: topic (iv) — a class project, planning and benefits — is set as an account with an evaluative angle.)*

### What the examiners look for

The CISCE draft marking scheme for 2027 states what each type must contain:

| Type | CISCE's guidance (2027 scheme, in brief) |
|---|---|
| story | original; must begin with the suggested words; the key element (e.g. "the funny or strange behaviour of the child") must be brought out; a clear, structured plot |
| personal account | first-person; within the plausible experience of a Class X student; the incident described clearly, highlighting where things went wrong; the steps taken (e.g. to prove innocence) included |
| argument | views either for or against accepted; take a **clear stand**; give **valid reasons**; a cohesive, logical argument based on reasoning and personal experience |
| account of a project | the project, the group, the sights and sounds of planning as the main body; how it would be beneficial |
| picture | wide interpretation accepted; ideas from the picture accepted; **a mere listing or description of the picture is to be avoided** |

**Core9X marking guide (20 marks):**

| What | Marks | What earns it |
|---|---|---|
| Content and relevance | 8 | answers exactly what the topic asks; every part of the topic covered; fresh, specific ideas |
| Organisation | 4 | clear beginning, middle and end; one idea per paragraph; smooth links |
| Expression | 6 | vivid, varied vocabulary; varied sentences; suitable tone |
| Accuracy | 2 | grammar, spelling and punctuation |

### Choosing your topic (1 minute)

Choose the topic on which you have **the most to say** and can **finish well**, not the one that looks impressive. Ask:

1. Do I understand **every part** of the topic? (e.g. "how you proved your innocence" is a required part.)
2. Can I think of **three or four** clear points or events?
3. Do I know a **real or realistic** situation to draw on?

### Planning (4 minutes)

Write a quick plan before you begin:

| Part | Words (approx.) | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 50–60 | hook the reader; set the scene or state your stand |
| Body 1 | 70–80 | first event or point, with detail |
| Body 2 | 70–80 | second event or point, with detail |
| Body 3 | 60–70 | climax / strongest point / main description |
| Ending | 40–50 | resolution, reflection or conclusion |

### Type 1 — The short story

- **Follow the instruction exactly:** if given opening words, begin with them **word for word**; if given characters, make each one important.
- **One main event**, with a problem (conflict), a rising action, a **climax** and a **resolution**.
- **Show, don't tell:** "My hands shook as I turned the key" rather than "I was afraid".
- **Dialogue** — a few short lines bring characters to life. Punctuate it correctly.
- **Keep the cast small:** two or three characters are enough in 350 words.
- **End clearly** — a twist, a lesson or a satisfying resolution; never "and then I woke up — it was a dream".

### Type 2 — The personal account

- Written in the **first person**, in the **past tense**.
- Must be **believable** for a Class X student (CISCE: "within the plausible experience of a Class X candidate").
- Include **when, where, who, what happened**, **how you felt**, and **what you learnt**.
- Cover **every part** of the question: if it asks "how you proved your innocence", that must be a full paragraph.

### Type 3 — The argument (for or against)

- **Take one side** and keep to it. Do not write "both sides are right" — CISCE asks for "a clear stand".
- **Introduction:** restate the statement and give your stand in one clear sentence.
- **Three body paragraphs**, each with **one reason**, an **explanation** and an **example** (from experience, school life, news or history).
- **One paragraph** may briefly mention the opposing view and **answer** it.
- **Conclusion:** restate your stand in fresh words; end with a strong line.
- Use **linking words:** *firstly, moreover, furthermore, on the other hand, however, therefore, in conclusion*.

### Type 4 — The description

- Organise the description **spatially** (as you walk along) or **by senses**.
- Use **all five senses**: sight, sound, smell, touch, taste.
- Choose **precise nouns, verbs and adjectives** ("the clatter of a pressure cooker", not "a noise").
- Use **figures of speech** sparingly — a well-chosen simile or personification.
- Include **people and activity** — a place is alive through the people in it.
- End with your **feelings** about the place (the 2025 topic asked what you "especially like").

### Type 5 — The picture composition

- Look at the picture for **one minute**: who, where, what is happening, what mood?
- **Choose:** a story, a description or an account — your choice must have a **clear connection** to the picture.
- **Do not simply describe or list** what is in the picture — use it as a **starting point**.
- Mention a detail from the picture early, so the examiner sees the link.

### Openings that work

| Kind of opening | Example |
|---|---|
| a vivid scene | The rain had been falling for three days when the river finally broke its banks. |
| dialogue | "Don't open that door," my grandmother whispered. |
| a question | Can a single lie change the way a whole class sees you? |
| a striking statement (argument) | Competition is not the enemy of growth; it is the engine of it. |
| a sense impression (description) | The smell of frying samosas reaches me before I turn the corner. |

### Endings that work

| Kind of ending | Example |
|---|---|
| a lesson learnt | I learnt that day that honesty is not only about telling the truth, but about standing by it. |
| a twist | The parcel on the doorstep was addressed to me — in my own handwriting. |
| a return to the opening image | The river was calm again, but none of us would ever look at it the same way. |
| a strong restatement (argument) | Peer pressure, then, is a force — but whether it is a force for good depends entirely on us. |

### Word count and time

- **300–350 words** is about **one and a half pages** of normal handwriting. Count the words in one line, multiply by the number of lines.
- Spend **1 minute choosing, 4 planning, 22 writing, 3 checking**.
- Going far beyond 350 words wastes time needed for Questions 2–5 and increases the chance of errors.

### Common mistakes

- Not beginning with the **given opening words** (or changing them).
- Leaving out a **required part** of the topic.
- In an argument, **arguing both sides** without a clear stand.
- In a picture composition, **only describing the picture**.
- Writing a story with **too many characters** or events.
- **"It was all a dream"** endings.
- Long, **unparagraphed** blocks of writing.
- **Tense shifts** in a narrative (mixing past and present).
- Ignoring **spelling and punctuation**, especially in dialogue.

## 2. Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, students will be able to:

1. **Identify** the five types of composition set in ICSE Paper 1 Question 1.
2. **Choose** a topic wisely and **plan** a 300–350-word composition in four minutes.
3. **Write** an original short story with a clear plot, following any given opening words or characters.
4. **Write** a personal account that covers every part of the topic and reflects on the experience.
5. **Write** a well-organised argument that takes a clear stand with valid reasons and examples.
6. **Write** a vivid description using the senses, and a picture composition with a clear link to the picture.
7. **Check** their writing for relevance, organisation, expression and accuracy.

## 4. Vocabulary

### 4.1 Linking words and phrases

| Purpose | Words and phrases |
|---|---|
| adding a point | moreover, furthermore, in addition, besides, what is more |
| contrasting | however, on the other hand, nevertheless, yet, although, whereas |
| giving reasons | because, since, as, owing to, due to |
| showing results | therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence |
| giving examples | for instance, for example, such as, to illustrate |
| sequencing events | at first, soon, meanwhile, suddenly, moments later, eventually, finally |
| concluding | in conclusion, to sum up, all things considered, on the whole |

### 4.2 Words for describing feelings

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| happy | delighted, elated, overjoyed, content, relieved |
| sad | dejected, heartbroken, gloomy, crestfallen, miserable |
| afraid | terrified, uneasy, apprehensive, petrified, alarmed |
| angry | furious, indignant, irritated, exasperated, livid |
| surprised | astonished, stunned, taken aback, amazed |
| nervous | anxious, jittery, restless, on edge |

### 4.3 Words for describing places and senses

| Sense | Useful words |
|---|---|
| sight | glittering, faded, towering, cramped, sprawling, dappled, hazy |
| sound | clatter, murmur, rustle, honk, chatter, hum, creak, thud |
| smell | fragrant, pungent, musty, earthy, smoky, the aroma of |
| touch | rough, smooth, damp, sticky, icy, velvety |
| taste | tangy, bitter, sweet, spicy, savoury |

### 4.4 Words for arguments

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| stating a stand | I firmly believe that…; I strongly disagree with the view that… |
| giving an opinion | In my view…; It seems to me that…; From my experience… |
| conceding | It is true that…; Admittedly…; Some may argue that… |
| refuting | However, this overlooks…; This argument fails to consider… |
| concluding | Clearly, …; For these reasons, I maintain that… |

**2026 Q1(i).**

**Type:** short story, beginning with the given words **exactly**.

**What the examiner looks for:** an original plot that grows from the opening — who rang and why; suspense; a clear climax and resolution.

**Ideas:** a neighbour's child, lost and frightened; a courier with a mysterious parcel; an old friend in trouble; a prank by cousins that turns into a real emergency.

**Plan:** (1) The opening words; the household's alarm. (2) Who goes to the door, and the first surprise. (3) The tension builds — a stranger's story, a sound outside. (4) Climax. (5) Resolution and a closing reflection.

**2026 Q1(ii).**

**Type:** reflective account (views with reasons).

**What the examiner looks for:** **one** period only; **why** it should change; **what** you would put in its place and **why** that is better (compare CISCE's 2023 scheme for a similar topic: one change, why it is necessary, the benefits and a plan).

**Plan:** (1) The period — e.g. a second Mathematics period on Saturdays, or a free period that is wasted. (2) Why it needs changing — fatigue, repetition, poor use of time. (3) The replacement — a library, yoga, coding, life-skills or gardening period. (4) Benefits to students. (5) Conclusion.

**2026 Q1(iii).**

**Type:** description with reasons.

**What the examiner looks for:** a vivid description using the **senses** (sights, smells, sounds, tastes) and **reasons** for your liking.

**Plan:** (1) The place — a roadside momo stall or a small family restaurant. (2) Sights — steam rising, the cook's quick hands, bright signboards, the crowd. (3) Smells and sounds — spices, sizzling oil, the clatter of plates, the owner's greeting. (4) Your favourite dish and its taste. (5) Why you love it — taste, value, the owner's warmth, family memories.

**2026 Q1(iv).**

**Type:** argumentative composition.

**What the examiner looks for:** a **clear stand**, valid reasons and a logical argument (CISCE's guidance for argument topics every year).

**For:** homework reinforces classwork, builds discipline and independent study, and shows teachers and parents where a student needs help — necessary, even if unwelcome.

**Against:** too much homework causes stress, takes away time for play, family, hobbies and sleep; learning should be completed in school; meaningful projects are better than routine exercises.

**Plan:** stand → three reasons with examples → reply to the other side → conclusion.

**2026 Q1(v).**

**Type:** picture composition.

**What the examiner looks for:** a wide interpretation with a clear connection; a mere description of the picture is penalised.

**Ideas:** a story of two friends who build a "bike" from scrap and dream of a real ride; a child who later becomes an engineer; an account of imagination and play without expensive toys; a message about road safety (they even wear helmets).

**Plan:** (1) Setting and characters. (2) Building the bike. (3) An adventure of the imagination or a real-life event. (4) Ending and message.

**2026 Improvement Q1(i).**

**Type:** short story.

**What the examiner looks for:** an original plot in which **a misunderstanding** is clearly shown, is **cleared up**, and leads to **an unexpected friendship**.

**Ideas:** a new student thought to be proud is really shy and hard of hearing; two neighbours quarrel over a "stolen" bicycle that was only borrowed; a boy blames a classmate for a lost book that his own dog had taken.

**Plan:** (1) The two characters and the first meeting. (2) The misunderstanding and its effects. (3) The truth comes out. (4) Apology and the beginning of friendship. (5) A closing scene showing the friendship.

**2026 Improvement Q1(ii).**

**Type:** personal account with reflection (first person).

**What the examiner looks for:** the **incident**, your **feelings** at the time, and how the experience **changed your outlook**.

**Ideas:** giving up a place on a school trip so a friend who had never travelled could go; giving your new bicycle to a cousin who walked miles to school; letting a younger sibling have the solo in the school concert.

**Plan:** (1) What you loved and why. (2) The situation that called for the sacrifice. (3) Your struggle and decision. (4) The other person's happiness. (5) What you learnt — the joy of giving, empathy, that happiness grows when shared.

**2026 Improvement Q1(iii).**

**Type:** description with reflection.

**What the examiner looks for:** a vivid description of **the quiet evening**, **how it differed** from usual evenings, and **what you gained**.

## 9. MCQs with Explanations

### Format and requirements (Q1–Q12)

**Q1.** In ICSE Paper 1, the composition in Question 1 should be
- (a) 500–600 words
- (b) 150–200 words
- (c) 300–350 words
- (d) 50 words

**Answer:** (c) 300–350 words
**Explanation:** The word limit stated in the specimen papers.

**Q2.** Question 1 carries
- (a) 5 marks
- (b) 10 marks
- (c) 8 marks
- (d) 20 marks

**Answer:** (d) 20 marks
**Explanation:** The largest single question in Paper 1.

**Q3.** CISCE advises spending no more than
- (a) 10 minutes on Question 1
- (b) 30 minutes on Question 1
- (c) 60 minutes on Question 1
- (d) 5 minutes on Question 1

**Answer:** (b) 30 minutes on Question 1
**Explanation:** And 20 minutes on Question 2.

**Q4.** How many topics are offered in Question 1?
- (a) three
- (b) two
- (c) five
- (d) one

**Answer:** (c) five
**Explanation:** You write on any one.

**Q5.** Which of these is NOT one of the five types usually offered?
- (a) a picture composition
- (b) a short story
- (c) an argument for or against
- (d) a poem

**Answer:** (d) a poem
**Explanation:** Compositions are in prose.

**Q6.** If a story topic gives opening words, you must
- (a) begin with those exact words
- (b) use them somewhere in the middle
- (c) change them slightly
- (d) use them as the title only

**Answer:** (a) begin with those exact words
**Explanation:** The 2027 scheme: "must begin with the suggested words".

**Q7.** In an argumentative composition, CISCE expects you to
- (a) write a story
- (b) give both sides equally without a conclusion
- (c) avoid giving reasons
- (d) take a clear stand for or against

**Answer:** (d) take a clear stand for or against
**Explanation:** "The candidate should take a clear stand."

**Q8.** A picture composition must
- (a) be a poem about the picture
- (b) describe every object in the picture
- (c) ignore the picture
- (d) have a clear connection with the picture

**Answer:** (d) have a clear connection with the picture
**Explanation:** "A mere itemization / listing / description of the picture is to be avoided."

**Q9.** A personal account in the 2027 scheme must be
- (a) written in the third person
- (b) about a famous person
- (c) within the plausible experience of a Class X student
- (d) fictional and fantastic

**Answer:** (c) within the plausible experience of a Class X student
**Explanation:** It must be believable.

**Q10.** The best way to use the first few minutes is to
- (a) count the words in the question
- (b) start writing at once
- (c) choose a topic and make a quick plan
- (d) copy the topic several times

**Answer:** (c) choose a topic and make a quick plan
**Explanation:** About five minutes in all.

**Q11.** Writing far more than 350 words
- (a) is required for top marks
- (b) always earns extra marks
- (c) wastes time and increases errors
- (d) is necessary in a picture composition

**Answer:** (c) wastes time and increases errors
**Explanation:** Relevance and quality earn marks.

**Q12.** **Statement I:** In the 2027 specimen, topic (ii) asked for an account of being punished unfairly. **Statement II:** It also required the steps you took to prove your innocence.
- (a) Both the statements are true.
- (b) Both the statements are false.
- (c) Statement I is true, but statement II is false.
- (d) Statement I is false, but statement II is true.

**Answer:** (a) Both the statements are true.
**Explanation:** Every part of the topic must be covered.

### Stories and accounts (Q13–Q26)

**Q13.** A good short story in 350 words should usually have
- (a) many unrelated events
- (b) ten characters
- (c) two or three characters and one main event
- (d) no conflict

**Answer:** (c) two or three characters and one main event
**Explanation:** Keep it focused.

**Q14.** The most important moment of tension in a story is the
- (a) introduction
- (b) title
- (c) climax
- (d) word count

**Answer:** (c) climax
**Explanation:** The turning point.

**Q15.** "Show, don't tell" means

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (20 marks each, 300–350 words)

### Stories (20 marks each)

**Q1.** Write an original short story that begins with the words: 'He was the funniest boy I had ever met…' *(ICSE 2027 specimen)*
**Model answer:** He was the funniest boy I had ever met, and he never once tried to be funny. Kabir joined our class in the middle of the term, wearing two different socks and carrying his books in a cricket-kit bag. When our teacher asked him to introduce himself, he bowed to the blackboard and said, "Good morning, everyone. I am new, but my jokes are old."

The class roared. Kabir looked puzzled; he had meant it. That was his gift: he said exactly what he thought, and what he thought was always a little sideways from everyone else. When the science teacher asked why leaves are green, he answered, "Because they are not ripe yet." When we lost a football match nine–nil, he shook hands with every player on the other team and thanked them "for the lesson".

Not everyone found him amusing. Rohit, our class captain, called him "the clown" and laughed at him, not with him. Kabir only shrugged.

Then came the inter-school quiz. Our team of four was one member short when Rohit fell ill, and our teacher, with some doubt, chose Kabir. In the final round we were three points behind. The question was: "Which animal sleeps standing up and can run soon after birth?" Our team froze. Kabir raised his hand calmly. "A horse," he said. "I know because I asked my uncle's horse." The quizmaster laughed — and gave the points. Then Kabir answered the tie-breaker about the Indian flag's proportions without a pause: "Three to two. I measured ours during assembly when I was bored."

We won. On the bus back, Rohit sat beside Kabir. "How do you know such strange things?" he asked.

Kabir thought about it. "I don't stop asking questions just because people laugh," he said.

From that day, nobody called him a clown. He was still the funniest boy I had ever met — but now we knew the jokes came from a mind that never stopped wondering.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: begins with the given words; the boy's funny, strange behaviour is clearly brought out; a structured plot with a turning point (the quiz) — 8 marks
- Organisation: introduction of the character, conflict (Rohit), climax, resolution — 4 marks
- Expression: dialogue, humour, varied sentences — 6 marks
- Accuracy: grammar, spelling, punctuation of dialogue — 2 marks

**Q2.** Write an original short story entitled: 'A Narrow Escape'. *(ICSE 2024 specimen)*
**Model answer:** The rope bridge swayed as I stepped onto it, and I gripped the frayed railing until my knuckles turned white. Below, the river roared over black rocks, swollen from three days of rain. "Just don't look down," my cousin Tara called from the far side. I looked down. That was my first mistake that afternoon; it was very nearly my last.

We had come to our grandparents' village in the hills for the holidays, and Tara had promised to show me a waterfall "that nobody from the city has ever seen". The path to it crossed this old bridge, used by villagers every day. "It's perfectly safe," she had said.

Halfway across, I heard a sharp crack. One of the wooden planks beneath my right foot split, and my leg plunged through the gap. I fell forward, my chin striking the plank ahead, my school bag swinging wildly from my shoulder. The whole bridge lurched sideways. For a terrible moment I was hanging by my arms, my legs dangling over the foaming water.

"Hold on!" Tara screamed. "Don't move!"

I could not have moved if I had wanted to. My arms were burning. Then I felt the bridge tremble again — not from the wind, but from footsteps. A farmer with a bundle of firewood on his back was crossing towards me, moving with the calm of someone who had done this a thousand times. He set down the firewood, lay flat on the planks, and gripped my wrist with a hand as rough as bark. "Now push with your knee," he said quietly. With one great heave, I was back on the bridge.

I crawled the rest of the way on my hands and knees. On the far side, Tara was crying, and so was I. The farmer simply picked up his firewood. "The planks are old," he said. "Walk at the edges, where the ropes hold them."

We never reached the waterfall that day. But every time I cross a bridge now, I walk at the edges — and I think of a stranger's rough, steady hand.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: a real danger, rising tension, a narrow escape at the climax — 8 marks
- Organisation: setting, build-up, climax, resolution, reflection — 4 marks
- Expression: vivid detail, sense impressions, short dramatic sentences — 6 marks
- Accuracy — 2 marks

**Q3.** Write an original short story in which a woman, her strange neighbour and a police officer form the main characters. *(ICSE 2025 specimen)*
**Model answer:** Mrs Lyngdoh had lived in Flat 3B for twenty years, and in all that time she had never had a neighbour as strange as the man in 3C. He moved in on a Monday with no furniture except a telescope and forty potted plants. He never went out during the day. At night, through the thin wall, she heard him talking — sometimes laughing, sometimes arguing — although she never saw anyone visit.

By the end of the month she was sure something was wrong. Parcels arrived for him wrapped in brown paper and marked "FRAGILE — LIVE". Once she heard a crash, then a shout: "Don't let it escape!"

That was when she called the police.

Sub-Inspector Bora arrived the next evening, a tired young officer who clearly expected nothing. "Madam, people are allowed to be odd," he said. But when they knocked on 3C's door and heard the scuffling and squeaking inside, he straightened his cap.

The door opened a crack. A thin man with paint on his spectacles peered out. "Is there a problem?"

"We'd like to look inside, sir," said the officer.

The man sighed and opened the door wide. Mrs Lyngdoh gasped. The flat was a jungle: plants on every surface, and among them glass tanks holding frogs, tortoises and one enormous, sleepy owl blinking from the top of a cupboard.

"I rescue injured animals," the man said. "The parcels are from a wildlife centre in Guwahati. I talk to them at night. It calms them." He paused. "And yesterday the owl did escape. I caught her in the bathroom."

Sub-Inspector Bora wrote something in his notebook, then looked up and smiled for the first time. "My daughter found a hurt pigeon last week. Could I bring it here?"

The next Sunday, Mrs Lyngdoh knocked on 3C with a plate of sweets — and a box containing a sparrow with a broken wing. Her strange neighbour, it turned out, was the kindest person in the building. She had simply never asked.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: all three characters important; mystery and resolution — 8 marks
- Organisation: suspicion, investigation, revelation, change — 4 marks
- Expression: characterisation, dialogue, a light twist — 6 marks
- Accuracy — 2 marks

### Personal accounts (20 marks each)

**Q4.** You were punished for something you had not done. Give an account of the incident and how you proved your innocence. *(ICSE 2027 specimen)*
**Model answer:** It happened in Class IX, the week before our half-yearly examinations, and even now I remember how hot my face felt when my name was called.

Our class teacher had kept the question paper envelopes for the unit test on her desk during lunch. When she returned, one envelope had been torn open. Someone said they had seen me near her desk. It was true — I had gone to return the attendance register, as monitor. Within minutes I was standing outside the Principal's office, accused of trying to see the question paper. I was told I would lose my monitor's badge, and my parents were called to school.

Things went wrong mainly because I panicked. Instead of explaining calmly, I kept repeating, "It wasn't me," which only made me look guilty.

That evening, after my parents had listened to me quietly, my father asked a simple question: "What exactly did you do at the desk?" Thinking it through, I remembered something important. When I put the register down, the envelopes were already on the left side of the desk — and I had noticed that one corner was wet, as though water had been spilled on it.

The next morning I asked our teacher whether I could look at the torn envelope. She agreed. The tear was not clean; the paper was soggy and puckered. I pointed out the water bottle that was always kept on the window-sill above the desk. During lunch the ceiling fan had been switched to full speed, and the bottle had tipped over. The caretaker confirmed that he had wiped up water from the desk and moved the envelopes to the dry side. The "tearing" had happened when the wet paper stuck to the desk and ripped as it was lifted.

The Principal apologised to me in front of the class and returned my badge.

I learnt two lessons that week. The first is that panic makes the truth sound like a lie; calm facts are more powerful than loud denials. The second is that it is unfair to judge anyone too quickly — a lesson I try to remember whenever someone else is accused.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: first-person; a plausible school incident; where things went wrong; clear steps to prove innocence (the scheme's requirements) — 8 marks
- Organisation: incident, mistake, investigation, proof, lesson — 4 marks
- Expression: feelings and reflection — 6 marks
- Accuracy — 2 marks

**Q5.** You recently took part in an interesting class project. Describe how your group planned and carried out the task. Explain how this will help you in the future. *(ICSE 2027 specimen)*
**Model answer:** Last month our geography teacher divided our class into groups of five and gave each group a challenge: to survey how much water our school wasted in one week, and to suggest ways of saving it. My group — Aiman, Daniel, Priyanka, Rishav and I — called ourselves "The Drip Detectives".

We began with a planning meeting in the library. The first half hour was chaos: everyone talked at once, and Rishav wanted to build a model dam. Then Priyanka suggested that we divide the work. Daniel and I would count the taps and note any leaks; Aiman would interview the caretaker and the canteen staff; Rishav would measure how long the overhead tank took to empty; and Priyanka would put everything together in charts.

For five days we went round the school with clipboards and a measuring jug. I still remember the sounds of that week: the hollow drip of a leaking tap in the junior wing, the gurgle of the drinking-water cooler that was never switched off, the hiss of the garden hose left running behind the auditorium. Holding a jug under a dripping tap for one minute, we found it filled almost a quarter of a litre — more than three hundred litres a day from one tap alone.

Aiman's interviews showed that the canteen washed vegetables under running water, and Rishav discovered that the tank overflowed every morning for twenty minutes. Priyanka turned our figures into colourful bar graphs, and we presented our findings at assembly with five simple suggestions, from fixing washers to installing an overflow alarm. Two weeks later, the leaking taps were repaired.

This project will help me in the future in many ways. I learnt that a plan with clear roles turns a noisy group into a team. I learnt to collect real data instead of guessing, and to present it clearly. Above all, I learnt that small actions — a new washer, a closed tap — can make a measurable difference, a lesson I hope to carry into whatever work I do.

**Q6.** Have you ever forgiven someone when it was hard to do so? Explain what helped you to forgive them, and the positive impact it had. What did you learn from the experience? *(ICSE 2024 specimen)*
**Model answer:** In Class VIII, my closest friend, Meera, told a secret I had trusted her with. It was not a dramatic secret — I had confided that I was terrified of performing in the school concert because I had stage fright — but by the next morning half the class knew, and some boys mimicked a trembling singer whenever I walked past. I felt humiliated and betrayed. For three weeks I refused to speak to Meera, even when she left notes in my desk.

Forgiving her was hard because the hurt kept coming back every time someone laughed. What finally helped was a conversation with my grandmother. When I told her the story, she did not take my side at once. Instead she asked, "Has Meera ever done anything kind for you?" I remembered how Meera had sat with me in hospital when I broke my arm, reading me comics for hours. "People are not only their worst mistakes," my grandmother said.

The next day I read Meera's last note properly. She explained that she had mentioned my fear only to one friend, to ask how she could help me, and had not imagined it would spread. She was miserable about it. I realised she had been careless, not cruel.

When I told her I forgave her, the positive impact was immediate. We went together to our music teacher, who arranged extra rehearsals in an empty hall so that I could practise in front of a small, friendly audience. Meera came to every one. On the night of the concert, I sang without a tremor, and the boys who had mocked me clapped loudest.

I learnt that forgiveness is not the same as pretending nothing happened. It means choosing to see the whole person, not just the moment that hurt you. I also learnt that holding on to anger had hurt me more than it hurt Meera. Our friendship, tested and repaired, is stronger today than it ever was.

### Arguments (20 marks each)

**Q7.** 'Too many cooks spoil the broth.' Express your views either for or against this statement. *(ICSE 2027 specimen)*
**Model answer:** The old proverb warns that when too many people try to manage one task, the result is ruined. Some say that this is out of date in an age of teamwork. I firmly agree with the proverb: too many cooks — too many people giving orders — do spoil the broth.

Firstly, many decision-makers produce conflicting instructions. Last year our class staged a play for Founder's Day, and five students appointed themselves "directors". One wanted a comedy, another a tragedy; one changed the costumes every day, another rewrote the script. The actors did not know whom to obey, and the rehearsals collapsed into arguments.

Secondly, too many leaders slow down decisions. In a group project, if every member must approve every detail, simple choices — a title, a colour for a chart — take hours. Deadlines are missed not because people are lazy, but because they are busy disagreeing. Business and government face the same problem: committees that are too large are famous for producing long reports and few results.

Thirdly, when everyone is in charge, no one is responsible. If the broth is spoiled, each cook can blame another. A team with a clear leader knows who must answer for a decision, and this makes everyone more careful.

It is true that many hands make light work, and that ideas from many people can improve a plan. But this supports my point rather than weakening it: many hands are useful only when one head coordinates them. A kitchen may have twenty helpers chopping and stirring, but it has one chef who decides what goes into the pot. Our play was finally saved when our teacher appointed a single director and gave everyone else a clear role.

In conclusion, cooperation and leadership are not opposites. Teamwork succeeds when roles are clear and one person takes final responsibility. Without that, too many cooks will always spoil the broth.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: a clear stand; three valid reasons with examples; the opposing view answered; cohesive and logical (the 2027 scheme) — 8 marks
- Organisation: introduction with stand, three body paragraphs, concession, conclusion — 4 marks
- Expression: formal, persuasive language; linking words — 6 marks
- Accuracy — 2 marks

**Q8.** 'Peer pressure is a force for good.' Express your views either for or against this statement. *(ICSE 2025 specimen)*
**Model answer:** Peer pressure usually has a bad name. We imagine teenagers being pushed into skipping classes or trying dangerous stunts. But I believe that peer pressure, used well, is a powerful force for good.

To begin with, friends can push us to work harder. In our class, a group of us decided to study together for the board examinations. Nobody wanted to be the one who had not finished the chapter, so we all kept up. The pressure came not from teachers or parents but from wanting to keep pace with our friends — and our marks improved together.

Moreover, peer pressure can spread healthy habits. When three of my classmates began cycling to school, others soon joined them, partly to save fuel and partly because they did not want to be left out. Within a term, the cycle stand was full. Health campaigns have long known that people copy those around them; that is why positive role models among young people are so effective.

Peer pressure can also strengthen good values. When a new student was teased in our class, it was the disapproval of the majority, not a teacher's warning, that stopped the bullies. Nobody wants to be seen as cruel by their friends.

Admittedly, peer pressure can lead young people astray, and some give in to bad influences. However, this shows that the force is neutral, not evil. The pressure is only as good as the group we choose. If we surround ourselves with friends who are kind, curious and hard-working, their influence pulls us upward.

To sum up, peer pressure is simply the human desire to belong. Wisely directed — by choosing good friends and by being a good influence ourselves — it becomes one of the strongest forces for good in a young person's life.

**Marking:**
- Content and relevance: a clear stand (for); valid reasons with examples; counter-argument answered — 8 marks
- Organisation — 4 marks
- Expression — 6 marks
- Accuracy — 2 marks

**Q11.** 'Competition hampers the overall development of students.' Express your views either for or against this statement. *(ICSE 2024 specimen)*
**Model answer:** It is fashionable to blame competition for the stress that students feel. I strongly disagree with the statement that competition hampers the overall development of students. Healthy competition, in my view, is one of the most important ways in which young people grow.

Firstly, competition brings out our best effort. When our school announced an inter-house science exhibition, students who had never opened a book outside the syllabus spent weeks researching solar cookers and water filters. They did not do it for marks; they did it because they wanted their house to shine. That extra effort taught them more than any textbook chapter.

Secondly, competition teaches us to handle both success and failure. Not every runner wins the race, and not every debater lifts the trophy. Learning to lose with dignity, to congratulate the winner and to try again is a life skill. The real world is competitive — in examinations, interviews and careers — and students who have never competed are often the least prepared for it.

Thirdly, competition helps students discover their talents. A shy classmate of mine entered an elocution contest only because her friends dared her. She came second, discovered that she loved public speaking, and is now the school's best anchor. Without the contest she might never have found her voice.

It is true that excessive competition, especially over marks, can cause anxiety and unhealthy rivalry. However, the answer is not to remove competition but to keep it balanced: to value effort over results, to offer a wide range of activities so that every student can excel in something, and to teach cooperation alongside it.

In conclusion, competition does not hamper development; poorly managed competition does. Fair, varied and well-guided competition helps students grow in confidence, resilience and skill — which is exactly what overall development means.

- Content and relevance: a clear stand (against); three valid reasons with examples; the opposing view answered — 8 marks
- Organisation — 4 marks
- Expression — 6 marks

## 15. Revision Notes

### The five types at a glance

| Type | Key rule |
|---|---|
| story | follow the given words, title or characters exactly; one event; climax and resolution |
| personal account | first person, past tense; plausible; cover every part of the question |
| argument | one clear stand; reason + explanation + example per paragraph; answer the other side |
| description | senses; precise words; people; your feelings |
| picture | clear connection; never just a list of what is in the picture |

### Time plan (30 minutes)

1 minute choosing · 4 minutes planning · 22 minutes writing · 3 minutes checking.

### Paragraph plan (about 330 words)

Opening (50–60) · Body 1 (70–80) · Body 2 (70–80) · Body 3 (60–70) · Ending (40–50).

### Checklist

- Given words copied exactly?
- Every part of the topic answered?
- Clear paragraphs, clear ending?
- Tenses consistent; dialogue punctuated?
- Around 300–350 words?

### Common mistakes

Changing the given opening · leaving out a required part · arguing both sides · listing a picture · "it was all a dream" · too many characters · no paragraphs · tense shifts · over-length.
