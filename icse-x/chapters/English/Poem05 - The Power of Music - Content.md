## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*The Power of Music* is a **comic poem** by the great Bengali writer of nonsense verse, **Sukumar Ray**. Its hero — or rather its comic villain — is **Bhishma Lochan Sharma**, a man who loves to sing but whose voice is so **enormously loud and harsh** that it causes chaos wherever it is heard.

When summer comes, Bhishma Lochan begins to sing, and his voice carries over huge distances — from **Delhi** all the way to **Burma**. People are dazed; some faint or fall ill; they beg him to stop. Carts overturn, horses stand stunned, animals collapse with their feet in the air, birds flip upside down in mid-flight, fish dive to the bottom of the lake in search of silence, trees crash, grand houses tumble — even the sky seems to weep. Nothing and nobody can stop him.

Then, at last, a **billy goat** arrives — a wise fellow. It lowers its horns, **charges** straight at the singer and answers his bellowing with its own. The song is scattered, Bhishma Lochan falls silent, and the world receives the precious **gift of silence**.

The title is **ironic**: the "power" of this music is not to delight or soothe, but to **destroy**. The poem is pure fun — a riot of **exaggeration**, **sound** and **absurd images** — but it also gently mocks people who inflict their talents on others without caring how others feel.

### About the poet

**Sukumar Ray** (1887–1923) was a Bengali poet, story-writer and illustrator, and one of the finest writers of **nonsense literature** in any language. He was the son of **Upendrakishore Ray Chowdhury**, who founded the children's magazine ***Sandesh***, and the father of the film-maker **Satyajit Ray**. Sukumar studied photography and printing technology in England, then returned to edit *Sandesh*. He died young, at 36. His most famous book, ***Abol Tabol*** (1923), is a collection of comic, absurd verses with his own drawings; *Ganer Guntho* (roughly "the shove of song" — a *guntho* is a push or butt, as from a goat's horns), translated as *The Power of Music*, comes from it. The English version is by the scholar **Sukanta Chaudhuri**, from his translations of Ray's nonsense verse.

### The poem's ideas, part by part

#### Part 1 — The singer and his voice

In the **summer**, **Bhishma Lochan Sharma** starts to sing. His voice is so powerful that it can be heard across vast distances — from **Delhi** to **Burma**. He is completely absorbed in his singing and pays no attention to anyone else.

#### Part 2 — The people suffer

The people who hear him are **dazed**. In the panic, some are **trampled**; others turn **pale and sick**. They **beg** him to stop, saying they cannot bear it — they feel they are near their end. But Bhishma Lochan goes on singing. *(2025 specimen Q1(viii): the people retire amazed.)*

#### Part 3 — Nature in chaos

The poet describes the effects of the singing in a series of wildly **exaggerated** pictures:

| Who or what | What happens |
|---|---|
| bullock-carts | overturn |
| horses | stand along the roadside, stunned |
| animals | collapse, **whining and staring** with their feet in the air |
| birds | flip over in mid-flight — "**the feathered fly turn turtle while they're winging**" |
| fish | dive to the bottom of the lake, searching for silence |
| trees | **shake and crash** down, with a noise heard far away |
| grand mansions | tumble down |
| the sky | **weeps** |

*(2025 specimen Q1(viii): the sky weeps tears, the trees shake and crash, and the people retire amazed — but it is the animals, not "turtles", that whine and stare. 2027 specimen Q1(xi) quotes the line about the birds.)*

#### Part 4 — The billy goat

Just when it seems nothing can stop the singer, a **billy goat** appears — described as a **sagacious** (wise) fellow. It **lowers its horns** and **charges straight** at Bhishma Lochan, meeting his bellowing with a bellow of its own. With this sudden, rough blow, the strains of the song are **scattered** and **tossed aside**.

#### Part 5 — Silence

Bhishma Lochan's song comes to an end, and the world receives the **gift of silence** — which, after such "music", seems as precious as gold. The goat, not any human being, has rescued everyone.

### Conclusion: what the poem shows

- **Humour through exaggeration:** a man's singing is so terrible it wrecks the whole world.
- **Irony:** music is supposed to delight; this "power of music" is destructive.
- **Sound and rhythm:** the poem itself is noisy and lively, full of rhyme, alliteration and onomatopoeia.
- **A gentle lesson:** people should consider how their actions affect others; enthusiasm without consideration becomes a nuisance.
- **Comic justice:** the sagacious goat silences the singer, and peace returns.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The singer

- **Points to note:**
  - **Bhishma Lochan Sharma** — a grand-sounding name for a comic character; the name "Bhishma" (a mighty hero of the *Mahabharata*) adds mock-heroic humour.
  - He sings in **summer**; his voice carries from **Delhi** to **Burma** — **hyperbole**.
  - He ignores everyone's suffering — comic self-absorption.

### Section 2 — The people

- **Points to note:**
  - People are **dazed**, **trampled** in panic, **pale and sick**.
  - They **beg** him to stop, feeling close to death — comic exaggeration of distress.
  - *(2025 specimen Q1(viii) option: "The people retire amazed" — an effect of the singing.)*

### Section 3 — The chaos in nature

- **Points to note:**
  - **Carts overturn; horses stand stunned** — the roads are in chaos.
  - **Animals whine and stare** with their feet in the air — absurd, cartoon-like image.
  - **Birds** "**turn turtle while they're winging**" — flip upside down mid-flight; *turn turtle* = overturn. The line has **alliteration** ("feathered fly"; "turn turtle"; "while … winging"). *(2027 specimen Q1(xi))*
  - **Fish** dive to the bottom seeking silence — even underwater is not safe.
  - **Trees shake and crash**; **mansions** tumble; the **sky weeps** — personification and hyperbole. *(2025 specimen Q1(viii))*
  - **Trap:** "**The turtles whine and stare**" is NOT an effect — it mixes up "animals whine and stare" with the birds that "turn turtle". *(2025 specimen Q1(viii) answer (d))*

### Section 4 — The goat and the silence

- **Points to note:**
  - A **billy goat**, called **sagacious** (wise) — comic irony: the wisest creature in the poem is a goat.
  - It **lowers its horns** and **charges**; bellow meets bellow.
  - The song is scattered; Bhishma Lochan stops; the world gets the **gift of silence**.
  - **Comic justice:** a crude force ends a crude noise.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Humour and absurdity:** the joy of nonsense, exaggeration and comic images.
- **Irony of "power":** music meant to please becomes a destructive force.
- **Consideration for others:** enthusiasm that ignores others' comfort becomes a nuisance.
- **Comic justice:** the inconsiderate singer meets a stronger, cruder force.
- **The value of silence:** after noise, quiet seems precious.

### 7.1 Tone and mood

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| tone | playful, mocking, mock-heroic |
| mood | hilarious, chaotic, then relieved |
| humour sources | exaggeration, absurd images, sound effects, the wise goat |

### 7.2 Significance of the title

"The Power of Music" sounds like praise — music that moves hearts and calms beasts. The poem turns this upside down: Bhishma Lochan's "music" has **power to destroy** — to overturn carts, fell trees and make the sky weep. The title is therefore **ironic**, and the joke is completed when the most "powerful" force of all turns out to be a goat.

### 7.3 Comparing poems in the syllabus

| Point | *The Power of Music* | *The Glove and the Lions* |
|---|---|---|
| tone | comic, nonsense | dramatic, moral |
| animals | animals suffer from the singing; a goat rescues all | lions fight for royal entertainment |
| human fault | inconsiderate self-absorption | vanity |
| ending | silence restored by a goat | vanity rebuked by de Lorge and the King |
| device in common | alliteration (both specimens test it) | |

**(i) What is he known for? When can one hear him? Explain 'You catch his strain'.** [3]
He is known for his **loud, harsh singing**, which carries enormous distances. One hears him **in summer**. "You catch his strain" means **you hear his song (tune)** — wherever you are, from Delhi to Burma.

**(ii) How can we tell that his singing was not appreciated?** [3]
People are **dazed**; some are **trampled** in the rush, some turn **pale and sick**; they **cry that they are going to die** and **beg him to stop singing**, pleading and grumbling — but he goes on.

**(iii) Three ridiculous, exaggerated images of the voice's effect.** [3]
Any three: **bullock-carts overturn**; **horses stand stunned** by the roadside; **animals collapse with their feet in the air**, whining and staring; **birds turn turtle** in mid-flight; **fish dive** to the bottom of the lake for silence; **trees crash** down; **mighty mansions tumble**; **the sky weeps**.

**(iv) How is the billy goat described? What does it do?** [3]
It is a **sagacious (wise) billy goat**. It **lowers its horns and charges straight at Bhishma Lochan**, answering his bellow with its own; the blow **scatters his song**, he stops, and the world gets the **gift of silence**.

**(v) Significance of the title; whom do you sympathise with, and why?** [4]
The title is **ironic**: music is meant to **delight and soothe**, but this "power" is the power to **destroy** — to frighten people, upset nature and knock down houses; the real relief comes only with silence. *Personal response:* **the listeners**, because they suffer terribly and are given no choice; *or* **Bhishma Lochan**, a man who simply loves to sing, is butted by a goat and silenced. ### 8.2 Practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A (paraphrased).** Each summer Bhishma Lochan Sharma lifts his voice in song, and it is heard from Delhi to Burma. People stagger about in a daze; some fall ill; they beg him to stop.

**(i)** Who is Bhishma Lochan Sharma? What is special about his voice? [3]
**Answer:** A man who loves to sing; his voice is **enormously loud and harsh**, carrying over vast distances — from **Delhi to Burma**.

**(ii)** What figure of speech is used in "heard from Delhi to Burma"? What is its effect? [3]
**Answer:** **Hyperbole** (exaggeration); it makes the singer's loudness comically impossible and sets the playful tone of the poem.

**(iii)** How do the people react? [3]
**Answer:** They are **dazed and amazed**; some are **trampled** in the panic; others turn **pale and sick**; they **beg** him to stop, feeling close to their end.

**(iv)** Does Bhishma Lochan stop? What does this show about him? [3]
**Answer:** **No** — he goes on singing; he is **self-absorbed and inconsiderate**, caring only for his own performance.

**(v)** What is ironic about the title of the poem? [4]
**Answer:** "The power of music" usually means music's power to **delight and soothe**; here the singing's "power" is to **destroy** — people fall ill, carts overturn, trees crash and the sky weeps. The title praises what the poem mocks, and the final irony is that the only power strong enough to stop him is a **goat**.

**Extract B (paraphrased).** The birds flying overhead flip upside down in mid-air; fish plunge to the bottom of the lake in search of silence; trees shake and crash, and even the sky weeps.

**(i)** Which line in the poem describes the birds? Name its device. [3]
**Answer:** "the **feathered fly turn turtle while they're winging**" — **alliteration** (f, t, w sounds); "turn turtle" means flip over.

**(ii)** Why do the fish dive to the bottom? [3]
**Answer:** To escape the noise — they search for **silence** in the depths; even underwater the singing can be felt.

**(iii)** Give two other effects of the singing on the natural or man-made world. [3]
**Answer:** (Any two) **bullock-carts overturn**; **horses stand stunned**; **animals whine and stare** with their feet in the air; **grand mansions tumble down**.

**(iv)** Name two devices in "the sky weeps". [3]
**Answer:** **Personification** (the sky crying) and **hyperbole** (exaggerating the effect of the singing).

**(v)** How does the poet create humour in this part of the poem? [4]
**Answer:** Through a rapid list of **absurd, exaggerated images** — birds upside down, fish hunting for silence, animals with legs in the air, the sky in tears — and through lively **sound effects**, **rhyme** and **alliteration**; the comic contrast between a man "singing" and a world collapsing makes us laugh.

**Extract C (paraphrased).** At last a billy goat — a wise fellow — lowers its horns, charges straight at the singer and answers bellow with bellow. The song is scattered, and the world is given the gift of silence.

**(i)** How is the goat described? [3]
**Answer:** As a **sagacious** (wise) fellow — comically, the wisest creature in the poem.

**(ii)** What does the goat do? [3]
**Answer:** It **lowers its horns**, **charges** straight at Bhishma Lochan, and answers his **bellowing** with its own.

**(iii)** What happens to the song? [3]
**Answer:** It is **scattered and tossed aside** by the goat's rough blow; Bhishma Lochan **stops singing**.

**(iv)** What is the "gift" the world receives? Why is it precious? [3]
**Answer:** **Silence**; after such terrible noise, quiet seems as precious as **gold**.

**(v)** Why is the ending satisfying? [4]
**Answer:** It brings **comic justice**: the inconsiderate singer, who ignored everyone's pleas, is silenced by a stronger, cruder force; the irony that a **goat** is the wise hero completes the joke; and the return of **silence** gives relief to all the suffering creatures.

## 9. MCQs with Explanations

### The poet and the poem (Q1–Q14)

**Q1.** *The Power of Music* was written by
- (a) Leigh Hunt
- (b) Rabindranath Tagore
- (c) Sukumar Ray
- (d) Robert Frost

**Answer:** (c) Sukumar Ray
**Explanation:** A Bengali writer of nonsense verse.

**Q2.** The poem was originally written in
- (a) English
- (b) Hindi
- (c) Bengali
- (d) Sanskrit

**Answer:** (c) Bengali
**Explanation:** *Ganer Guntho*.

- (a) Satyajit Ray
- (b) Sukanta Chaudhuri

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** Who is Bhishma Lochan Sharma? What is special about his voice?
**Answer:** A man who loves to sing, especially in summer.
His voice is enormously loud and harsh.
It is heard from Delhi to Burma.

**Q2.** How do the people react to his singing?
**Answer:** They are dazed and amazed.
Some are trampled; others turn pale and sick.
They beg him to stop.

**Q3.** Name three effects of the singing on animals and birds.
**Answer:** Horses stand stunned by the roadside.
Animals whine and stare with their feet in the air.
Birds turn turtle in mid-flight.

**Q4.** What happens to the fish, the trees and the sky?
**Answer:** The fish dive to the bottom seeking silence.
The trees shake and crash.
The sky weeps.

**Q5.** Which is NOT an effect of the singing, and why? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** "The turtles whine and stare."
It is the animals that whine and stare.
The birds "turn turtle" — flip over — which is a different image.

**Q6.** Explain "the feathered fly turn turtle while they're winging".
**Answer:** The birds that fly overhead flip upside down in mid-air.
"Turn turtle" means overturn.
The line uses alliteration (f, t, w sounds).

**Q7.** Name the figure of speech in that line and match it with an example. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Alliteration.
The matching example is "You're one month on in the middle of May".
Both repeat initial consonant sounds.

**Q8.** How is the goat described, and what does it do?
**Answer:** As sagacious — wise.
It lowers its horns and charges at the singer.
Its bellow answers his bellow.

**Q9.** How does the poem end?
**Answer:** The goat's charge scatters the song.
Bhishma Lochan stops singing.
The world receives the gift of silence.

**Q10.** Why is the title ironic?
**Answer:** "The power of music" suggests delight.
Here the singing's power is destructive.
The only force stronger than it is a goat.

**Q11.** Give two examples of hyperbole in the poem.
**Answer:** The voice is heard from Delhi to Burma.
Mansions tumble down and the sky weeps.
(Also: trees crashing; birds flipping over.)

**Q12.** What does Bhishma Lochan's behaviour show about him?
**Answer:** He is self-absorbed.
He ignores everyone's pleas.
He thinks only of his own performance.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** How does the poet create humour in the poem?
**Answer:** Exaggeration: the voice reaches Burma; mansions fall.
Absurd images: birds upside down; animals with feet in the air.
Sound play: alliteration, rhyme, onomatopoeia.
Comic irony: a "sagacious" goat is the hero.

**Q14.** Describe the role of the billy goat.
**Answer:** It appears when nothing else can stop the singer.
It is called sagacious — comically wise.
It charges with lowered horns, meeting bellow with bellow.
It scatters the song and brings silence — comic justice.

**Q15.** What gentle lesson does the poem teach?
**Answer:** Enthusiasm should not ignore others' comfort.
Bhishma Lochan never considers his listeners.
His self-absorption causes chaos.
In the end he is silenced — a reminder to be considerate.

**Q16.** Explain the mock-heroic style of the poem.
**Answer:** The singer has a grand, heroic name — Bhishma.
His singing is described like a great natural disaster.
The rescuer is a goat praised as "sagacious".
The grand style for a trivial subject creates comedy.

**Q17.** Write a character sketch of Bhishma Lochan Sharma.
**Answer:** An enthusiastic, tireless singer.
Enormously loud — heard from Delhi to Burma.
Self-absorbed and inconsiderate — ignores pleas.
A comic figure finally silenced by a goat.

**Q18.** How does the poem use sound to match its subject?
**Answer:** Lively rhyme and rhythm make it noisy.
Onomatopoeic words — crash, whine, bellow.
Alliteration — "feathered fly turn turtle".
The poem "sounds" like the uproar it describes, until the silence at the end.

**Q19.** Compare *The Power of Music* with *The Glove and the Lions*.
**Answer:** Both use alliteration and animal imagery.
*The Power of Music* is comic nonsense; *The Glove* is dramatic and moral.
One mocks inconsiderate self-absorption; the other rebukes vanity.
Both end with a kind of justice — silence, and the lady's shame.

**Q20.** Why is silence called a "gift" at the end?
**Answer:** After unbearable noise, quiet is precious.
It brings relief to people, animals and nature.
It is given by an unexpected rescuer, the goat.
It completes the comic reversal of the poem.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Poem at a glance
- **Poet:** Sukumar Ray (1887–1923); Bengali *Ganer Guntho*, from *Abol Tabol* (1923); translated by **Sukanta Chaudhuri**.
- **Singer:** **Bhishma Lochan Sharma**; sings in **summer**; voice heard from **Delhi to Burma**.
- **People:** dazed, trampled, pale and sick; **beg him to stop**; he sings on.
- **Chaos:** carts overturn; horses stunned; **animals whine and stare** with feet in the air; birds "**turn turtle while they're winging**"; fish dive **seeking silence**; **trees shake and crash**; mansions tumble; **the sky weeps**.
- **Goat:** a **sagacious** billy goat lowers its horns and **charges**; bellow meets bellow; the song is scattered.
- **Ending:** the **gift of silence**.
- **Devices:** hyperbole, alliteration, onomatopoeia, personification, irony, mock-heroic style; lively rhyme.
- **Specimens:** 2025 — NOT an effect: **the turtles whine and stare**; 2027 — the birds line uses **alliteration**, like "You're one month on in the middle of May".

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The turtles whine and stare | The animals whine and stare; birds turn turtle. |
| The birds line uses a simile | It uses alliteration. |
| The people stop the singer | A goat stops him. |
| The goat is called foolish | It is called sagacious (wise). |
| The poem is free verse | It is lively rhymed verse. |
| The title praises music | It is ironic — the music is destructive. |
