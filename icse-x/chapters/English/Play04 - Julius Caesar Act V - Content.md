## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

Act V takes place on the **plains of Philippi**, in Macedonia (northern Greece). The armies of **Octavius and Antony** face the armies of **Brutus and Cassius**. In one day of battle the conspiracy ends: **Cassius** kills himself through a tragic mistake, and **Brutus**, defeated, runs upon his own sword. The play ends with the victors praising Brutus as the noblest of the conspirators.

The act completes the pattern of the play. Caesar's body died in Act III, but his **spirit** has been "ranging for revenge" ever since, and now the conspirators die by their own swords — the same swords that killed Caesar. As Brutus says, "**O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!**"

### Where we are: a recap of Act IV

The triumvirs (Antony, Octavius and Lepidus) condemned their enemies in Rome. Near Sardis, Brutus and Cassius quarrelled over Lucius Pella, the "itching palm" and the gold Brutus needed for his legions, then made up. Brutus revealed that **Portia** had died by swallowing fire. News came that Octavius and Antony were marching to **Philippi**. Against Cassius's advice, Brutus decided to march to meet them: "There is a tide in the affairs of men." That night the **Ghost of Caesar** told Brutus, "thou shalt see me at Philippi."

### Scene 1, retold: before the battle

#### 1. Antony and Octavius on the plains

**Octavius** notes that the enemy has come down from the hills to fight on the open plain — Antony had expected them to stay on high ground. Antony claims he understands their minds ("I am in their bosoms"): they are putting on a "fearful bravery" to make it seem that they have courage.

Antony orders Octavius to lead his army on the **left** side of the field. Octavius refuses: "**Upon the right hand I; keep thou the left.**" When Antony asks, "Why do you cross me in this exigent?" Octavius answers coolly, "**I do not cross you; but I will do so.**" Young Octavius is asserting himself; he will not take orders from Antony. This foreshadows the later rivalry between the two (outside the play, Octavius defeats Antony and becomes Emperor Augustus).

#### 2. The parley: words before blows

The four generals meet before the battle for a **parley** (a formal talk), which turns into an exchange of insults.

- Brutus: "Words before blows: is it so, countrymen?" — Octavius: "Not that we love words better, as you do."
- Brutus: "**Good words are better than bad strokes**, Octavius."
- Antony: "**In your bad strokes, Brutus, you give good words**: / Witness the hole you made in Caesar's heart, / Crying 'Long live! hail, Caesar!'" — Brutus stabbed Caesar while pretending to love him.
- Cassius mocks Antony's sweet speeches: "they **rob the Hybla bees, / And leave them honeyless**" (Hybla in Sicily was famous for honey). Antony: "Not stingless too." Brutus: Antony has stolen the bees' buzzing too, and "very wisely" threatens before he stings.
- Antony bursts out: the conspirators "show'd your teeth like **apes**, and fawn'd like **hounds**, / And bow'd like bondmen, kissing Caesar's feet; / Whilst damned **Casca, like a cur**, behind / Struck Caesar on the neck. **O you flatterers!**"
- Cassius turns on Brutus: "Now, Brutus, thank yourself: / This tongue had not offended so to-day, / If Cassius might have ruled" — a reminder that Cassius had wanted Antony killed with Caesar.
- Octavius draws his sword and vows never to sheathe it "till **Caesar's three and thirty wounds** / Be well avenged; or till another Caesar / Have added slaughter to the sword of traitors" (until he himself is killed by them).
- Brutus: "Caesar, thou canst not die by traitors' hands, / Unless thou bring'st them with thee" — the only traitors are in Octavius's own camp.
- Cassius calls Octavius "**a peevish schoolboy**" joined with "**a masker and a reveller**" (Antony, the party-lover). Antony: "**Old Cassius still!**"
- Octavius: "**Defiance, traitors, hurl we in your teeth**: / If you dare fight to-day, come to the field; / If not, when you have stomachs." They leave.

Cassius: "Why, now, blow wind, swell billow and swim bark! / The storm is up, and all is on the hazard." Everything is now at risk.

#### 3. Cassius's birthday and the omen of the eagles

Cassius speaks privately to **Messala**:

> "This is my birth-day; as this very day
> Was Cassius born."

He says that, like **Pompey**, he is forced against his will to risk all their liberties on a single battle. He has always followed the philosophy of **Epicurus**, who taught that the gods do not interfere in human life and that omens mean nothing — "now I change my mind, / And **partly credit things that do presage**" (partly believe in omens).

On the march from Sardis, **two mighty eagles** had swooped down on the foremost banner ("ensign"), perched there and fed from the soldiers' hands, and kept them company all the way to Philippi. **This morning they have flown away**. In their place **ravens, crows and kites** — birds that feed on dead bodies — circle overhead and look down "as we were sickly prey"; their shadows seem "a canopy most fatal" under which the army lies, "ready to give up the ghost." *(2027 specimen Q3(v).)*

Messala says, "Believe not so." Cassius answers that he believes it only partly; he is "fresh of spirit and resolved / To meet all perils very constantly."

#### 4. Brutus's philosophy

Cassius asks Brutus what he will do if they lose. Brutus answers by his **Stoic philosophy**: he once **blamed Cato** (Portia's father) for killing himself; he finds it "cowardly and vile" to cut short one's life for fear of what might happen; he would rather arm himself with **patience** and wait for the will of the gods ("the providence of some high powers / That govern us below").

#### 5. The farewell

Cassius presses him:

> **Cassius:** Then, if we lose this battle,
> You are contented to be led in triumph
> Thorough the streets of Rome?
> **Brutus:** **No, Cassius, no**: think not, thou noble Roman,
> That ever Brutus will go bound to Rome;
> He bears too great a mind. But this same day
> **Must end that work the ides of March begun;**
> And whether we shall meet again I know not.
> Therefore our everlasting farewell take:
> **For ever, and for ever, farewell, Cassius!**
> If we do meet again, why, we shall smile;
> If not, why then, this parting was well made.

Brutus will **never** be led as a prisoner through Rome in the victors' **triumph** (victory procession). The battle will finish what the Ides of March began. Not knowing whether they will meet again, he takes an **everlasting farewell**. Cassius answers in the same words: "For ever, and for ever, farewell, Brutus! / If we do meet again, we'll smile indeed; / If not, 'tis true this parting was well made." Brutus wishes a man could know "**The end of this day's business ere it come!** / But it sufficeth that the day will end, / And then the end is known." *(2027 specimen Q3(i), (ii).)*

Note the contradiction: a moment ago Brutus said suicide was "cowardly and vile"; now he says he will never be taken to Rome alive. His pride is stronger than his philosophy.

### Scene 2, retold: Brutus attacks

Brutus sends **Messala** with written orders ("bills") to the legions on the other wing: "Let them set on at once; for I perceive / But **cold demeanour in Octavius' wing**, / And sudden push gives them the overthrow." He sees weakness in Octavius's side and attacks at once.

### Scene 3, retold: the death of Cassius

#### 6. Cassius's army in trouble

On another part of the field, Cassius is losing. His own **standard-bearer** ("ensign") was running away; Cassius killed him and seized the banner himself. **Titinius** explains the problem: "**Brutus gave the word too early**; / Who, having some advantage on Octavius, / Took it too eagerly: his soldiers **fell to spoil**" (began plundering), "Whilst we by Antony are all enclosed."

**Pindarus** runs in: Antony is in Cassius's tents; Cassius must flee further. Cassius sees his tents on fire. He sends Titinius on his own horse to find out whether some distant troops are **friends or enemies**, and sends Pindarus up a hill to watch, since "my sight was ever thick" (poor).

#### 7. "My life is run his compass"

While Pindarus climbs, Cassius says:

> "This day I breathed first: time is come round,
> And where I did begin, there shall I end;
> **My life is run his compass.**"

It is his **birthday**; his life has come **full circle**, like a compass drawing a circle back to its starting point — he will end where he began. *(2025 specimen Q1(vi).)*

#### 8. The fatal mistake

Pindarus reports, wrongly, that Titinius is **surrounded by horsemen**, that they are lighting down, that "he's ta'en" (captured), and that they "shout for joy". In fact the horsemen are **Brutus's** men, welcoming Titinius with joy because Brutus has defeated Octavius. Cassius, with his poor sight and darker mood, believes the worst: "**O, coward that I am, to live so long, / To see my best friend ta'en before my face!**"

He calls down Pindarus, his **bondman**, whom he had taken prisoner in **Parthia** and whose life he had spared on the oath that Pindarus would do whatever Cassius asked. Now he gives Pindarus his freedom in return for one service: "with this good sword, / **That ran through Caesar's bowels, search this bosom.**" Pindarus stabs him. Cassius dies saying:

> "**Caesar, thou art revenged,
> Even with the sword that kill'd thee.**"

Pindarus, now free, runs away "Where never Roman shall take note of him."

#### 9. Titinius returns too late

**Titinius** returns with **Messala**, bringing good news: Octavius is overthrown by Brutus, just as Cassius's legions are by Antony — the battle is even. They find Cassius dead. Messala laments: "**Mistrust of good success hath done this deed. / O hateful error, melancholy's child**" — Cassius's gloomy nature made him believe something false.

Messala goes to tell Brutus. Titinius places on Cassius's head the **wreath of victory** that Brutus's men gave him: "Why didst thou send me forth, brave Cassius? / Did I not meet thy friends? and did not they / Put on my brows this wreath of victory, / And bid me give it thee?" Then, out of loyalty, he kills himself with Cassius's sword: "**Come, Cassius' sword, and find Titinius' heart.**"

#### 10. "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!"

Brutus arrives with Messala, **Young Cato**, Strato, Volumnius and Lucilius. Seeing the bodies he cries:

> "**O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!**
> Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords
> In our own proper entrails."

Caesar's spirit is turning the conspirators' swords against their own bodies. Brutus praises Cassius as "**The last of all the Romans**": Rome will never breed his equal. "Friends, I owe more tears / To this dead man than you shall see me pay. / **I shall find time, Cassius, I shall find time.**" He sends the body to **Thasos** for burial, so that the funeral will not discourage the army, and orders a **second fight** before night: "'Tis three o'clock."

### Scene 4, retold: Young Cato and Lucilius

**Young Cato** (Portia's brother, son of Marcus Cato) fights bravely, crying that he is "A foe to tyrants, and my country's friend", and is killed. **Lucilius**, to protect Brutus, pretends to be him: "**And I am Brutus, Marcus Brutus, I; / Brutus, my country's friend; know me for Brutus!**" He is captured by Antony's soldiers.

Antony recognises him: "This is not Brutus, friend; but, I assure you, / A prize no less in worth: keep this man safe; / Give him all kindness: **I had rather have / Such men my friends than enemies.**" Lucilius declares that Brutus will never be taken alive: "When you do find him, or alive or dead, / He will be found like Brutus, like himself." Antony shows **generosity and respect for loyalty**.

### Scene 5, retold: the death of Brutus

#### 11. "Come, poor remains of friends"

Brutus, defeated, rests on a rock with the last of his followers: "**Come, poor remains of friends, rest on this rock.**" He whispers to **Clitus**, then to **Dardanius**, asking each to kill him; both refuse in horror. He tells **Volumnius** that "**The ghost of Caesar hath appear'd to me / Two several times by night; at Sardis once, / And, this last night, here in Philippi fields: / I know my hour is come.**" Volumnius refuses too: "That's not an office for a friend, my lord."

Brutus says the enemy has driven them to the edge of the pit; "It is more worthy to leap in ourselves, / Than tarry till they push us." The alarm sounds; the enemy is near.

#### 12. Brutus's farewell and death

> "Countrymen,
> **My heart doth joy that yet in all my life
> I found no man but he was true to me.**
> I shall have glory by this losing day
> More than Octavius and Mark Antony
> By this vile conquest shall attain unto."

Brutus is comforted that **no man was ever false to him** (ironically, he himself was false to Caesar). He believes he will win more glory by this defeat than the victors by their "vile conquest".

He asks **Strato**, "a fellow of a good respect", to hold his sword and turn away his face. Brutus **runs upon the sword** and dies with these words:

> "**Caesar, now be still:
> I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.**"

He tells Caesar's spirit to rest; he kills himself far more willingly than he killed Caesar — a final admission of how much he loved Caesar.

#### 13. "This was the noblest Roman of them all"

**Octavius, Antony**, Messala and the captured Lucilius arrive. Strato reports that Brutus died by his own hand: "**Brutus only overcame himself, / And no man else hath honour by his death.**" Lucilius: "So Brutus should be found." Octavius takes Brutus's followers into his own service, and Strato agrees to serve him.

Antony then speaks the play's final tribute:

> "**This was the noblest Roman of them all:**
> All the conspirators save only he
> Did that they did in envy of great Caesar;
> He only, in a general honest thought
> And common good to all, made one of them.
> His life was gentle, and the elements
> So mix'd in him that Nature might stand up
> And say to all the world '**This was a man!**'"

All the other conspirators acted out of **envy** of Caesar; **only Brutus** acted from an honest belief in the **common good**. His life was gentle, and the "elements" (the qualities that make up a person) were so well **balanced** in him that Nature herself could proclaim, "This was a man!" *(2027 specimen Q3(iv).)*

Octavius orders that Brutus be buried with all honour, his body lying that night in Octavius's own tent, "Most like a soldier", and calls the field to rest, "To part the glories of this happy day."

### Conclusion: why this act matters

- It completes the **revenge of Caesar's spirit**: Cassius and Brutus die by the swords that killed Caesar, and each acknowledges Caesar as he dies.
- It brings the **tragedy of Brutus** to its end: a noble man who made a terrible mistake, honoured even by his enemies.
- It shows **Cassius's tragic error**, born of a gloomy nature and poor sight — "melancholy's child".
- It shows **loyalty** at its finest: Titinius, Lucilius, Strato, Young Cato.
- It shows the new order: **Octavius**, cool and commanding, takes control.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Antony and Octavius (V.1)

- **What happens:** the enemy comes down to the plain; Octavius refuses to take the left wing.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**I am in their bosoms**" — Antony claims to know their secret thoughts.
  - "**Upon the right hand I; keep thou the left.**" — Octavius insists on the place of honour.
  - "**I do not cross you; but I will do so.**" — a calm assertion of independence; foreshadows Octavius's rise.

### Section 2 — The parley (V.1)

- **What happens:** the generals trade insults before the battle.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Good words are better than bad strokes**" (Brutus) — talk is better than violence.
  - "**In your bad strokes, Brutus, you give good words**" (Antony) — Brutus spoke love while he stabbed.
  - "**they rob the Hybla bees, / And leave them honeyless**" (Cassius) — Antony's words are honey-sweet but empty.
  - "**You show'd your teeth like apes, and fawn'd like hounds**" (Antony) — the conspirators were flatterers before they were killers.
  - "**Caesar's three and thirty wounds**" (Octavius) — he will not rest until these are avenged.
  - "**A peevish schoolboy … Join'd with a masker and a reveller!**" (Cassius) — scorn for young Octavius and pleasure-loving Antony.
  - "**Old Cassius still!**" (Antony) — the same sour, sharp-tongued Cassius.

### Section 3 — The eagles and the farewell (V.1)

- **What happens:** Cassius tells Messala of his birthday and the omen; Brutus and Cassius bid farewell.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**You know that I held Epicurus strong … now I change my mind, / And partly credit things that do presage**" — the Epicurean now half-believes in omens. *(2027 specimen Q3(v))*
  - "**ravens, crows and kites … As we were sickly prey**" — scavenger birds waiting for the dead.
  - "**By which I did blame Cato for the death / Which he did give himself**" — Brutus once condemned suicide as cowardly.
  - "**No, Cassius, no … That ever Brutus will go bound to Rome**" — he will never be led as a captive. *(2027 specimen Q3(i))*
  - "**this same day / Must end that work the ides of March begun**" — the battle will settle the outcome of the assassination.
  - "**Therefore our everlasting farewell take: / For ever, and for ever, farewell, Cassius!**" — hints at defeat, death, and never meeting again. *(2027 specimen Q3(ii))*

### Section 4 — The battle begins (V.2)

- **What happens:** Brutus orders an immediate attack on Octavius's wing.
- **Key lines:** "**cold demeanour in Octavius' wing**" — Brutus sees a lack of spirit there; "**sudden push gives them the overthrow**".

### Section 5 — The death of Cassius (V.3)

- **What happens:** Cassius, believing Titinius captured, has Pindarus kill him.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Brutus gave the word too early**" (Titinius) — Brutus's soldiers began plundering while Antony surrounded Cassius.
  - "**My life is run his compass**" — his life has come full circle on his birthday. *(2025 specimen Q1(vi))*
  - "**O, coward that I am, to live so long, / To see my best friend ta'en before my face!**" — self-blame and despair.
  - "**That ran through Caesar's bowels, search this bosom**" — the sword that killed Caesar now kills Cassius.
  - "**Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee.**" — Cassius's last words.

### Section 6 — Titinius and Brutus's lament (V.3)

- **What happens:** Titinius returns, crowns the dead Cassius and kills himself; Brutus mourns.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Mistrust of good success hath done this deed**" (Messala) — Cassius died because he expected the worst.
  - "**O hateful error, melancholy's child**" — the mistake was born of a gloomy mind.
  - "**Come, Cassius' sword, and find Titinius' heart.**" — Titinius's loyalty to death.
  - "**O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! / Thy spirit walks abroad**" — Caesar's spirit is revenging itself.
  - "**The last of all the Romans, fare thee well!**" — Brutus's praise of Cassius.

### Section 7 — Young Cato and Lucilius (V.4)

- **What happens:** Young Cato dies fighting; Lucilius pretends to be Brutus and is spared by Antony.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**And I am Brutus, Marcus Brutus, I**" — Lucilius risks his life to protect his master.
  - "**I had rather have / Such men my friends than enemies**" — Antony admires loyalty.
  - "**He will be found like Brutus, like himself**" — Brutus will die nobly.

### Section 8 — The death of Brutus and Antony's tribute (V.5)

- **What happens:** Brutus's friends refuse to kill him; Strato holds the sword; Brutus dies; Antony and Octavius honour him.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The ghost of Caesar hath appear'd to me / Two several times by night**" — at Sardis and at Philippi.
  - "**My heart doth joy that yet in all my life / I found no man but he was true to me**" — irony: he was not true to Caesar.
  - "**Caesar, now be still: / I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.**" — his last words.
  - "**Brutus only overcame himself**" (Strato) — no enemy can claim the honour of his death.
  - "**This was the noblest Roman of them all**" (Antony) — the only conspirator who acted for the common good. *(2027 specimen Q3(iv))*
  - "**the elements / So mix'd in him that Nature might stand up / And say to all the world 'This was a man!'**" — a perfectly balanced character.

### 5.2 Key events in order

1. Octavius refuses Antony's order: "Upon the right hand I."
2. The parley: insults exchanged; Octavius vows revenge for Caesar's thirty-three wounds.
3. Cassius tells Messala it is his birthday and describes the omen of the eagles.
4. Brutus and Cassius bid an everlasting farewell.
5. Brutus sends Messala to attack Octavius's wing.
6. Brutus defeats Octavius, but his soldiers fall to plunder; Antony surrounds Cassius.
7. Cassius sends Titinius to scout; Pindarus wrongly reports Titinius captured.
8. Cassius has Pindarus kill him: "Caesar, thou art revenged."
9. Titinius returns with the wreath, crowns Cassius and kills himself.
10. Brutus: "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!"; the second fight.
11. Young Cato dies; Lucilius poses as Brutus; Antony spares him.
12. Brutus asks Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius to kill him; they refuse.
13. Brutus runs on his sword held by Strato: "Caesar, now be still."
14. Antony: "This was the noblest Roman of them all"; Octavius orders an honourable burial.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- There were actually **two battles of Philippi**, about three weeks apart, in October 42 BC. Cassius died after the first and Brutus after the second; Shakespeare compresses them into one day.
- Octavius later defeated Antony (and Cleopatra) at Actium in 31 BC and became **Augustus**, the first Roman emperor — the very one-man rule the conspirators had tried to prevent.
- Shakespeare continued Antony's story in his later play *Antony and Cleopatra*.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Revenge and the power of Caesar's spirit:** Caesar dominates the act though he is dead; both conspirators die acknowledging him.
- **Fate and omens:** the eagles, the ravens, the Ghost — the supernatural foretells defeat; even the sceptical Cassius half-believes.
- **Error and misjudgement:** Cassius dies through a mistake ("melancholy's child"); Brutus's early attack loses the battle.
- **Honour and suicide:** the Romans prefer death to the shame of being led in triumph.
- **Loyalty and friendship:** Titinius, Lucilius, Strato and the others show devotion to the end.
- **The tragic hero:** Brutus is noble, but his idealism and errors of judgement bring disaster to himself and Rome.

### 7.1 Irony in the act

1. Cassius dies on his **birthday** — his life comes "full circle".
2. Cassius kills himself just when the battle has turned in his favour: Titinius was surrounded by **friends**, not enemies.
3. The **same sword** that killed Caesar kills Cassius; Brutus "kill'd not thee with half so good a will".
4. Brutus, who condemned Cato's suicide as "cowardly and vile", dies by his own hand.
5. Brutus rejoices that no man was ever untrue to him — though he himself was untrue to Caesar.
6. The man who led the attack on Caesar's ambition is praised by Caesar's avenger as "the noblest Roman of them all".

### 7.2 Why Brutus is the tragic hero

| Quality of a tragic hero | Brutus |
|---|---|
| noble and admired | "the noblest Roman of them all" |
| a tragic flaw | idealism that blinds him to people's motives; over-confidence in his own judgement |
| errors of judgement | sparing Antony; letting Antony speak last; marching to Philippi; attacking too early |
| suffering | loss of Portia, of Cassius, of the battle |
| recognition | "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!" |
| death that arouses pity | runs on his sword, honoured by his enemies |

**2025 specimen Q1(vi).** **(b) his life had come full circle.** It is Cassius's birthday: "This day I breathed first: time is come round, / And where I did begin, there shall I end."

**2027 specimen Q3.**

**(i) Why does Brutus say, 'No, Cassius, no'? What conditions may force Brutus to go bound to Rome? Brutus says, 'And whether we shall meet again I know not.' What do these words imply?** [3]
When Cassius asks whether he is content to be led in triumph through Rome, Brutus answers **vehemently** that he would **never allow himself to be taken through the streets of Rome as a captive**. He might be forced to go bound to Rome **if they lose the battle / if he is taken captive**. The words imply **uncertainty about the outcome** of the battle — he is **resigned to his fate**, and either of them may die in battle.

**(ii) Explain the following lines in your own words: 'Therefore our everlasting farewell take: / Forever, and forever, farewell, Cassius.' How do these words hint at the manner of Cassius and Brutus' death?** [3]
Since they may never meet again, Brutus bids Cassius a **farewell for all time** — the words **imply the possibility of death and defeat**. They hint (any two) at their **defeat** in the battle, at the fact that **they will not meet again** alive, and at their **tragic deaths** — both, in fact, kill themselves.

**(iii) How does Brutus die? How does his manner of dying contradict the philosophy by which he had lived his life?** [3]
Brutus **takes his own life**: he **runs on his sword, held by Strato**. He had lived by the **Stoic philosophy**, which teaches one to accept suffering and joy with **equanimity** (calm patience) — he had even blamed Cato for killing himself. Yet, **unwilling to accept the humiliation of defeat** at the hands of the Second Triumvirate, he kills himself.

**(iv) At the end of the play, Antony calls Brutus, 'the noblest Roman of them all'. Why does he say this? What does this reveal of Antony's character?** [3]
**Reasons:** Brutus was the **only conspirator who joined in the assassination without envy of Caesar**; he was **motivated by the common good of Rome**; the **elements of Nature were perfectly balanced** in him, so that Nature could proclaim he was an ideal man — "This was a man!" **Antony's character (any one):** he is **noble** — he acknowledges greatness even in an enemy; he **values honour**.

**(v) What strange occurrence had Cassius observed when his army was marching from Sardis to Philippi? What impact did he admit the experience had on him?** [4]
**Occurrence (any two):** **two mighty eagles** had accompanied them from Sardis to Philippi, perching on the foremost banner and feeding from the soldiers' hands; **they had fled that morning**; they were **replaced by scavenging birds — ravens, crows and kites** — which looked down on the army as if it were sickly prey. **Impact:** Cassius had followed the **Epicurean philosophy** and **did not believe in omens**; now he admits that he **partly believes in portents** and superstitions.

**2026 Q1(vi).** **(c) He persuades Pindarus to help him take his life**, giving him his freedom and the sword that killed Caesar.

**2026 Q3.**

**(i) Explain 'O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! …'** [3]
Even in death **Caesar is still powerful**: his **spirit roams the world taking revenge**, making the conspirators **turn their swords against their own bodies**. Cassius and Titinius have just **killed themselves**, Cassius with the very sword that stabbed Caesar.

**(ii) What were Cassius's last thoughts? How did Titinius honour the dead Cassius?** [3]
Believing that **Titinius had been captured** and the battle lost, Cassius thought of his birthday, his life come full circle, and of **Caesar's revenge**: "**Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee.**" Titinius placed **the wreath of victory sent by Brutus on Cassius's brow**, and then, "by your leave, gods", **killed himself with Cassius's sword** — "a Roman's part".

**(iii) What does Brutus promise? Why? What does it reveal of his character?** [3]
He promises that he will **find time later to mourn Cassius fully** and shed the tears he owes him. He cannot weep now because **the battle must go on** and he must lead his men. It reveals his **deep love and loyalty for his friend**, together with his **Stoic self-control** and **sense of duty**.

**(iv) Explain 'funerals'. Why not in the camp? Where will they be held?** [3]
"Funerals" means the **funeral rites (burial ceremonies)**. Brutus does not want them in the camp **lest the sight discourage (dishearten) the soldiers** before the second battle. Cassius's body is to be **sent to Thasos**, an island near Philippi.

**(v) How does Lucilius show loyalty? What does it say of Brutus? How does Antony reward him, and what does this show?** [4]
In the battle Lucilius **pretends to be Brutus** ("I am Brutus, Marcus Brutus, I") to draw the enemy to himself and **save Brutus from capture**; taken prisoner, he declares that **no enemy shall ever take Brutus alive**. It shows that Brutus **inspired deep love and loyalty**, like a true and noble leader. Antony orders his men to **keep Lucilius safe and treat him with all kindness**, saying he would rather have **such men as friends than enemies**. This shows that Antony is **generous** and **admires loyalty**, even in an enemy.

**2026 Improvement Q3.**

**(i) Why a 'losing day'? How will he 'have glory'? Who helps him?** [3]
Because his army has been **defeated at Philippi** and the cause is lost. He will win glory by **dying nobly by his own hand** rather than be captured; his honour in defeat will be **greater than the glory** Octavius and Antony win by their "**vile conquest**". His servant **Strato** helps him, holding the sword on which Brutus runs.

**(ii) Explain 'I found no man but he was true to me'. Was Brutus himself 'true'?** [3]
In all his life he **never met a man who was disloyal to him**; all his friends and followers stayed faithful to the end (Lucilius, Titinius, Strato). Brutus was **true to Rome and to his ideals**: he joined the conspiracy only for the common good, refused bribes, and would not swear an oath because a Roman's word should be enough. **(i) Why does Brutus say, 'No, Cassius, no'? What conditions may force Brutus to go bound to Rome? What do the words 'And whether we shall meet again I know not' imply?** [3]
He is answering Cassius's question: he will **never accept the humiliation of being led through Rome as a captive**; his mind is too great for that. **(ii) To what does Brutus refer by 'the work which the ides of March begun'? How was it begun? What political change would follow if they lose?** [3]
The work of **freeing Rome from tyranny** and restoring the Republic and its liberty. It was begun by **killing Julius Caesar** on the ides of March. If they lose, **Antony and Octavius** (with Lepidus, the **Second Triumvirate**) will take over the rule of Rome.

**(iii) Describe the strange occurrence Cassius confided to Messala.** [3]

**(iv) How does Brutus die? How does his manner of dying contradict the philosophy by which he had lived his life?** [3]
He **runs upon his own sword**, held by his servant **Strato**. Yet, unwilling to face the shame of capture, he **takes his own life**.

His life was gentle, and **the elements were so mixed in him** that Nature might say, "**This was a man!**" It shows that Antony is **noble and generous**: he can **recognise and honour greatness in an enemy**, like a statesman who values honour.

Because the enemy has **come down from the hills to the plains of Philippi**, where Octavius and Antony **wanted to fight them**. His tone is **triumphant and slightly mocking** — he enjoys proving Antony wrong. Antony had expected them to **stay on the hills and upper regions** and not come down.

**(ii) What does 'I am in their bosoms' mean? Why, according to Antony, has the enemy come down?** [3]
"**I know their secret thoughts**" — he understands exactly why they act. They come down with "**fearful bravery**": they would rather be elsewhere, but they hope that by this **bold show** they will **convince us that they have courage**. It is only a show — "'tis not so".

**(iii) Who else is in the Second Triumvirate? How do Antony and Octavius differ in their opinion of him?** [3]
**Lepidus**. Antony scorns him as "**a slight unmeritable man, / Meet to be sent on errands**", fit only to carry burdens like an ass or to be trained like a horse. Octavius defends him as "**a tried and valiant soldier**".

Cassius wanted to **stay where they were** and **let the enemy come to them**, so that the enemy would waste his means and tire his soldiers while they stayed rested and ready. Brutus wanted to **march to Philippi**, because the people on the way were unfriendly and would swell the enemy's army, and because "**there is a tide in the affairs of men**" which must be taken at the flood. **Brutus won**; Cassius gave way.

**A messenger** enters, telling them to be ready: **the enemy comes on in gallant show**, their **bloody sign of battle is hung out**, and something must be done at once. *Personal response; for example:* **Antony**, for his **loyalty to Caesar** and his generosity to Brutus at the end; *or* **Octavius**, for his **calm firmness** — he refuses to take the left side when Antony orders him to ("I do not cross you; but I will do so"), showing the strength of a future emperor.

> **Cassius:** This day I breathed first: time is come round,

**(i)** Where is Cassius, and why has Pindarus gone up the hill? [3]
**Answer:** On a hill on the battlefield of Philippi, where Cassius's army is being surrounded by Antony's. Cassius has sent **Titinius** to find out whether some distant troops are friends or enemies, and sent **Pindarus** up the hill to watch, because his own sight "was ever thick".

**(ii)** Explain "My life is run his compass". [3]
**Answer:** It is Cassius's **birthday**; time has come round in a circle, and he will **end where he began** — his life has come **full circle**, like a line drawn by a compass returning to its starting point. *(2025 specimen Q1(vi))*

**(iii)** What does Pindarus report, and what has really happened? [3]
**Answer:** He reports that Titinius is **surrounded by horsemen**, has dismounted and is **taken**, and that they shout for joy. In reality the horsemen are **Brutus's men**, rejoicing because Brutus has defeated Octavius; they welcome Titinius and give him a **wreath of victory** for Cassius.

**(iv)** How does Cassius die? What are his last words? [3]
**Answer:** He gives **Pindarus**, his bondman, his freedom and orders him to stab him with "this good sword, / That ran through Caesar's bowels". His last words: "**Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee.**"

**(v)** What does Messala call the cause of Cassius's death? What does Titinius do on finding the body? [4]
**Answer:** Messala says, "**Mistrust of good success hath done this deed. / O hateful error, melancholy's child**" — Cassius's gloomy, pessimistic nature made him believe the worst. **Titinius** places the **wreath of victory** on Cassius's head, asks Brutus to see how he regarded Cassius, and **kills himself with Cassius's sword**: "Come, Cassius' sword, and find Titinius' heart."

**Extract B.**
> **Antony:** This was the noblest Roman of them all:
> All the conspirators save only he
> Did that they did in envy of great Caesar;
> He only, in a general honest thought
> And common good to all, made one of them.

**(i)** Of whom is Antony speaking? How had this man died? [3]
**Answer:** Of **Brutus**. Defeated at Philippi, he asked his friends Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius to kill him; all refused; he then **ran upon his sword held by Strato**.

**(ii)** Explain the lines in your own words. [3]
**Answer:** Brutus was the noblest of all the Romans: every other conspirator killed Caesar out of **envy**; Brutus alone joined them from an **honest belief** that it was for the **good of all Rome**.

**(iii)** What does Antony go on to say about Brutus's nature? [3]
**Answer:** "His life was gentle, and **the elements / So mix'd in him** that Nature might stand up / And say to all the world '**This was a man!**'" — his qualities were perfectly balanced.

**(iv)** What were Brutus's last words, and what do they mean? [3]
**Answer:** "**Caesar, now be still: / I kill'd not thee with half so good a will.**" He asks Caesar's spirit to rest at last, and admits that he killed himself far more willingly than he killed Caesar — showing how much he had loved Caesar.

**(v)** What does Octavius order for Brutus? How does the ending of the play show the restoration of order? [4]
**Answer:** Octavius orders that Brutus be treated according to his virtue, "With all respect and rites of burial", his body to lie that night in Octavius's own tent "Most like a soldier". He takes Brutus's followers into his service and calls the field to rest. The **conspirators are dead**, Caesar is **avenged**, and the victors **honour their noble enemy**; a new order under Octavius begins.

**Q2.** How does Antony insult the conspirators at the parley?
**Answer:** He says they showed their teeth like apes and fawned like hounds.
They bowed like slaves, kissing Caesar's feet.
Meanwhile "damned Casca, like a cur", struck Caesar on the neck from behind — "O you flatterers!"

**Q3.** What does Octavius vow at the parley?
**Answer:** He draws his sword against the conspirators.
He will not sheathe it till Caesar's thirty-three wounds are avenged.
Or till he himself, "another Caesar", is killed by the traitors.

**Q4.** Why does Cassius tell Messala that it is his birthday? What is he forced to do?
**Answer:** He asks Messala to witness that, on his birthday, he is acting against his will.
Like Pompey, he is compelled to risk all their liberties on one battle.
This shows his doubts about Brutus's decision.

**Q5.** Describe the omen Cassius saw between Sardis and Philippi. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Two mighty eagles perched on the foremost banner and fed from the soldiers' hands.
They came with the army to Philippi, but this morning they flew away.
Ravens, crows and kites now fly overhead as if the army were sickly prey.

**Q6.** Why does Brutus say, "No, Cassius, no"? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Cassius asks whether he would accept being led in triumph through Rome if they lose.
Brutus will never be taken to Rome as a captive.
"He bears too great a mind."

**Q7.** Explain "My life is run his compass." *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** It is Cassius's birthday.
Time has come round in a circle; he will end where he began.
His life has come full circle.

**Q8.** How does Pindarus's report lead to Cassius's death?
**Answer:** Pindarus reports that Titinius has been surrounded and captured by horsemen.
Cassius, believing his best friend taken, calls himself a coward to live so long.
He makes Pindarus stab him with the sword that killed Caesar.

**Q9.** What had really happened to Titinius? What does he do on finding Cassius dead?
**Answer:** He had met Brutus's victorious men, who gave him a wreath of victory for Cassius.
He places the wreath on dead Cassius's head.
He kills himself with Cassius's sword.

**Q10.** Explain "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!"
**Answer:** Brutus sees Cassius and Titinius dead.
Caesar's spirit is still powerful and walks abroad.
It turns the conspirators' swords into their own bodies.

**Q11.** What does Lucilius do to protect Brutus, and how does Antony respond?
**Answer:** He pretends to be Brutus and is captured.
Antony recognises him and orders him kept safe and treated kindly.
"I had rather have / Such men my friends than enemies."

**Q12.** How does Brutus die? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius refuse to kill him.
He asks Strato to hold his sword and turn away his face.
He runs upon the sword: "Caesar, now be still."

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** How does Brutus's manner of dying contradict his philosophy? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Brutus was a Stoic, accepting good and bad fortune with patience.
He blamed Cato for killing himself, calling it "cowardly and vile".
Yet he will not accept the humiliation of being led captive through Rome.
So he kills himself, as Cato did.

**Q14.** Why does Antony call Brutus "the noblest Roman of them all"? What does this reveal of Antony? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** All the other conspirators acted out of envy of Caesar.
Brutus alone acted from an honest thought of the common good.
His nature was perfectly balanced — "This was a man!"
Antony is noble and values honour, acknowledging greatness even in an enemy.

**Q15.** Explain how Cassius's death is caused by a "hateful error".
**Answer:** Cassius's sight was poor, so Pindarus watched for him.
Pindarus mistook Brutus's joyful men for enemies capturing Titinius.
Cassius, gloomy by nature, believed the worst and killed himself.
Messala: "Mistrust of good success hath done this deed."

**Q16.** How do Cassius and Brutus each acknowledge Caesar at their deaths?
**Answer:** Cassius: "Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee."
Brutus, at Cassius's death: "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!"
Brutus, dying: "Caesar, now be still: / I kill'd not thee with half so good a will."
Caesar's spirit has had its revenge, as Antony prophesied.

**Q17.** Show how Act V is full of loyalty.
**Answer:** Titinius kills himself with Cassius's sword.
Lucilius pretends to be Brutus to protect him.
Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius refuse to kill their master.
Strato holds the sword so that Brutus may die with honour.

**Q18.** What changes do we see in Cassius in Act V?
**Answer:** The Epicurean who scorned omens now partly believes them.
He is gloomy, saying the army lies "ready to give up the ghost".
He acts against his will in fighting at Philippi.
He dies quickly through despair and mistrust.

**Q19.** Why is Brutus considered the tragic hero of the play?
**Answer:** He is noble and admired — "the noblest Roman of them all".
His idealism blinds him; he trusts too much in his own judgement.
His errors (sparing Antony, letting him speak, marching to Philippi, attacking early) bring ruin.
His death arouses pity, and even his enemies honour him.

**Q20.** How does the play end? What is the mood of the final scene?
**Answer:** Antony praises Brutus as the noblest Roman.
Octavius orders an honourable burial in his own tent and takes Brutus's men into his service.
He calls the field to rest to share "the glories of this happy day".
The mood is solemn and respectful, with order restored under Octavius.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (16 marks each)

### Extract-based questions (16 marks each)

**Q1.** Read the extract and answer. **Cassius:** "You know that I held Epicurus strong / And his opinion: now I change my mind, / And partly credit things that do presage." (i) To whom is Cassius speaking? What day is it? [3] (ii) What did Epicurus teach about omens? Why does Cassius change his mind? [3] (iii) Describe the omen he has seen. [3] (iv) How does Messala respond, and how does Cassius reply? [3] (v) How does this omen foreshadow the outcome of the battle? [4]
**Answer:** (i) To Messala; it is Cassius's birthday, the day of the battle of Philippi.
(ii) Epicurus taught that the gods do not interfere in human affairs, so omens mean nothing; Cassius changes his mind because of what he has seen on the march.
(iii) Two mighty eagles perched on the foremost banner on the way from Sardis, feeding from the soldiers' hands, and came with them to Philippi; this morning they have flown away, and ravens, crows and kites fly overhead, looking down as if on sickly prey; their shadows seem "a canopy most fatal".
(iv) Messala says, "Believe not so"; Cassius answers that he believes it only partly, and is "fresh of spirit and resolved / To meet all perils very constantly".
(v) The eagles (symbols of Rome and victory) leaving, and the scavenger birds arriving, foretell defeat and death; Cassius soon dies by his own order, Titinius too, and Brutus by his own sword — the army does "give up the ghost".

**Q2.** Read the extract and answer. **Brutus:** "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! / Thy spirit walks abroad and turns our swords / In our own proper entrails." (i) What has Brutus just discovered? [3] (ii) How had Cassius died? [3] (iii) How had Titinius died, and why? [3] (iv) Explain the lines in your own words. [3] (v) What tribute does Brutus pay to Cassius, and what arrangements does he make? [4]
**Answer:** (i) The dead bodies of Cassius and Titinius, at the place where Messala had found Cassius.
(ii) Believing Titinius captured, Cassius made his bondman Pindarus stab him with the sword that killed Caesar; his last words were "Caesar, thou art revenged".
(iii) Titinius returned with the wreath of victory from Brutus's men, found Cassius dead, put the wreath on his head, and killed himself with Cassius's sword out of loyalty and grief.
(iv) Caesar, though dead, is still powerful; his spirit roams the world and makes the conspirators turn their swords against their own bodies.
(v) He calls Cassius "The last of all the Romans", says Rome can never breed his equal, and that he owes him more tears than he can now shed — "I shall find time, Cassius"; he sends the body to Thasos so that the funeral will not discourage the army, and orders a second fight before night.

**Q3.** Read the extract and answer. **Brutus:** "Countrymen, / My heart doth joy that yet in all my life / I found no man but he was true to me. / I shall have glory by this losing day / More than Octavius and Mark Antony / By this vile conquest shall attain unto." (i) What is Brutus's situation at this moment? [3] (ii) Whom had he asked to kill him, and how did they respond? [3] (iii) What is ironic about "I found no man but he was true to me"? [3] (iv) How does Brutus die, and what are his last words? [3] (v) What do Strato, Lucilius and Antony say of Brutus after his death? [4]
**Answer:** (i) He has lost the battle; with the last of his friends he rests on a rock; the enemy is close and the alarm sounds — he means to die rather than be captured.
(ii) Clitus, Dardanius and Volumnius; all refused — Volumnius: "That's not an office for a friend, my lord."
(iii) Brutus himself was not true to Caesar, who loved him; yet he rejoices that no one was ever false to him.
(iv) He asks Strato to hold his sword and turn away his face, and runs upon it; his last words: "Caesar, now be still: / I kill'd not thee with half so good a will."
(v) Strato: "Brutus only overcame himself, / And no man else hath honour by his death"; Lucilius: "So Brutus should be found"; Antony: "This was the noblest Roman of them all" — the only conspirator who acted for the common good, so balanced that Nature could say, "This was a man!"

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Checking facts)** Cassius killed himself because of a wrong report. What lesson does this teach about acting on information?
**Model answer:** Always verify information before acting on it, especially when the decision cannot be undone; fear and gloom make people believe the worst.

**Q2. (Respect for opponents)** Antony honours Brutus after defeating him. Why is it important to respect opponents, in sport or in life?
**Model answer:** Respect for opponents shows maturity and fairness; it recognises their good qualities and makes reconciliation possible.

**Q3. (Spot the error)** "Cassius died after hearing that Brutus had been defeated by Octavius." Correct this.
**Model answer:** Brutus had defeated Octavius; Cassius died believing (wrongly) that his friend Titinius had been captured.

**Q4. (Values)** Is Brutus's suicide brave or cowardly? Give your view with one reason from the play.
**Model answer:** (Personal response.) Brave in Roman eyes — he refused the shame of being led captive ("He bears too great a mind"); or cowardly by his own Stoic standard — he had called Cato's suicide "cowardly and vile".

**Q5. (Character sketch)** Write three adjectives for Octavius as he appears in Act V, each with a supporting line.
**Model answer:** Self-assertive ("Upon the right hand I"); determined ("Never, till Caesar's three and thirty wounds / Be well avenged"); generous in victory (orders Brutus to be buried "Most like a soldier").

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) A is true, but R is false.
- (b) A is false, but R is true.
- (c) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (d) Both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A.

**Q1.**
**A:** Cassius now partly believes in omens.

**Answer:** (c)

**Q2.**
**A:** Octavius took the left wing of the army.
**R:** Octavius wanted to assert his independence from Antony.
**Answer:** (b)
**Explanation:** He took the right wing.

**Q3.**
**A:** Cassius killed himself.
**R:** He wrongly believed that Titinius had been captured.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** "Mistrust of good success hath done this deed."

**Q4.**
**A:** Titinius killed himself with Cassius's sword.
**R:** Titinius had been taken prisoner by Antony.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** He was never captured; he died out of loyalty.

**Q5.**
**A:** Antony ordered Lucilius to be treated kindly.

**A:** Brutus asked Strato to hold his sword.

**Answer:** (c)

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each extract and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — The parley

> **Antony:** Villains, you did not so, when your vile daggers
> Hack'd one another in the sides of Caesar:

**(i)** Who is Antony addressing?
**Answer:** Brutus and Cassius (the conspirators).

**Answer:** A simile.

**(i)** Who is the bondman?
**Answer:** Pindarus.

**(ii)** Where had Cassius captured him?
**Answer:** In Parthia.

**(iii)** What are Cassius's last words?
**Answer:** "Caesar, thou art revenged, / Even with the sword that kill'd thee."

**(iv)** Who had really surrounded Titinius?
**Answer:** Brutus's victorious men.

> **Antony:** His life was gentle, and the elements
> So mix'd in him that Nature might stand up
> And say to all the world 'This was a man!'

**(i)** Of whom is Antony speaking?
**Answer:** Brutus.

**(ii)** Who held the sword on which he died?
**Answer:** Strato.

**(iii)** Why, according to Antony, did the other conspirators kill Caesar?
**Answer:** Out of envy of Caesar.

**(iv)** Where does Octavius order the body to lie that night?
**Answer:** In Octavius's own tent.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Act at a glance
- **V.1:** enemy on the plain; Octavius: "Upon the right hand I"; parley — "Words before blows"; "Good words are better than bad strokes"; Antony: apes, hounds, Casca like a cur, "O you flatterers!"; Cassius: "If Cassius might have ruled"; Octavius: 33 wounds; "peevish schoolboy … masker and a reveller"; "Old Cassius still!"; Cassius's birthday; Epicurus; eagles gone, ravens, crows and kites; Brutus on Cato; "No, Cassius, no"; "this same day / Must end that work the ides of March begun"; "For ever, and for ever, farewell".
- **V.2:** Brutus orders an attack on Octavius's wing ("cold demeanour").
- **V.3:** Cassius kills his fleeing ensign; "Brutus gave the word too early"; Titinius sent; "My life is run his compass"; Pindarus's false report; "Caesar, thou art revenged"; Pindarus flees; Titinius's wreath; "Mistrust of good success"; Titinius dies; "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet!"; "The last of all the Romans"; body to Thasos.
- **V.4:** Young Cato dies; Lucilius: "I am Brutus"; Antony spares him.
- **V.5:** "poor remains of friends"; Clitus, Dardanius, Volumnius refuse; Ghost seen twice; "beat us to the pit"; "no man but he was true to me"; Strato holds the sword; "Caesar, now be still"; "Brutus only overcame himself"; "the noblest Roman of them all"; burial in Octavius's tent.
