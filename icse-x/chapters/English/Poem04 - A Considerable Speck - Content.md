## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*A Considerable Speck* is a short poem by the American poet **Robert Frost**, written with his typical mixture of **humour and seriousness**. The speaker, a **writer**, is sitting at his desk with his **pen raised** over a sheet of paper. He notices a **tiny speck** on the page. At first he takes it for a grain of dust and is ready to stop it with his pen. Then he realises that it is a **"living mite"** — a creature so small it is almost invisible — and that it seems to have **a will of its own**.

He watches it closely. It reacts to his pen and to the wet ink as if it were **suspicious, frightened and cunning**; it hurries, hesitates, and finally crouches as if expecting death. The speaker is struck by this display of **intelligence** in so tiny a creature. He decides **not to kill it**, but to let it lie on the paper until it falls asleep. He says he does not act out of any sentimental, fashionable "love of everything" — but he **recognises a mind** when he sees one, and respects it. The poem ends with a wry joke: he is always glad to find any sign of **mind** on a sheet of paper.

The poem's title is itself a joke: a **speck** is the smallest of things, yet this one proves **"considerable"** — worthy of serious thought.

### About the poet

**Robert Frost** (1874–1963) is one of the best-loved American poets. Though born in San Francisco, he is closely linked with **New England**, where he farmed and taught, and his poems often use rural scenes and everyday speech to explore deep questions. He won the **Pulitzer Prize for Poetry four times** and read his poem *The Gift Outright* at President John F. Kennedy's inauguration in 1961. His famous poems include *The Road Not Taken*, *Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening*, *Mending Wall* and *Birches*. *A Considerable Speck* was first published in 1939 and collected in *A Witness Tree* (1942).

### The poem's ideas, part by part

#### Part 1 — The speck on the paper

The speaker is **writing**: his pen is raised and ready over a sheet of paper. He notices a speck on the page so small that it could easily be missed. His first thought is that it is a bit of **dust**, and he is about to stop it with the tip of his pen — perhaps to check whether it moves at all, perhaps simply to flick it away.

Then he looks again. It is **"no dust speck"** blown along by his breath, but **"unmistakably a living mite"** with **"inclinations it could call its own"** — that is, a creature with **its own wishes and will**. *(2027 specimen Q7 extract; Q7(i): he first imagined it was a dust particle; "speck" and "mite" tell us it was tiny and seemingly insignificant — yet a living creature.)*

#### Part 2 — The mite's behaviour

The speaker now watches the mite as it reacts to the **pen** and the **wet ink**:

- it **pauses**, as if **suspicious** of the pen;
- it **races** wildly away;
- it seems to **loathe** the smell or taste of the ink;
- it **runs in terror**, then **creeps cunningly**;
- it **falters**, and finally **cowers**, as if resigned to death.

From the way it moves, the speaker concludes — with a touch of humour — that it **must have had feet**, though they are far too small to see. And from the way it **responds** to danger — wary, frightened, clever, desperate to survive — he concludes that he is dealing with **"an intelligence"**. *(2027 specimen Q7(iii) and Q7(iv).)*

#### Part 3 — The decision

The speaker's **first impulse** had been to stop the speck with his pen — and with a drop of ink he could easily have ended the mite's life. But he does not. He decides to **let it lie there till it slept**. *(2027 specimen Q7(v).)*

He explains himself carefully. He does **not** claim to be moved by the fashionable, sentimental **"love of all creatures"** that he thinks is sweeping the modern world — he is not that kind of person. His reason is different: he has **a mind himself**, and he **recognises a mind** wherever he meets it, in whatever form. The mite has shown thought and will; that earns his **respect**. *(2027 specimen Q1(x): the poem expresses respect for intelligent life.)*

#### Part 4 — The final joke

The poem ends with a dry, humorous remark: no one can know, he says, how glad he is to find **any** sign of a mind on a sheet of paper. The joke has two edges. On the surface, he means the mite. Underneath, he is teasing the **writing** that crosses his desk — so many pages, he implies, show very little thought! The serious idea and the humour sit side by side, which is typical of Frost.

### Conclusion: the poet's message

- **Intelligence deserves respect**, wherever it appears — even in the tiniest creature.
- Our first impulse may be careless or destructive; **reflection** can lead to a wiser choice.
- Frost distinguishes **genuine respect for mind** from vague, sentimental "love of everything".
- Size does not measure importance: a speck can be **"considerable"**.
- The poem mixes **close observation**, **humour** and **philosophy** — its tone is reflective and gently ironic.

*(2025 specimen Q1(xi): the poem captures the journey of a mite across a sheet of paper.)*

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The speck

- **Points to note:**
  - **Setting:** a writer's desk; the speaker has a **pen** and a sheet of **paper** with wet **ink** — so he is **writing**. *(2027 specimen Q7(ii): writing; any two of paper / ink / manuscript / pen.)*
  - **First thought:** a **dust particle**. *(Q7(i))*
  - **Words "speck" and "mite":** both mean something **tiny**; they suggest it is **insignificant** — yet it is a **living creature**.
  - **"no dust speck … a living mite / With inclinations it could call its own"** — it has its own **will and desires**. *(2027 extract)*

### Section 2 — Signs of intelligence

- **Points to note:**
  - The mite **responds to stimulus** — the pen and the ink — as a thinking creature would.
  - **Actions (any three, 2027 Q7(iii)):** paused, suspicious of the pen; raced wildly; seemed to loathe the smell/taste of ink; ran in terror; crept cunningly; faltered; cowered; desperate to survive.
  - **Movements showing it had feet (any three, Q7(iv)):** ran, raced, crept, faltered, tried to get away, turned to flee.
  - **Personification:** the mite is given human feelings — suspicion, terror, cunning, despair — which makes us see it as a fellow being.

### Section 3 — The decision

- **Points to note:**
  - **First impulse:** to stop it with his **pen** (a drop of ink would have killed it). *(Q7(v))*
  - **Decision:** to **let it lie there till it slept**. *(Q7(v))*
  - **Reason (any one, Q7(v)):** respect for intelligent life; it was harmless, not evil; it had a mind of its own; sensitivity to life, however small; appreciation of intelligence.
  - **Not sentimentality:** he rejects the vague, fashionable "love of all" and grounds his mercy in **respect for mind**.

### Section 4 — The ending and the tone

- **Points to note:**
  - The closing joke — glad to find any sign of mind **on a sheet of paper** — refers to the mite and also teases thoughtless **writing**.
  - **Tone (2027 Q7(v), personal response):** **philosophical / reflective / meditative** — with gentle **humour and irony**.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Respect for intelligent life:** a mind deserves respect wherever it is found.
- **Reflection over impulse:** the speaker's first impulse is to stop the speck; thought leads him to mercy.
- **True respect vs sentimentality:** Frost values recognising mind, not vague "love of all".
- **Small things, large meanings:** a speck becomes "considerable".
- **Humour and seriousness:** a light incident carries a serious idea.

### 7.1 Tone and mood

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| tone | philosophical, reflective, meditative — with gentle irony and humour |
| mood | curious, amused, thoughtful |
| shift | from casual (dust) to fascinated (a living creature) to respectful (a mind) to wry (the closing joke) |

### 7.2 Significance of the title

**"Considerable"** means large or important; a **"speck"** is tiny. The title is a **paradox** and a joke: the tiny mite turns out to be **worthy of consideration**, because it shows a mind. The title sums up the poem's discovery.

**Solutions — MCQs**

**2025 specimen Q1(xi).** **(a) the journey of a mite across a sheet of paper.**
**2025 Q1(x) / 2027 specimen Q1(x).** **(b) respect for intelligent life.** He spares the mite because he recognises a mind; he explicitly rejects mere sentimental "love".

**2027 specimen Q7.**

**(i) What did the narrator first imagine the 'speck' to be? What does his use of the words 'speck' and 'mite' tell us about it?** [3]
He first imagined it was **a dust particle**. "Speck" and "mite" tell us it was **little / tiny**, and seemingly **insignificant / unimportant** — yet a **living creature**.

**(ii) What was the narrator doing when he spotted the speck? Which two words from the poem helped you come to this conclusion?** [3]
He was **writing**. Words (any two): **paper**, **ink**, **manuscript**, **pen**.

**(iii) What made him realise that he was dealing with 'an intelligence'?** [3]
Its **responses to stimulus** (any three): it **paused, suspicious of his pen**; it **raced wildly**; it showed **loathing for the smell / taste of ink**; it **ran terrified**; it **crept cunningly**; it **faltered**; it **cowered**; it was **desperate to survive**.

**(iv) What actions of the mite led the narrator to conclude that the mite had feet?** [3]
Its **movements** (any three): it **ran (with terror)**, **raced**, **crept**, **faltered**, **tried to get away / escape death**, **turned to flee**.

**(v) Describe the narrator's initial response to the speck. What did he eventually decide to do about it? Why does he make this decision? Give one word to describe the tone of the poem.** [4]
**Initial response:** he wanted to **stop it with his pen** (a drop of ink). **Decision:** to **let it lie there till it slept**. **Reason (any one):** **respect for intelligent life**; it was **harmless, not evil**; it **had a mind of its own**; he is **sensitive to life forms, however small**; he **appreciates intelligence**. **Tone (one word):** **philosophical** (or reflective / meditative).

**2026 Q1(xv).** **(c) Statement 1 is the reason for Statement 2.** The mite's fearful, purposeful movements showed the speaker that it had a mind.

**2026 Improvement Q1(xv).** **(d) It is small but intelligent.** Tiny as a speck, it deserves consideration because it has a mind of its own.

**2025 Q7.**

**(i) What did the narrator first imagine the 'speck' to be? What do the words 'speck' and 'mite' tell us about it?** [3]
He first took it for **a speck of dust** blown by his breath. The words show that it was **tiny** and seemed **insignificant**, yet "mite" also tells us it was **a living creature**.

**(ii) What was the narrator doing when he spotted the speck? Which two words helped you decide?** [3]
He was **writing**. Any two words from the poem: **sheet (paper)**, **ink**, **pen**, **manuscript**.

**(iii) What made him realise that he was dealing with 'an intelligence'?** [3]
Its **responses to danger**: it **paused**, as if **suspicious of his pen**; it **raced wildly** away, seemingly **disliking the smell of the ink**; it **ran in terror**, then **faltered**, **crept** and finally **cowered** — clearly **desperate to survive**.

**(iv) Why did the narrator conclude that the mite had feet?** [3]
Because of the way it **moved**: it **ran**, **raced** and **crept** across the paper, trying to **escape** — so it must have had feet, too small to be seen.

**(v) Describe his initial response, his decision, his reason, and what it reveals of him.** [4]
At first he raised his pen **to stop it** with a drop of ink (to kill it). He finally decided **to let it lie there till it slept**. He spared it because he **respects intelligence** wherever he finds it; the mite was harmless and showed a mind of its own. This reveals him as **sensitive and thoughtful**, with **respect for life** however small.

**2025 Improvement Q1(x).** **(b) let it lie on the sheet of paper** till it slept, out of respect for its intelligence.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A (paraphrased).** Watching the tiny creature, the speaker sees it stop short at the wet ink as if wary of his pen, then dash away, then creep, hesitate and crouch as if expecting the end.

**(i)** Where is the creature, and what is the speaker doing? [3]
**Answer:** On a **sheet of paper** on the speaker's desk; the speaker is **writing** with a **pen** and wet **ink**.

**(ii)** Which actions suggest that the creature is wary and afraid? [3]
**Answer:** It **pauses, suspicious** of the pen; it **runs in terror**; it **cowers** as if expecting death.

**(iii)** Which actions suggest cunning? [3]
**Answer:** It **creeps cunningly**, changing its pace and direction, and **turns to flee** — as if planning its escape.

**(iv)** What does the speaker conclude from its movements? [3]
**Answer:** That it **must have had feet**, though they were too small to see — and, from its responses, that it was **an intelligence**.

**(v)** How does the poet make us feel sympathy for the creature? [4]
**Answer:** Through **personification** — giving the mite human feelings of suspicion, terror, cunning and despair — and through the **contrast** between the tiny, helpless creature and the huge writer with his pen; we see it as a living being struggling to survive, not as a speck to be brushed away.

**Extract B (paraphrased).** The speaker says he does not pretend to the fashionable, all-embracing love of creatures that is popular in the modern world; but he has a mind himself, and recognises a mind when he meets one.

**(i)** What decision has the speaker just made? [3]
**Answer:** **Not** to kill the mite with his pen, but to **let it lie there till it slept**.

**(ii)** What kind of "love" does he reject? [3]
**Answer:** A vague, **sentimental**, fashionable **"love of all"** that he sees sweeping the modern world — feeling without real thought.

**(iii)** What is his real reason for sparing the mite? [3]
**Answer:** **Respect for intelligence** — he has a mind himself and recognises **a mind** in the mite, however tiny.

**(iv)** How does the poem end? [3]
**Answer:** With a joke: he says no one can know how glad he is to find **any sign of a mind on a sheet of paper** — referring to the mite and teasing the thoughtless writing he often reads.

**(v)** Comment on the tone of the poem. [4]
**Answer:** The tone is **philosophical and reflective**: a small incident leads to a serious idea about respecting intelligent life. It is also **humorous and ironic** — the deduction that the mite "must have had feet", the paradox of a "considerable" speck, and the final dig at thoughtless writing keep the poem light.

### 8.3 Writing practice linked to the poem (Paper 1 skills)

**Task A (descriptive).** Describe a small creature you once watched closely — an ant, a spider, a beetle, a bird.

**Tips:** begin with the moment you noticed it; describe its size, colour and movement precisely; show its "behaviour" as Frost does (wary, busy, cunning); end with what you learnt or felt.

**Task B (argumentative).** "Every living creature, however small, deserves respect." Express your views.

**Plan:** stand → small creatures' roles in nature (pollination, soil, food chains) → respect for intelligence and life (Frost's mite) → balance: hygiene, pests and human safety → conclusion.

**Q1.** What did the narrator first imagine the speck to be? What do "speck" and "mite" tell us? *(2027 specimen)*

**Answer:** Writing.
Any two of: paper, ink, manuscript, pen.

**Answer:** It paused, suspicious of his pen.
It raced wildly and seemed to loathe the ink.
It crept cunningly, faltered and cowered — desperate to survive.

**Answer:** It ran in terror.
It raced and crept.
It faltered and turned to flee.

**Answer:** To stop the speck with his pen.
A drop of ink would have ended its life.
He thought at first it was only dust.

**Q6.** What did he finally decide, and why?
**Answer:** To let it lie there till it slept.
Out of respect for intelligent life.
He recognised a mind in the mite.

**Q7.** What kind of "love" does the speaker reject?
**Answer:** A vague, sentimental, fashionable "love of all".
He thinks it is feeling without thought.
His mercy comes instead from respect for mind.

**Q8.** Explain the title.
**Answer:** A speck is tiny; "considerable" means important.
The title is a paradox.
The tiny mite proves worth serious thought because it shows a mind.

**Q9.** How does the poem end?
**Answer:** With a wry joke.
He is glad to find any sign of mind on a sheet of paper.
It refers to the mite and teases thoughtless writing.

**Q10.** How does the poet create sympathy for the mite?
**Answer:** By personification — giving it suspicion, terror and cunning.
By showing its desperate struggle to survive.
By contrasting it with the huge writer and his pen.

**Q11.** What is the tone of the poem? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Philosophical / reflective / meditative.
It is also gently humorous and ironic.
A small incident leads to a serious idea.

**Q12.** Why is the deduction about the mite's feet humorous?
**Answer:** The feet are far too small to see.
The speaker reasons that it "must have had" them.
It is a playful, mock-scientific conclusion.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Poem at a glance
- **Poet:** Robert Frost (1874–1963); first published 1939; in *A Witness Tree* (1942).
- **Situation:** a writer at his desk, **pen** raised, **paper**, wet **ink**.
- **Speck → dust?** He is ready to stop it with his pen.
- **Not dust** — "unmistakably a **living mite**" with **its own inclinations**.
- **Responses (intelligence):** paused, **suspicious of the pen**; raced; **loathed the ink**; ran in terror; **crept cunningly**; faltered; **cowered**; desperate to survive.
- **Movements (feet):** ran, raced, crept, faltered, turned to flee — it **must have had feet**.
- **Decision:** **let it lie there till it slept** — **respect for intelligent life**; not sentimental "love of all"; mind recognises mind.
- **Ending:** wry joke — glad to find any sign of **mind on paper**.
- **Tone:** philosophical / reflective / meditative, with humour.
- **Specimens:** 2025 — captures the **journey of a mite across a sheet of paper**; 2027 — **respect for intelligent life**.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The poem is about a fly | It is about a mite. |
| The poet wanted to kill the mite in the end | He let it lie till it slept. |
| He spared it from sentimental love | From respect for intelligence. |
| He saw its legs | He deduced its feet from its movements. |
| He was reading | He was writing (pen, ink, paper, manuscript). |
| The tone is angry | Philosophical / reflective, with humour. |
