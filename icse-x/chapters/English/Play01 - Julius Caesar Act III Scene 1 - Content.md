## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*Julius Caesar* is a tragedy by **William Shakespeare**, first performed in **1599**. It is based on real events in ancient Rome in **44 BC**, which Shakespeare read about in Sir Thomas North's English translation of **Plutarch's *Lives***. Julius Caesar, a great general, has defeated his rival Pompey and returned to Rome in triumph. Some Romans fear that he wants to become **king** and end the Roman **Republic**. A group of senators, led by **Caius Cassius** and **Marcus Brutus**, plot to kill him.

**Act III, Scene 1** is the **turning point (climax)** of the play. Caesar is assassinated in the Capitol on the **Ides of March (15 March)**. In the second half of the scene, **Mark Antony**, Caesar's loyal friend, cleverly wins the conspirators' trust and gets permission to speak at Caesar's funeral — a decision that will destroy the conspirators.

### Where we are: a recap of Acts I and II

1. **Act I:** The common people celebrate Caesar's triumph. A **Soothsayer** warns Caesar, "**Beware the ides of March**." At the feast of the **Lupercal**, Antony offers Caesar a crown three times; Caesar refuses it each time, though, as Casca reports, he seemed reluctant to refuse. Cassius, jealous of Caesar, works on the noble **Brutus**, suggesting that Caesar is becoming a tyrant. On a stormy night full of strange omens, Cassius gathers the conspirators.
2. **Act II:** Brutus, after a sleepless night, decides that Caesar must die "for the general good" — not for any personal hatred, but because power might corrupt him. He receives forged letters (planted by Cassius) urging him to act. The conspirators meet at his house. Brutus **rejects** Cassius's proposal to kill **Antony** too ("Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers"), and **refuses** to bind them with an oath. His wife **Portia** begs to know his secret. **Calpurnia**, Caesar's wife, dreams of Caesar's statue spouting blood and begs him to stay home; Caesar agrees, but **Decius Brutus** reinterprets the dream flatteringly and mentions that the Senate means to offer him a crown, so Caesar goes. Artemidorus prepares a letter warning Caesar, and Portia waits anxiously.

### The scene, retold in our own words

#### 1. On the way into the Capitol

A crowd waits before the Capitol, among them **Artemidorus** and the **Soothsayer**. Caesar, in high spirits, sees the Soothsayer and says, "**The ides of March are come**" — meaning, "your warning has proved false; the day is here and I am safe." The Soothsayer quietly replies, "**Ay, Caesar; but not gone**" — the day is not yet over.

Artemidorus presses his written warning (his "**schedule**") on Caesar: "O Caesar, read mine first; for mine's a suit / That touches Caesar nearer." Decius Brutus distracts Caesar with Trebonius's petition. Caesar, playing the selfless ruler, replies: "**What touches us ourself shall be last served**." When Artemidorus insists, Caesar thinks him mad, and Cassius pushes him aside: "What, urge you your petitions in the street? / Come to the Capitol." Caesar never reads the warning — a striking piece of **dramatic irony**.

#### 2. Popilius Lena alarms Cassius

A senator, **Popilius Lena**, whispers to Cassius, "**I wish your enterprise to-day may thrive**," and then goes to speak with Caesar. Cassius panics: "I fear our purpose is discovered." He even swears that if the plot is known, "Cassius or Caesar never shall turn back, / For I will slay myself." **Brutus stays calm**: "Cassius, be constant: / Popilius Lena speaks not of our purposes; / For, look, he smiles, and Caesar doth not change." Meanwhile, as planned, **Trebonius draws Mark Antony out of the way**.

#### 3. In the Senate: Metellus Cimber's petition

Caesar takes his seat and asks, "**Are we all ready? What is now amiss / That Caesar and his senate must redress?**" — the words of a king rather than a fellow senator. **Metellus Cimber** kneels: "Most high, most mighty, and most puissant Caesar, / Metellus Cimber throws before thy seat / An humble heart." He begs Caesar to **recall his brother, Publius Cimber**, from banishment.

Caesar cuts him short. Such bowing and flattery ("**couchings**", "**low-crooked court'sies**", "**base spaniel-fawning**") might move ordinary men, but not Caesar. "Thy brother by decree is banished: / If thou dost bend and pray and fawn for him, / **I spurn thee like a cur out of my way**."

One by one the conspirators crowd around him to support the plea: **Brutus** kisses his hand "but not in flattery", and **Cassius** falls at his feet. Caesar is amazed that even Brutus pleads, but he refuses again in his most famous boast:

> "But I am **constant as the northern star**,
> Of whose true-fix'd and resting quality
> There is no fellow in the firmament."

Just as the Pole Star alone stays fixed while all other stars move, Caesar claims to be the one man who never changes his mind. When Cinna and Decius kneel, he scorns them: "Hence! **wilt thou lift up Olympus?**" — as if he were a mountain of the gods that no one could move.

#### 4. The assassination

**Casca** strikes first with the words, "**Speak, hands, for me!**" The others stab Caesar in turn. When Caesar sees **Brutus** among them, he cries, "**Et tu, Brute! Then fall, Caesar!**" ("You too, Brutus?") and dies — at the **foot of Pompey's statue**, the statue of the rival he had defeated.

#### 5. "Liberty! Freedom!" — confusion in the Capitol

Cinna shouts, "**Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead!**" Cassius wants it proclaimed from the public pulpits. The senators flee in panic. Brutus tries to calm everyone: "People and senators, be not affrighted; / Fly not; stand still: **ambition's debt is paid**" — that is, Caesar's ambition has been paid for with his life. Old **Publius**, too shocked to move, is reassured that no harm is meant to him or any other Roman.

Trebonius reports that Antony has fled home "amazed", and that men, wives and children "stare, cry out and run / As it were doomsday."

Brutus calmly accepts that all men must die some day. When Casca says that whoever cuts off twenty years of a man's life cuts off twenty years of fearing death, Brutus agrees: "So are we Caesar's friends, that have abridged / His time of fearing death." He then asks the conspirators to **bathe their hands in Caesar's blood** up to the elbows, smear their swords, and walk to the market-place crying "**Peace, freedom and liberty!**" Cassius proudly predicts that their "**lofty scene**" will be acted again in ages to come, in countries not yet born and languages not yet known — another dramatic irony, since we are watching it being acted.

#### 6. Antony's servant

A **servant of Antony** arrives, kneels and delivers a carefully worded message: Brutus is "noble, wise, valiant, and honest"; Caesar was "mighty, bold, royal, and loving." Antony will love Brutus living more than Caesar dead — **if** Brutus will let him come safely and **explain why Caesar deserved to die**.

Brutus, trusting, promises Antony safety on his honour: "I know that we shall have him well to friend." **Cassius is uneasy**: "yet have I a mind / That fears him much; and my misgiving still / Falls shrewdly to the purpose."

#### 7. Antony meets the conspirators

Antony enters and speaks first to the corpse: "**O mighty Caesar! dost thou lie so low? / Are all thy conquests, glories, triumphs, spoils, / Shrunk to this little measure?**" Turning to the conspirators, he boldly offers his own life: if they mean to kill him too, there is no better hour than Caesar's death-hour, and no better weapons than the swords made rich with Caesar's blood.

Brutus assures him that their hands look bloody but their **hearts are full of pity** — pity for the wrongs of Rome, which drove out pity for Caesar ("As fire drives out fire, so pity pity"). They will receive him with love. **Cassius**, practical as ever, offers Antony a share of power: "Your voice shall be as strong as any man's / In the disposing of new dignities."

Antony then **shakes the bloody hand of each conspirator** by name — Brutus, Cassius, Decius, Metellus, Cinna, Casca, and finally Trebonius ("Though last, not least in love"). He admits his position is on "slippery ground": they may think him either a **coward** or a **flatterer**. He apologises to Caesar's spirit for making peace with his enemies, comparing Caesar to a noble **deer (hart)** brought down by **hunters**: "Here wast thou bay'd, brave hart; / Here didst thou fall; and here thy hunters stand, / Sign'd in thy spoil, and crimson'd in thy lethe."

Cassius asks plainly whether Antony will be counted among their friends. Antony replies that he will be a friend to all of them **on the condition that they give him reasons** why Caesar was dangerous. Then he makes his real request: to **carry Caesar's body to the market-place and speak at his funeral**.

#### 8. Brutus's fatal decision

Brutus agrees at once. **Cassius warns him** in an aside: "You know not what you do: do not consent / That Antony speak in his funeral: / Know you how much the people may be moved / By that which he will utter?" Brutus overrules him: he himself will speak first and explain the reasons for Caesar's death, and will announce that Antony speaks with their permission — which, he believes, will do them more good than harm. Cassius mutters, "I know not what may fall; I like it not."

Brutus then lays down **conditions** for Antony:

1. Antony must **not blame the conspirators** in his speech;
2. he must **speak all the good he can** of Caesar;
3. he must say he speaks **by their permission**;
4. he must speak from **the same pulpit**, **after Brutus has finished**.

Otherwise, Antony shall have no part in the funeral. Antony agrees: "Be it so; / I do desire no more."

#### 9. Antony's soliloquy

Left alone with the body, Antony drops his mask:

> "**O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth,**
> **That I am meek and gentle with these butchers!**
> Thou art the ruins of the noblest man
> That ever lived in the tide of times.
> **Woe to the hand that shed this costly blood!**
> Over thy wounds now do I prophesy …"

He prophesies a **curse** on Italy: **civil war** ("Domestic fury and fierce civil strife"), so much blood and destruction that mothers will merely smile to see their infants cut to pieces, and Caesar's spirit, "**ranging for revenge**, / With **Ate** by his side come hot from hell," will "**Cry 'Havoc,' and let slip the dogs of war**", until the foul deed "shall smell above the earth / With carrion men, groaning for burial."

#### 10. Octavius's servant

A servant arrives: **Octavius Caesar**, Caesar's adopted heir, is within **seven leagues** of Rome. Antony tells him that Rome is dangerous for Octavius now; he must wait until Antony has tested, in his funeral speech, **how the people take** the conspirators' "cruel issue". The two carry off Caesar's body.

### Conclusion: why this scene matters

- It is the **climax** of the play: Caesar dies, but his **spirit** and his friend Antony will dominate the second half.
- It shows **Caesar's pride** at its height — the "northern star" speech — just before his fall.
- It shows **Brutus's idealism and his fatal mistakes**: he trusts Antony, ignores Cassius, and lets Antony speak last.
- It reveals **Antony's skill**: he pretends friendship, wins permission to speak, and then reveals his true feelings in his soliloquy — preparing for the great Forum speech in Scene 2.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Before the Capitol: the Soothsayer and Artemidorus

- **What happens:** Caesar mocks the Soothsayer's warning; Artemidorus's letter is brushed aside.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The ides of March are come.**" — said lightly by Caesar, to show that the warning has come to nothing. "**Ay, Caesar; but not gone.**" — the danger is still ahead.
  - "**What touches us ourself shall be last served.**" — Caesar uses the royal "we/us" and pretends to put his people before himself. Ironically, the letter that "touches" him most could have saved his life.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q2(ii), Q2(iv).

### Section 2 — Popilius Lena and Trebonius

- **What happens:** Popilius wishes the plot success; Cassius fears discovery; Brutus calms him. Trebonius leads Antony away.
- **Key lines:** "I fear our purpose is discovered" (Cassius); "Cassius, be constant … he smiles, and Caesar doth not change" (Brutus).
- **Character contrast:** Cassius is nervous and impulsive; Brutus is steady and observant.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q2(iii).

### Section 3 — Metellus Cimber's suit and Caesar's pride

- **What happens:** Metellus pleads for his banished brother; Brutus and Cassius join; Caesar refuses.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Are we all ready? What is now amiss / That Caesar and his senate must redress?**" — Caesar speaks as master of the Senate: "Caesar and his senate".
  - "**Most high, most mighty, and most puissant Caesar**" — *puissant* = powerful; the triple praise is deliberately excessive.
  - "**I spurn thee like a cur out of my way**" — *cur* = worthless dog; Caesar's contempt for flattery.
  - "**I am constant as the northern star**" — a simile and an image of supreme pride; Caesar sees himself as unchanging and above other men.
  - "**Wilt thou lift up Olympus?**" — hyperbole; Caesar compares himself to the home of the gods.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q2(i).

### Section 4 — "Et tu, Brute!"

- **What happens:** Casca strikes first; Brutus strikes last; Caesar dies at the foot of Pompey's statue.
- **Key lines:** "Speak, hands, for me!" (Casca); "Et tu, Brute! Then fall, Caesar!" (Caesar) — Caesar gives up the struggle when he sees his dearest friend among the killers.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q1(i).

### Section 5 — "Liberty! Freedom!": the conspirators' triumph

- **What happens:** the conspirators proclaim freedom; Brutus calms the senators; they bathe their hands in Caesar's blood.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Ambition's debt is paid**" (Brutus) — Caesar's ambition has been paid for with his death. *(2027 specimen Q1(ii))*
  - "**So are we Caesar's friends, that have abridged / His time of fearing death**" — a twisted logic: by killing him early they have spared him years of fearing death.
  - "**How many ages hence / Shall this our lofty scene be acted over / In states unborn and accents yet unknown!**" (Cassius) — dramatic irony; the scene is indeed acted over in theatres and classrooms today.

### Section 6 — Antony's servant and Antony's arrival

- **What happens:** Antony's servant asks for safe-conduct; Antony mourns over Caesar, offers to die, shakes hands.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Brutus is noble, wise, valiant, and honest; / Caesar was mighty, bold, royal, and loving**" — a balanced, diplomatic message.
  - "**O mighty Caesar! dost thou lie so low?**" — apostrophe (addressing the dead); contrast between Caesar's greatness and his present state.
  - "**Here wast thou bay'd, brave hart … here thy hunters stand, / Sign'd in thy spoil, and crimson'd in thy lethe**" — hunting imagery: Caesar is the noble deer (hart) hunted down; the conspirators are the hunters marked with his blood. A pun on **hart / heart**.
  - "**Either a coward or a flatterer**" — Antony admits how his friendship with Caesar's killers must look.

### Section 7 — Permission to speak

- **What happens:** Antony asks to speak at the funeral; Brutus agrees; Cassius protests; Brutus sets conditions.
- **Key lines:** "You know not what you do" (Cassius); "You shall not in your funeral speech blame us, / But speak all good you can devise of Caesar …" (Brutus).
- **Brutus's mistakes:** he trusts Antony's words; he believes reason will beat emotion; he lets Antony speak **after** him, so Antony has the last word.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q1(ii); 2027 specimen Q2(ii) (the conditions).

### Section 8 — Antony's soliloquy and Octavius

- **What happens:** alone, Antony reveals his grief and anger and prophesies civil war; Octavius is near Rome.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth**" — Antony addresses Caesar's bloody body (not his robe, not the ground). *(2027 specimen Q1(i))*
  - "**That I am meek and gentle with these butchers**" — he admits his friendly behaviour was an act; he calls the "sacrificers" (Brutus's word) **butchers**.
  - "**Woe to the hand that shed this costly blood!**" — a curse showing his **rage**. *(2025 specimen Q1(iii))*
  - "**Cry 'Havoc,' and let slip the dogs of war**" — *Havoc* was the order for an army to slaughter and plunder without mercy; *let slip* = release, as hounds from a leash. War is pictured as hunting dogs let loose.
  - "**Ate**" — the Greek goddess of discord and revenge.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- The real Caesar was stabbed 23 times, according to the ancient historian Suetonius; in Act V, Octavius speaks of "three and thirty wounds".
- "Et tu, Brute?" is not found in the ancient sources in Latin; Shakespeare made it famous.
- The month of July is named after Julius Caesar.
- After Caesar's death, Rome did become an empire under Octavius (Augustus), the very thing the conspirators had tried to prevent.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Pride before a fall:** Caesar's boast that he is "constant as the northern star" comes moments before his death.
- **Fate and free will:** omens and warnings are ignored; Caesar chooses to go to the Capitol and to set Artemidorus aside.
- **Honour and idealism vs political realism:** Brutus's honourable trust is no match for Antony's cunning or Cassius's realism.
- **Appearance and reality:** Antony appears friendly but is planning revenge; the conspirators call themselves "sacrificers" but act as "butchers".
- **The power of words:** the right to speak at the funeral proves more powerful than the daggers.

### 7.1 Dramatic irony in the scene

1. Caesar puts aside Artemidorus's letter — which could save him — saying his own concerns will be "last served".
2. Caesar boasts of being unshakable just before he is struck down.
3. Cassius imagines the scene acted "in states unborn and accents yet unknown" — exactly what happens in theatres today.
4. Brutus believes letting Antony speak "shall advantage more than do us wrong" — it ruins them.

### 7.2 The turning point

Up to this scene, the conspirators control events; after it, **Antony** and **Caesar's spirit** take control. The scene's second half is therefore called the beginning of the **falling action**: everything that follows flows from Brutus's decision to let Antony speak.

### 7.3 Foreshadowing

- "Ay, Caesar; but not gone" — Caesar's death.
- Cassius's misgivings about Antony — Antony's speech and the civil war.
- Antony's prophecy of "fierce civil strife" and Caesar's spirit "ranging for revenge" — Acts IV and V, the ghost at Sardis and Philippi.

**2025 specimen Q2.**

**(i) Where are the speakers? What does 'puissant' mean? Explain: 'Metellus Cimber throws before thy seat a humble heart'.** [3]
The speakers are in the **Capitol (the Senate house) in Rome**. *Puissant* means **powerful**. Metellus means that he **humbly begs the mighty Caesar to listen to his plea**, offering his humble heart at the foot of Caesar's seat.

**(ii) At the beginning of the scene, Caesar says, 'The Ides of March are come.' Why do you think Caesar said this? What does the Soothsayer say in response?** [3]
The Soothsayer had earlier warned Caesar to **beware the Ides of March**. Caesar reminds him, mockingly, that the day **has arrived and nothing has happened** to him. The Soothsayer replies, "**Ay, Caesar; but not gone**" — the day has come but is **not yet over**.

**(iii) What specific duties do the conspirators allot to Trebonius and Casca? Why does Cassius become nervous when Popilius Lena speaks to him as they enter?** [3]
**Trebonius** is to **draw Antony out of the way**, so that Antony is not near Caesar during the attack. **Casca** is to **strike the first blow**. Popilius Lena openly **wishes Cassius success in "your enterprise"** and then goes to talk to Caesar, so Cassius fears that **their plot has been discovered** and will be revealed.

**(iv) What does Artemidorus want of Caesar? How does Caesar respond to his plea?** [3]
Artemidorus **begs Caesar to read his paper ("schedule") at once**, because it **concerns Caesar very closely** — it is a warning of the plot. Caesar replies, "**What touches us ourself shall be last served**" — matters that concern him personally will be dealt with **last**; he thinks the man is mad and does not read it.

**(v) Shortly after this exchange, Caesar is stabbed to death by the conspirators. Whom do you sympathise with — Julius Caesar or the conspirators? Give reasons for your choice.** [4]
*(Personal response — either side, with three plausible reasons.)* **Model answer (Caesar):** I sympathise with Caesar. (1) He was murdered by men who had pretended to be his friends and petitioners, especially **Brutus**, whom he loved — "Et tu, Brute!" (2) He had done nothing illegal; the conspirators acted on **fear of what he might become**, not on proof. (3) His death brought **civil war** and more suffering to Rome, as Antony foretells. **Model answer (conspirators):** I sympathise with Brutus and the honest conspirators. (1) Caesar was **arrogant**, treating the Senate as his own and comparing himself to the northern star and Olympus. (2) He seemed to want to be **king**, which would end the Republic and Romans' freedom. (3) Brutus acted not for personal gain but, as he believed, "for the general good".

**2026 Q1(ii).** **(b) grief and sorrow.** Antony mourns over the body of the great Caesar, whose glories have shrunk to a corpse.

**2026 Q2.**

**(i) Why does Antony say, 'Pardon me, Julius!'?** [3]
He has just **shaken the bloody hands of Caesar's murderers**, one by one, and is **making peace with them in the presence of Caesar's corpse**. He feels this must grieve Caesar's spirit more than his death, so he **asks Caesar's forgiveness** for seeming to be friends with his killers.

**(ii) Where was Caesar 'bay'd'? Explain 'Here wast thou bay'd, brave hart'.** [3]
In **the Capitol (the Senate House)**, at the **foot of Pompey's statue**. A hunted deer is "at bay" when the hounds have **cornered it** and it can no longer escape. Antony means: **here you were trapped and brought down, noble deer**, like a brave stag surrounded by hunters (with a pun on *hart* and *heart*).

**(iii) Name two 'hunters' present. Why are they 'sign'd' and 'crimson'd'?** [3]
Any two: **Brutus**, **Cassius**, **Casca**, **Decius Brutus**, **Metellus Cimber**, **Cinna**. They are "**sign'd in thy spoil**" because they **bear the marks of the slaughter**, and "**crimson'd in thy lethe**" because they are **red with Caesar's life-blood**: they have bathed their hands and swords in it, like hunters smeared with the blood of their kill.

**(iv) What does Cassius ask Antony next? How does Antony respond?** [3]
Cassius asks **what compact Antony means to have with them**: will he be counted among their **friends**, or shall they go on without depending on him? Antony replies that he took their hands **as a friend** and loves them all, **on the hope that they will give him reasons why and wherein Caesar was dangerous** — though he was "sway'd from the point" by looking at Caesar's body.

**(v) Antony's request; who warns Brutus; the reason; what it shows of this person.** [4]
He asks to **produce Caesar's body in the market-place** and **speak at his funeral from the pulpit**. **Cassius** warns Brutus not to consent, because he knows "**how much the people may be moved / By that which he will utter**" — Antony's speech could turn the people against them. This shows that Cassius is **shrewd, practical and a good judge of men**, far more realistic than the idealistic Brutus.

**2026 Improvement Q1(ii).** **(c) Popillius Lena.** He wishes Cassius well in "your enterprise to-day" and then goes to speak to Caesar, so Cassius fears their plot is known.

**2026 Improvement Q1(iii).** **(b) Cassius.** "You know not what you do: do not consent / That Antony speak in his funeral."

**2026 Improvement Q2.**

**(i) Whom does Antony call 'the choice and master spirits of this age'? Name two.** [3]
**The conspirators** who have just killed Caesar — the finest and most powerful men of the time (he says it with bitter irony). Any two: **Brutus**, **Cassius**, **Casca**, **Decius Brutus**, **Metellus Cimber**, **Cinna**.

**(ii) What had the men just done? Why are their hands purpled and reeking?** [3]
They had **assassinated Julius Caesar** in the Capitol. At Brutus's bidding they had **bathed their hands up to the elbows in Caesar's blood** and smeared their swords with it; the blood is still **warm and fresh**, so their hands are red ("purpled") and **steaming** ("reek and smoke").

**(iii) Why does Antony say he would be content to die now?** [3]
**No hour** would be as fit as **the hour of Caesar's death**; **no instrument** as worthy as **their swords, made rich with Caesar's blood**; **no place** would please him so well as **here beside Caesar**; and no death so honourable as one at the hands of "the choice and master spirits of this age". Even if he lived a thousand years he would never be so ready to die.

**(iv) What curse does Antony call down after they leave him alone with Caesar's corpse?** [3]
In his soliloquy over the body he prophesies that **a curse shall fall upon men**: **domestic fury and fierce civil strife** shall burden all Italy; **blood and destruction** shall become so common that **mothers will only smile** to see their infants cut to pieces by war; and **Caesar's spirit, ranging for revenge** with **Ate** (goddess of discord) by his side, shall **cry "Havoc!" and let slip the dogs of war**.

**(v) One word for Antony's conduct; why he behaves so; how the listeners respond.** [4]
*One word or phrase:* **bold**, **daring**, or **cunning and diplomatic**. He offers his life to **test the conspirators' intentions** and **win their trust**, while showing his **loyalty to Caesar**; he knows they will not kill him in cold blood after such a speech. **Brutus** answers, "**O Antony, beg not your death of us**": their hands look bloody, but their hearts are full of pity, and they receive him with **love and respect**. **Cassius** adds that Antony's voice shall be **as strong as any man's** in giving out the new honours.

**2025 Improvement Q1(i).** **(c) Mt. Olympus.** "Hence! wilt thou lift up Olympus?" — he is as immovable as the mountain of the gods (just before, he called himself "constant as the northern star").

**2025 Improvement Q2.**

**(i) Where does the scene take place? What has Cassius just said to Caesar? Who had first made that appeal?** [3]
In **the Capitol (the Senate House) in Rome**. Cassius has fallen at Caesar's feet, begging **"enfranchisement" (the recall from banishment) for Publius Cimber**. The appeal was first made by **Metellus Cimber**, Publius's brother.

**(ii) What does Caesar mean by the first two lines? What does this reveal of his character?** [3]
If he were **like them** — ordinary men — he could be moved by their pleading; if he were the kind of man who **begged others to change their minds**, he too would be moved by prayers. But he is not such a man. The lines reveal his **arrogance, pride and inflexibility**: he sees himself as above other men.

**(iii) Explain the comparison with the northern star.** [3]
The **northern (pole) star** is the one star that **stays fixed** in its place while all the others move; it has **no equal** in the sky. The sky is full of countless stars, all bright, but only one holds its place. Likewise, Caesar claims, among all men he alone is **firm and unshakable in his decisions** — he will not change his order to banish Publius Cimber.

**(iv) What was the task assigned to (a) Trebonius, (b) Metellus Cimber, (c) Casca?** [3]
(a) **Trebonius** was to **draw Antony out of the way** (outside the Senate) so that he could not defend Caesar. (b) **Metellus Cimber** was to **present his petition** for his brother's recall, so that the conspirators could crowd round Caesar. (c) **Casca** was to **strike the first blow** ("Speak, hands, for me!").

**(v) What historic event follows? What were Caesar's last words? What are your feelings for Caesar now?** [4]
**The assassination of Julius Caesar** by the conspirators. His last words: "**Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar!**" *Personal response; for example:* **pity** — whatever his pride, he dies stabbed by men he trusted, and the blow of his dear friend Brutus breaks his heart; *or* little sympathy — his boastful arrogance just before shows the very ambition the conspirators feared. (Either view earns marks with a reason from the text.)

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> **Caesar:** I could be well moved, if I were as you;
> If I could pray to move, prayers would move me:
> But I am constant as the northern star,
> Of whose true-fix'd and resting quality
> There is no fellow in the firmament.

**(i)** Whom is Caesar addressing? What request have they made? [3]
**Answer:** He is addressing the conspirators, especially **Cassius, Brutus and Metellus Cimber**. They have begged him to **recall Publius Cimber**, Metellus's brother, from **banishment**.

**(ii)** Explain "I could be well moved, if I were as you". [3]
**Answer:** If Caesar were an ordinary man like them, their prayers would move him to change his mind; but he is not ordinary, and he cannot be moved by pleading.

**(iii)** Why does Caesar compare himself to the northern star? [3]
**Answer:** The northern (Pole) star alone stays fixed while the other stars move; Caesar claims that he alone among men is **firm and unchanging** in his decisions.

**(iv)** What do these lines reveal about Caesar's character? [3]
**Answer:** They show his **pride, arrogance and self-glorification**; he thinks himself superior to all men, almost a god — ironic, since moments later he is killed.

**(v)** What happens immediately after these lines? Who strikes first and what are Caesar's last words? [4]
**Answer:** Cinna and Decius kneel; Caesar scorns them ("Hence! wilt thou lift up Olympus?"). **Casca** strikes first ("Speak, hands, for me!"), the others follow, and Brutus stabs last. Caesar's last words are "**Et tu, Brute! Then fall, Caesar!**" — he gives up when he sees Brutus among the killers.

**Extract B.**
> **Antony:** O, pardon me, thou bleeding piece of earth,
> That I am meek and gentle with these butchers!
> Thou art the ruins of the noblest man
> That ever lived in the tide of times.

**(i)** Where is Antony and who has just left? [3]
**Answer:** In the Capitol, beside Caesar's body; **Brutus and the other conspirators** have just left after granting him permission to speak at the funeral.

**(ii)** Why does Antony ask pardon? Whom does he call "butchers"? [3]
**Answer:** For having been **gentle and friendly** with Caesar's killers, even shaking their bloody hands. He calls the **conspirators** butchers — reversing Brutus's claim that they were "sacrificers".

**(iii)** What does Antony prophesy over Caesar's wounds? [3]
**Answer:** A **curse** on men; **domestic fury and fierce civil strife** throughout Italy; blood and destruction so common that mothers will smile to see their infants killed; Caesar's spirit, with Ate, crying "Havoc".

**(iv)** Explain "Cry 'Havoc,' and let slip the dogs of war". [3]
**Answer:** "Havoc" was the command for soldiers to kill and plunder without mercy; "let slip" means to unleash hounds. Caesar's spirit will unleash total, merciless war, like hunting dogs released on their prey.

**(v)** What does this soliloquy reveal about Antony? How does it change our view of his earlier behaviour? [4]
**Answer:** It shows his **deep love and grief** for Caesar, his **anger** and his determination to take **revenge**. His earlier friendliness with the conspirators was a **clever act**: he deceived them to gain permission to speak, so we now see him as a shrewd and dangerous opponent.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** At the beginning of the scene, Caesar says, "The Ides of March are come." Why does he say this, and what does the Soothsayer reply? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** The Soothsayer had warned Caesar to beware the Ides of March.
Caesar mocks him: the day has come and nothing has happened.
The Soothsayer replies, "Ay, Caesar; but not gone" — the day is not yet over.

**Q2.** What does Artemidorus want of Caesar? How does Caesar respond? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** He begs Caesar to read his paper at once, as it concerns Caesar closely.
Caesar says, "What touches us ourself shall be last served."
He thinks Artemidorus mad and never reads the warning.

**Q3.** What duties do the conspirators allot to Trebonius and Casca? Why does Cassius become nervous when Popilius Lena speaks to him? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** Trebonius is to draw Antony out of the way.
Casca is to strike the first blow.
Popilius wishes their "enterprise" success, so Cassius fears the plot is discovered.

**Q4.** Where are the speakers when Metellus kneels? What does "puissant" mean? Explain "Metellus Cimber throws before thy seat a humble heart". *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** In the Capitol (Senate house), Rome.
Puissant means powerful.
Metellus humbly begs the mighty Caesar to hear his plea.

**Q5.** Why does Caesar refuse Metellus Cimber's plea? What does he say about flattery?
**Answer:** Publius Cimber was banished by decree, and Caesar will not change his decision.
Flattery — bowing, sweet words, "spaniel-fawning" — may move ordinary men, not Caesar.
If Metellus fawns for his brother, Caesar will "spurn" him "like a cur".

**Q6.** Explain "I am constant as the northern star". What does it reveal about Caesar?
**Answer:** The northern star alone keeps its place while other stars move.
Caesar claims he alone among men is firm and never changes his mind.
It reveals his pride and arrogance, just before his fall.

**Q7.** What does "Et tu, Brute!" mean, and why does Caesar say it?
**Answer:** It means "You too, Brutus?"
Caesar is shocked to see his dear friend Brutus among his murderers.
He stops resisting: "Then fall, Caesar!"

**Q8.** Explain "ambition's debt is paid". Who says it, and to whom?
**Answer:** Brutus says it.
He speaks to the frightened people and senators.
Caesar's ambition has been paid for with his life.

**Q9.** What message did Antony's servant bring to Brutus?
**Answer:** Brutus is noble, wise, valiant and honest; Caesar was mighty, bold, royal and loving.
Antony loves Brutus and honours him.
If he may come safely and be told why Caesar deserved to die, he will follow Brutus.

**Q10.** Why does Antony shake hands with each conspirator? How does he think they will judge him?
**Answer:** To gain their trust and appear reconciled.
He names each in turn, ending with Trebonius, "last, not least in love".
He fears they will think him either a coward or a flatterer.

**Q11.** State any three conditions Brutus lays down for Antony's funeral speech.
**Answer:** Antony must not blame the conspirators.
He must speak all the good he can of Caesar and say he speaks by their permission.
He must speak from the same pulpit, after Brutus has finished.

**Q12.** Why did Cassius object to Antony's speaking? How did Brutus answer him?
**Answer:** He feared the people would be moved by Antony's words.
Brutus said he would speak first and explain their reasons.
He believed Antony speaking by their permission would help them more than harm them.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** Caesar is stabbed to death by the conspirators. Whom do you sympathise with — Caesar or the conspirators? Give reasons. *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** (Personal response; either side with reasons.) Caesar: betrayed by friends, especially Brutus.
He had done nothing illegal; the killers acted on fear of what he might become.
His death brought civil war and suffering to Rome.
(Or conspirators: Caesar's arrogance, wish for a crown, danger to the Republic; Brutus acted for the general good.)

**Q14.** What does Antony's soliloquy reveal about him? How does it change our view of his earlier behaviour?
**Answer:** It reveals deep grief and love for Caesar.
It shows his fury — "Woe to the hand that shed this costly blood!" — and his resolve for revenge.
He prophesies civil war and Caesar's avenging spirit.
His earlier friendliness was a clever act to win permission to speak.

**Q15.** Give two examples of dramatic irony in this scene and explain them.
**Answer:** Caesar sets aside Artemidorus's warning, saying his own concerns will be "last served".
The letter that could save his life is ignored.
Cassius imagines their "lofty scene" acted in ages to come.
The audience is watching exactly that.

**Q16.** Contrast Brutus and Cassius as they appear in this scene.
**Answer:** Brutus is calm; Cassius panics at Popilius Lena's words.
Brutus is idealistic and trusting; Cassius is practical and suspicious.
Cassius offers Antony power and warns against his speaking.
Brutus overrules him, trusting reason and honour.

**Q17.** What mistakes does Brutus make in this scene, and what are their consequences?
**Answer:** He trusts Antony's message and promises him safety.
He ignores Cassius's warning and allows Antony to speak at the funeral.
He lets Antony speak last, after his own speech.
Antony turns the crowd against the conspirators, leading to civil war and their deaths.

**Q18.** Explain "Cry 'Havoc,' and let slip the dogs of war" and the prophecy it belongs to.
**Answer:** "Havoc" was the order for merciless slaughter; "let slip" means to unleash hounds.
Caesar's spirit, with Ate, will unleash total war.
Antony prophesies civil strife throughout Italy.
Blood and cruelty will become so common that pity is choked.

**Q19.** How does Antony show his cleverness when he meets the conspirators?
**Answer:** He sends a respectful message praising Brutus before coming.
He offers his own life, which disarms them.
He shakes every bloody hand and admits his "slippery ground".
He asks only for reasons and a chance to speak at the funeral.

**Q20.** Explain "So are we Caesar's friends, that have abridged / His time of fearing death". What does the bathing of hands show about Brutus?
**Answer:** Brutus argues that by killing Caesar early they spared him years of fearing death.
It is a strange, self-justifying logic.
Bathing their hands in blood is meant as a ritual of sacrifice and freedom.
It shows Brutus's idealism, but also his blindness to how bloody and horrifying the act appears.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (16 marks each)

### Extract-based questions (16 marks each)

**Q1.** Read the extract and answer. **Popilius:** "I wish your enterprise to-day may thrive." **Cassius:** "What enterprise, Popilius?" **Popilius:** "Fare you well." … **Cassius:** "I fear our purpose is discovered." (i) Where does this conversation take place? Who is Popilius Lena? [3] (ii) Why is Cassius alarmed, and what does he threaten to do? [3] (iii) How does Brutus calm him? [3] (iv) What does Trebonius do at this moment, and why? [3] (v) Compare Brutus and Cassius as shown in this episode. [4]
**Answer:** (i) Before the Capitol in Rome, on the Ides of March; Popilius Lena is a senator who is not one of the conspirators.
(ii) Popilius openly wishes their "enterprise" success and goes to Caesar, so Cassius fears the plot is known; he says that if it is discovered, he will kill himself.
(iii) Brutus tells him to be "constant", observing that Popilius is smiling and Caesar's face does not change, so their purpose has not been revealed.
(iv) Trebonius draws Mark Antony out of the way, so that Antony will not be near Caesar to defend him.
(v) Cassius is nervous, impulsive and quick to fear the worst; Brutus is calm, observant and steady; Cassius acts on emotion, Brutus on reason — the same contrast shows later when Brutus overrules Cassius about Antony.

**Q2.** Read the extract and answer. **Antony:** "O mighty Caesar! dost thou lie so low? / Are all thy conquests, glories, triumphs, spoils, / Shrunk to this little measure? Fare thee well." (i) Where is Antony, and what had he done before entering? [3] (ii) Explain the lines in your own words. [3] (iii) What offer does Antony make to the conspirators immediately after this? [3] (iv) How do Brutus and Cassius respond to him? [3] (v) What does this episode reveal of Antony's character? [4]
**Answer:** (i) In the Capitol, beside Caesar's body; he had fled home and then sent his servant to Brutus asking for safe-conduct and an explanation of Caesar's death.
(ii) The great Caesar now lies dead on the ground; all his victories, honours and spoils of war have shrunk to the small space of his body. Antony bids him farewell.
(iii) He offers his own life: if they mean to kill him too, there is no better hour than Caesar's death-hour and no better weapons than their swords stained with Caesar's blood.
(iv) Brutus assures him their hearts are full of pity and they will receive him with love; Cassius offers him an equal voice in sharing out new offices and honours.
(v) He is loyal and grieving, but also bold and clever: by offering his life and shaking hands he wins their trust, hides his real feelings, and gains permission to speak at the funeral — a skilful politician and actor.

**Q3.** Read the extract and answer. **Brutus:** "You shall not in your funeral speech blame us, / But speak all good you can devise of Caesar, / And say you do't by our permission; / Else shall you not have any hand at all / About his funeral." (i) To whom is Brutus speaking, and what request has been granted? [3] (ii) What other condition does Brutus lay down? [3] (iii) What objection had Cassius raised, and how did Brutus overrule it? [3] (iv) What does Antony reveal when he is left alone? [3] (v) Why is this decision of Brutus considered the turning point of the play? [4]
**Answer:** (i) To Mark Antony; Antony is allowed to take Caesar's body to the market-place and speak at his funeral.
(ii) Antony must speak from the same pulpit as Brutus, after Brutus's speech is ended.
(iii) Cassius warned that the people might be greatly moved by Antony's words; Brutus said he would speak first, explain their reasons, and announce that Antony spoke with their permission, which would help them.
(iv) He calls the conspirators "butchers", begs pardon of Caesar's "bleeding piece of earth", and prophesies civil war and Caesar's spirit crying "Havoc".
(v) It hands the last and most powerful word to Antony, who turns the crowd against the conspirators; from here the conspirators lose control, flee Rome, and are defeated at Philippi — Caesar's spirit triumphs.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (In Brutus's place)** If you were Brutus, would you have allowed Antony to speak at the funeral? Give reasons.
**Model answer:** No — Antony was Caesar's closest friend and a gifted speaker; letting him speak last gave him the power to sway the crowd. (A "yes" answer may argue that refusing would have looked guilty; but then Brutus should have spoken after Antony.)

**Q2. (Leadership)** What lesson about pride and flattery does Caesar's "northern star" speech teach a leader?
**Model answer:** A leader who believes he is above everyone and cannot be wrong stops listening to warnings; pride blinds him to danger.

**Q3. (Spot the error)** "Caesar read Artemidorus's letter and still went to the Senate." Correct this.
**Model answer:** Caesar never read the letter; he said personal matters would be "last served".

**Q4. (Debate)** "The end justifies the means." How would Brutus and Antony each respond after this scene?
**Model answer:** Brutus would say killing Caesar was justified to save Rome's freedom; Antony would call it butchery and murder that can never be justified.

**Q5. (Body language)** How does Shakespeare use actions (kneeling, hand-kissing, hand-shaking) to show deception in this scene?
**Model answer:** The conspirators kneel and kiss Caesar's hand as if begging, only to stab him; Antony shakes their bloody hands as if reconciled, while planning revenge — outward gestures hide true intentions.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each extract and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — "But not gone"

> **Caesar:** The ides of March are come.
> **Soothsayer:** Ay, Caesar; but not gone.

**(i)** What warning had the Soothsayer given earlier?
**Answer:** "Beware the ides of March."

**(ii)** Why does Caesar speak these words?
**Answer:** To mock the warning, as nothing has happened.

**(iii)** What does the Soothsayer mean?
**Answer:** The day has come but is not yet over.

**(iv)** What does this exchange foreshadow?
**Answer:** Caesar's death that very day.

### Case 2 — "Et tu, Brute!"

> The conspirators surround Caesar. One cries, "Speak, hands, for me!" and stabs him; others follow. Seeing his friend among them, Caesar cries, "Et tu, Brute! Then fall, Caesar!" and dies.

**(i)** Who cries "Speak, hands, for me!"?
**Answer:** Casca.

**(ii)** What does "Et tu, Brute!" mean?
**Answer:** "You too, Brutus?"

**(iii)** Where does Caesar fall?
**Answer:** At the foot of Pompey's statue.

**(iv)** What does Cinna cry immediately afterwards?
**Answer:** "Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead!"

### Case 3 — Antony's message

> **Servant:** Brutus is noble, wise, valiant, and honest;
> Caesar was mighty, bold, royal, and loving:
> Say I love Brutus, and I honour him …

**(i)** Who sent this message?
**Answer:** Mark Antony.

**(ii)** What did the sender want?
**Answer:** Safe-conduct and an explanation of why Caesar deserved to die.

**(iii)** How did Brutus reply?
**Answer:** He promised Antony safety on his honour.

**(iv)** How did Cassius feel?
**Answer:** He feared and distrusted Antony.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Scene at a glance
- Before the Capitol: "The ides of March are come" — "Ay, Caesar; but not gone"; Artemidorus's warning ignored ("last served").
- Popilius Lena alarms Cassius; Brutus calms him; Trebonius draws Antony away.
- Metellus pleads for Publius Cimber; Brutus and Cassius join; Caesar: "constant as the northern star", "lift up Olympus".
- Casca: "Speak, hands, for me!"; Caesar: "Et tu, Brute!"; dies at the foot of Pompey's statue.
- "Liberty! Freedom!"; Brutus: "ambition's debt is paid"; hands bathed in blood; Cassius: "lofty scene … acted over".
- Antony's servant (Brutus noble; Caesar mighty); Antony mourns, offers his life, shakes hands, asks to speak.
- Cassius objects; Brutus sets conditions (no blame, all good of Caesar, by permission, same pulpit, after Brutus).
- Soliloquy: "bleeding piece of earth", "butchers", "Woe to the hand", civil war, "Cry 'Havoc'".
- Octavius within seven leagues of Rome.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Brutus struck first | Casca struck first; Brutus struck last. |
| "bleeding piece of earth" = the ground | It is Caesar's bloody body. |
| Caesar read the warning | He never read Artemidorus's letter. |
| Popilius Lena was a conspirator | He was not; his remark only alarmed Cassius. |
| Antony spoke before Brutus | Brutus spoke first; Antony last. |
| Antony's friendship was sincere | It was an act; the soliloquy shows his anger. |
