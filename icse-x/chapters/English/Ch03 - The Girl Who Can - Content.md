## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*The Girl Who Can* is a warm, quietly funny story by the Ghanaian writer **Ama Ata Aidoo**. It is told by **Adjoa**, a bright **seven-year-old girl** who lives in the village of **Hasodzi** in Ghana with her mother, **Maami**, and her grandmother, **Nana**.

From the day Adjoa was born, the two women she loves most have been discussing one thing: her **legs**. Nana thinks they are **too thin and long**. In her traditional view, a woman's body exists to **marry and bear children**, and such thin legs will never support the strong hips needed for **child-bearing**. Adjoa cannot see anything wrong with legs that carry her five kilometres to school every day and run faster than anyone else's.

Then Adjoa is chosen to **run for her school** at the **district sports**. She wins the cup for the **best all-round junior athlete** — and Nana, who had always called her legs useless, **carries the gleaming cup home on her back, the way women carry babies**. Nana now admits that thin legs can be useful for something besides child-bearing. Adjoa thinks: that is what I felt and thought all along.

The title says everything: Adjoa is "the girl who **can**" — who can learn, run and win, whatever others think her body is for.

### About the author

**Ama Ata Aidoo** (1942–2023) was one of Africa's most important women writers. She grew up in a **Fante** family in central Ghana, where her father founded the first school in their village. She studied at the **University of Ghana**, became a lecturer, and in 1982 served as Ghana's **Minister of Education**, working to make education free for all. Her works include the plays *The Dilemma of a Ghost* (1965) and *Anowa* (1970), the novel *Our Sister Killjoy* (1977), and the novel *Changes: A Love Story* (1991), which won the Commonwealth Writers' Prize (Africa). *The Girl Who Can* is the title story of her collection *The Girl Who Can and Other Stories* (1997). Her writing often draws on the **oral storytelling** of her people and questions the narrow roles society gives to women.

### The story, retold in our own words

#### 1. "They say I was born in Hasodzi"

Adjoa begins like a storyteller speaking to listeners: people say she was born in **Hasodzi**, a very big village in the **Central Region of Ghana**. She is **seven**, and she lives in a small household of **three women**: herself, **Maami** (her mother) and **Nana** (her grandmother). Her father is absent; Nana refers to him scornfully, as a poor choice of husband that Maami made, and such remarks bring Maami close to tears.

#### 2. Grown-ups and their puzzling rules

Adjoa is a sharp observer and asks many questions. She finds the grown-ups confusing. Sometimes when she says something, Nana is shocked and tells her **never, never, but NEVER** to repeat it. At other times Nana laughs till she cries at what Adjoa has said and repeats it to her friends. Adjoa cannot work out which of her thoughts are forbidden and which are funny, so she learns to keep many of them to herself.

#### 3. The "legs" debate

As far as Adjoa can tell, Nana and Maami have been discussing her **legs** since the day she was born — it was, she says, the first topic she met when she came into the world. Nana thinks they are **too thin and too long**. In Nana's view, a girl's legs must be **strong and fleshy**, able to support **hips wide enough for child-bearing**, because a woman's main purpose is to **marry and have children**. Thin legs, she implies, are almost a misfortune — and she half blames Maami (and Maami's choice of husband) for them.

Maami defends her daughter quietly, but she has little power against her mother's strong opinions. Adjoa, for her part, cannot see the problem. Her legs carry her everywhere; they walk and run perfectly well. She wishes she could tell her two favourite people **not to worry** — that her legs are nothing to fight over. She wonders, too, why legs should be judged only by whether they can carry babies.

#### 4. School: Maami's dream

The other subject on which the two women disagree is **school**. Maami never went to school and feels her lack of education keenly. She is determined that Adjoa will be educated, so that she does not grow up in ignorance as Maami did. Nana thinks school is largely a **waste of time** for a girl. Maami wins: Adjoa goes to school.

The school is about **five kilometres** away, and Adjoa walks there and back every day. Other children complain about the distance; Adjoa does not mind, because for her, simply, **school is nice**.

#### 5. The secret runner

At school Adjoa has been **winning races** for a long time, but she has never told anyone at home — it did not seem important. Then she is **selected to run for the school** in the **junior section of the district sports**. The news causes a stir at home. Nana can hardly believe it and **goes to the school herself** to find out whether it is true.

#### 6. Nana changes

Something surprising happens. Nana begins to **take care of Adjoa's school uniform** every day — washing it and **ironing it with a charcoal iron borrowed from a neighbour** until it is stiff and shining. She puts on fresh clothes and **walks to the school** to watch the practice, keeping a little behind the children. She tells Adjoa she has no interest in running races — but her actions show that she is proud. She starts to look at Adjoa's legs in a new way.

#### 7. The cup

At the district sports Adjoa **wins her races** and is awarded the cup for the **best all-round junior athlete**. Nana, who had always grumbled about those "useless" thin legs, insists on **carrying the gleaming cup home on her back** — **the way women carry babies and other precious things**.

Nana now says that even if Adjoa's thin legs may not be the kind people expect for carrying children, they are clearly good for **other things** — such as running and winning. Adjoa reflects quietly that **this is exactly how she had been feeling and thinking all along**. *(2027 specimen Q1(xv).)* Maami, overwhelmed, says nothing at all — her silence full of joy and relief.

### Conclusion: what the story teaches

- A girl's worth is **not limited** to marriage and child-bearing; her body and mind can do many things.
- **Education** opens doors: it is at school that Adjoa's talent is discovered.
- **Traditions can change** when people see new possibilities — Nana changes her mind out of love and pride.
- Children often understand things before adults do; Adjoa "knew all along".
- The story is told with gentle **humour**, through the innocent but clear eyes of a child.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The opening and the household

- **What happens:** Adjoa introduces herself, her village and her family of three women.
- **Points to note:**
  - The **oral opening** ("They say I was born in Hasodzi") sounds like a story told aloud.
  - **Setting:** Hasodzi, a big village in the Central Region of Ghana.
  - **Family:** a household of three generations of women; the father is absent and spoken of with disapproval.

### Section 2 — Adjoa and the adults

- **What happens:** Adjoa describes how adults react unpredictably to what she says.
- **Points to note:**
  - **"never, never, but NEVER"** — Nana's emphatic order not to repeat some remarks; the capital letters show the speaking voice.
  - The **contradiction**: some of Adjoa's remarks are forbidden, others are laughed at and repeated — so she learns to stay quiet.
  - This shows how **children's (especially girls') voices** are controlled by adults.

### Section 3 — The legs

- **What happens:** the long-running debate about Adjoa's thin legs.
- **Points to note:**
  - Nana disapproves because the legs are **slender / thin and long**. *(2025 specimen Q1(xiii))*
  - Nana's reasoning: thin legs cannot support **child-bearing hips**; a woman's role is to **marry and bear children**.
  - Maami defends Adjoa but is silenced by her mother and by reminders of her failed marriage.
  - Adjoa sees no problem — her legs serve her well — and wishes her two favourite people would stop worrying.

### Section 4 — The argument about school

- **What happens:** Maami insists on school; Nana thinks it a waste of time.
- **Points to note:**
  - Maami, **unlettered** herself, wants her daughter to escape ignorance.
  - Adjoa walks **five kilometres** each way and loves it: **school is nice**.
  - Education is the path by which Adjoa's talent is discovered.

### Section 5 — Selected for the district sports

- **What happens:** Adjoa, who has secretly won races for years, is chosen for the junior section of the district sports; Nana goes to school to check.
- **Points to note:** Adjoa's modesty (she never boasted); Nana's disbelief; the news shifts the household's view of her legs.

### Section 6 — Nana's new care

- **What happens:** Nana washes and irons Adjoa's uniform daily, walks to school to watch, and looks at her legs with new eyes.
- **Points to note:**
  - Actions speak louder than words: Nana says she does not care for races, but her care shows **pride and support**.
  - The **charcoal iron borrowed from a neighbour** and the stiff, shining uniform are signs of her effort.

### Section 7 — The cup and the ending

- **What happens:** Adjoa wins the cup for the best all-round junior athlete; Nana carries it on her back like a baby; Nana admits thin legs can be useful; Adjoa says she thought so all along.
- **Points to note:**
  - **Symbolism:** carrying the cup **like a baby** — the achievement replaces the baby as the thing precious enough to carry on one's back.
  - **Nana's change:** tradition bends when faced with real achievement and love.
  - **Adjoa's final thought** — her legs were useful for more than child-bearing. *(2027 specimen Q1(xv))*
  - **Maami's silence** at the end — speechless with joy; perhaps she feels vindicated.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- In many communities in Ghana and elsewhere in West Africa, babies are carried on the mother's back, tied with a cloth. Nana's carrying of the cup in the same way is a powerful image.
- The **Fante** (Fanti) people of Ghana, like several Akan groups, are traditionally **matrilineal** — family descent is traced through the mother's line.
- Ghana has produced many women athletes, and girls' participation in sport is encouraged by schools and national programmes. Sport is widely recognised as a way of building girls' confidence.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Gender roles:** the story challenges the belief that a woman's body and life exist only for marriage and child-bearing.
- **Education:** school gives Adjoa knowledge, opportunity and the discovery of her talent.
- **Tradition and change:** the old view (Nana) gives way to new possibilities when faced with achievement and love.
- **Self-belief:** Adjoa trusts her own body and judgement even when adults doubt her.
- **Three generations of women:** the story shows love, conflict and solidarity among grandmother, mother and daughter.
- **The voice of the child:** adults silence children, but the child sees clearly.

### 7.1 Symbols in the story

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Adjoa's legs | how society judges a girl's worth by her body |
| school | knowledge, opportunity, the modern world |
| running | freedom, ability, moving beyond old limits |
| the ironed uniform | Nana's new support and pride |
| the cup carried like a baby | achievement valued as highly as motherhood; tradition embracing change |

### 7.2 The child narrator and the oral style

| Feature | Example |
|---|---|
| storyteller's opening | "They say" she was born in Hasodzi |
| speaking voice | "never, never, but NEVER" in capitals |
| simple, direct judgements | "school is nice" |
| humour from innocence | Adjoa's puzzlement at adult rules |
| gaps the reader fills | Maami's silences; the absent father |

### 7.3 Significance of the title

"The Girl Who **Can**" stresses **ability** — what a girl *can* do, rather than what others say she *cannot* or *should* do. It answers Nana's doubts: Adjoa can learn, can run, can win. The title also suggests that every girl is a "girl who can" when given the chance.

**2026 Improvement Q5.**

**(i) Who are Nana and Maami? What was Adjoa's problem?** [3]
**Nana** is Adjoa's **grandmother** and **Maami** her **mother**. Adjoa's own problem was that she **thought things she had no words to say**, and she **could not tell when to speak and when to keep quiet**: some of her remarks made Nana order her "never, never, but NEVER" to repeat them, while others made Nana laugh till she cried. (Nana's "problems" were real hardships such as drought and hunger.)

**(ii) Maami's and Nana's views on Adjoa's education. Why did Maami want it?** [3]
**Maami** insisted that Adjoa go to school; **Nana** thought school **a waste of time** for a girl (though she laughed that with legs like hers Adjoa might as well go). Maami, who had **never been to school**, felt her own **ignorance** keenly and wanted her daughter to **learn to read, write and do sums** and so have a better life.

**(iii) Why was Nana critical of Adjoa's legs?** [3]
She thought them **too thin and long**. In her traditional view a girl needs **strong, fleshy legs to carry wide, child-bearing hips**, since a woman's chief purpose is to **marry and bear children**; thin legs, she feared, would make Adjoa unfit for that role.

**(iv) Three instances of Nana's pride after Adjoa's selection.** [3]
Any three: she **went herself to check** that the news was true; she **washed the school uniform** and **ironed it with a charcoal iron borrowed from a neighbour** until it was stiff and shining; she put on **fresh clothes** and **followed the children to watch the practice** every afternoon; she kept **looking at Adjoa's legs** with a new expression; at the end she **carried the cup home on her back like a baby**.

### 8.2 Practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A (paraphrased).** Adjoa says that her grandmother and her mother seem to have been talking about her legs since the day she was born. She has always wanted to tell her two favourite people not to worry, and that her legs need not be something for them to quarrel over.

**(i)** Who is the narrator? Where does she live, and with whom? [3]
**Answer:** **Adjoa**, a **seven-year-old** girl. She lives in **Hasodzi**, a big village in the Central Region of **Ghana**, with her mother **Maami** and her grandmother **Nana**.

**(ii)** What does Nana think is wrong with Adjoa's legs? Why does it matter to her? [3]
**Answer:** They are **too thin and long (slender)**. Nana believes a woman's legs must be strong enough to support **child-bearing hips**, because a woman's main role is to **marry and have children**.

**(iii)** How does Maami respond? Why is her position difficult? [3]
**Answer:** She **defends** Adjoa quietly. Her position is difficult because Nana is the powerful head of the family, and Nana reminds her of her **poor choice of husband**, which hurts her and silences her.

**(iv)** How does Adjoa herself see her legs? [3]
**Answer:** She sees **nothing wrong** with them: they carry her **five kilometres** to school every day and they are good at **running**. She cannot understand why legs should be judged only by child-bearing.

**(v)** What does the "legs" debate reveal about the society Adjoa lives in? Give your view. [4]
**Answer:** It reveals a **traditional society** in which a girl's worth is measured by her **body** and her future role as **wife and mother**; her talents and wishes count for little, and even her mother and grandmother judge her by these standards. *(Personal view:)* such ideas are **unfair and limiting**, because they ignore a girl's mind and abilities — as the story shows, Adjoa's "useless" legs make her a champion.

**Extract B (paraphrased).** When the news comes that Adjoa has been picked to run for the school at the district sports, Nana cannot believe it and goes to the school herself to check. After that, every day, she washes Adjoa's uniform and irons it with a charcoal iron borrowed from a neighbour.

**(i)** Why had Adjoa's family not known about her running? [3]
**Answer:** Adjoa had been **winning races at school for a long time** but **never told** anyone at home — she did not think it important, and she was **modest**.

**(ii)** Why does Nana go to the school? What does this show about her? [3]
**Answer:** To **check whether the news is true** — she can hardly believe it of the girl whose legs she thought useless. It shows she is **practical, strong-willed** and **deeply interested** in Adjoa, even if she hides it.

**(iii)** What does Nana's care of the uniform reveal? [3]
**Answer:** It shows her **change of heart**: though she says she does not care for running, she is **proud** and wants Adjoa to look her best; she takes trouble, even **borrowing a charcoal iron**.

**(iv)** What other thing does Nana do while Adjoa is preparing? [3]
**Answer:** She puts on fresh clothes and **walks to the school** to watch, keeping a little behind the children; she looks at Adjoa's legs **in a new way**.

**(v)** "Actions speak louder than words." How does this apply to Nana? [4]
**Answer:** Nana's **words** remain gruff — she claims she has no interest in races — but her **actions** show love and pride: she checks the news herself, irons the uniform until it shines, walks to school to watch, and finally **carries the cup home on her back like a baby**. **Extract C (paraphrased).** After the district sports, Nana carries the shining cup home on her back, the way women carry babies and precious things, and remarks that thin legs can be good for something after all. Adjoa thinks that this is what she had felt and thought all along.

**(ii)** Why is it significant that Nana carries the cup on her back? [3]
**Answer:** Women carry **babies** on their backs; Nana carries the cup in the same way, showing that she now values Adjoa's **achievement** as highly as motherhood — a powerful **symbol** of her changed view.

**(iii)** What does Nana now say about thin legs? [3]
**Answer:** That even if they may not be the kind people expect for **child-bearing**, they are clearly good for **other things** — such as **running and winning**.

**(iv)** What does Adjoa's final thought suggest? [3]
**Answer:** That she had **known all along** her legs were useful for **more than child-bearing** (2027 specimen Q1(xv)); she had trusted herself even when adults doubted her.

**(v)** What message does the ending give to readers today? [4]
**Answer:** That girls should not be limited by **stereotypes** about their bodies or their roles; with **education and opportunity**, they can achieve in any field. ### 8.3 Writing practice linked to the story (Paper 1 skills)

**Task A (argumentative, Paper 1 Q1 style).** "Girls and boys should have equal opportunities in sport." Express your views for or against.

**Plan:**
1. **Introduction:** state your position clearly.
2. **Point 1:** sport builds health and confidence for everyone (Adjoa's example).
3. **Point 2:** stereotypes ("girls are not built for it") limit talent.
4. **Point 3:** equal facilities and encouragement from families and schools.
5. **Counter-argument and reply:** some say traditions or safety concerns; answer with fair rules and support.

**Task B (narrative).** Write a story about a time when someone doubted you, and you proved them wrong.

**Tips:** begin in the moment of doubt; show the doubter's words and your feelings; build to a clear turning point; end with a change in the doubter (as with Nana) or a quiet realisation (as with Adjoa).

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** Who is the narrator? Describe her household.
**Answer:** Adjoa, a seven-year-old girl from Hasodzi in Ghana.

**Q2.** Why does Adjoa find the adults puzzling?
**Answer:** Sometimes Nana is shocked by what she says and forbids her to repeat it.

**Q3.** Why did Nana disapprove of Adjoa's legs? *(2025 specimen)*

**Q4.** How does Maami respond to Nana's criticism?
**Answer:** She defends Adjoa quietly.

Nana's remarks about her failed marriage hurt and silence her.

**Q5.** What did Adjoa think about her own legs?
**Answer:** She saw nothing wrong with them.

She wished Nana and Maami would stop worrying and arguing about them.

**Q6.** What did Maami and Nana disagree about besides the legs?
**Answer:** School.

**Answer:** She enjoys it: "school is nice".

**Q9.** How does Nana show her change of attitude before the sports?
**Answer:** She washes and irons Adjoa's uniform daily with a borrowed charcoal iron.

**Q10.** What did Adjoa win, and how did Nana carry it home?
**Answer:** The cup for the best all-round junior athlete at the district sports.
Nana carried it on her back.
That is the way women carry babies and precious things.

**Q11.** What does Adjoa's final thought suggest? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** She thinks this is how she had felt and thought all along.
She had known her legs were useful for more than child-bearing.
She had trusted herself even when adults doubted her.

**Q12.** How does Maami react at the end?
**Answer:** She is speechless.
Her silence is full of joy and relief.
She is vindicated in her belief in education and in her daughter.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** Trace Nana's change of heart in the story.
**Answer:** At first she criticises Adjoa's thin legs as useless for child-bearing.
When Adjoa is selected, she goes to the school to check the news.
She irons the uniform daily and walks to school to watch.
Finally she carries the cup home like a baby and admits thin legs are useful.

**Q14.** Explain the symbolism of Nana carrying the cup on her back.
**Answer:** Women carry babies on their backs.
Nana carries the cup in the same way.
She now values Adjoa's achievement as highly as motherhood.
Tradition embraces a new possibility for girls.

**Q15.** How does the story challenge traditional ideas about girls?
**Answer:** Nana judges Adjoa only by her future as a wife and mother.
Adjoa's "useless" legs make her a champion athlete.
Education and sport open new paths for her.
Even Nana accepts that a girl's body is good for more than child-bearing.

**Q16.** What role does education play in the story?
**Answer:** Maami insists on it so Adjoa will not live in ignorance.
Adjoa loves school and walks five kilometres daily.
At school her running talent is discovered.
Education leads to the achievement that changes the family's views.

**Q17.** Write a character sketch of Adjoa.
**Answer:** A bright, observant seven-year-old who notices adults' contradictions.
Loving towards her "two favourite people".
Hard-working, modest and talented — the best all-round junior athlete.
Quietly confident in herself all along.

**Q18.** Compare Maami and Nana.
**Answer:** Nana is the strong-willed head of the house; Maami is quiet with little power.
Nana is traditional; Maami values education.
Nana criticises Adjoa's legs; Maami defends her.
At the end, Nana proudly carries the cup; Maami is speechless with joy.

**Q19.** What is the effect of using a seven-year-old narrator?
**Answer:** We see adult behaviour through innocent eyes, which creates humour.
Adjoa's simple logic exposes the unfairness of the "legs" debate.
Gaps in her understanding (Maami's silences, the absent father) let the reader read between the lines.
It gives a girl's voice the central place in the story.

**Q20.** Discuss the significance of the title.
**Answer:** "Can" stresses ability, not limitation.
It answers Nana's doubts: Adjoa can learn, run and win.
It celebrates a girl's potential beyond traditional roles.
It suggests every girl is a "girl who can" when given a chance.
