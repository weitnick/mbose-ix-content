## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

Act IV takes place some time after Caesar's funeral. Rome is now torn by **civil war**. Two sides face each other:

- In **Rome**, **Mark Antony**, **Octavius Caesar** (Caesar's young adopted heir) and **Lepidus** have joined together as the **triumvirs** (a ruling group of three). They are drawing up lists of enemies to be put to death.
- In **Asia Minor**, near **Sardis**, **Brutus** and **Cassius** have raised armies. But the partnership between them is cracking.

Act IV shows that both sides are far from noble. The triumvirs coldly bargain over the lives of their own relatives; Antony despises his partner Lepidus. Brutus and Cassius quarrel bitterly over money and honour, make up, and then make the fatal decision to march to **Philippi**. The act ends with **Caesar's Ghost** appearing to Brutus — "thou shalt see me at Philippi."

### Where we are: a recap of Act III

Caesar was assassinated in the Capitol (III.1). In the Forum, Brutus explained the killing in a calm prose speech and was cheered; then Antony, in his great funeral speech, turned the people against the conspirators with irony, Caesar's will, his torn mantle and his bloody body (III.2). The mob rioted; Brutus and Cassius "are rid like madmen through the gates of Rome"; Octavius arrived; and the innocent Cinna the poet was killed for his name (III.3).

### Scene 1, retold: the triumvirs in Rome

#### 1. "These many, then, shall die"

**Antony, Octavius and Lepidus** sit around a table at Antony's house in Rome, marking ("**pricking**") the names of men who are to be put to death. This is a **proscription**: a list of political enemies whose lives and property are forfeit.

They bargain over lives like merchants. Octavius tells Lepidus that his **brother** must die; Lepidus agrees, on condition that **Publius, Antony's sister's son**, also dies. Antony agrees at once: "He shall not live; look, with a spot I damn him." The coldness is shocking: each sacrifices a relative to gain a political advantage. The men who accused the conspirators of murder are now planning many more murders.

#### 2. Cutting Caesar's legacies

Antony sends Lepidus to **Caesar's house** to fetch **the will**, so that they can "determine / How to cut off some charge in legacies" — that is, reduce the gifts Caesar left to the people. This is the same will Antony waved before the crowd in the Forum to win their love. Now that the people have served his purpose, he wants to **cut their legacies**. It shows Antony as a **cynical, ruthless politician**.

#### 3. "A slight unmeritable man"

As soon as Lepidus has gone, Antony speaks of him with contempt:

> "**This is a slight unmeritable man,
> Meet to be sent on errands:** is it fit,
> The three-fold world divided, he should stand
> One of the three to share it?"

*Slight* means insignificant; *unmeritable* means without merit; *meet* means fit. Antony thinks Lepidus is fit only to run errands, not to share the "three-fold world" (the Roman world divided among three rulers). *(2025 specimen Q1(iv).)*

Octavius points out that Antony himself chose Lepidus and accepted his vote on who should die. Antony replies that he has "seen more days" than Octavius. They are using Lepidus to carry the **blame** ("slanderous loads") for their harsh actions. Antony then compares Lepidus to an **ass** carrying gold: he will "groan and sweat under the business, / Either led or driven, as we point the way"; when he has carried their treasure where they want it, they will "take down his load, and turn him off, / Like to the empty ass, to shake his ears, / And graze in commons."

Octavius objects that Lepidus is "a tried and valiant soldier." Antony answers, "**So is my horse, Octavius**": Antony feeds his horse and teaches it to fight, turn, stop and run; Lepidus is no more than that:

> "**He must be taught, and train'd, and bid go forth;
> A barren-spirited fellow;** one that feeds
> On objects, arts and imitations,
> Which, out of use and staled by other men,
> Begin his fashion: do not talk of him,
> But as a property."

Lepidus is "**barren-spirited**" (without ideas of his own); he picks up fashions only after others have tired of them; he is merely a "**property**" — a tool to be used. *(2027 specimen Q1(iv).)*

#### 4. "Brutus and Cassius are levying powers"

Then Antony turns to "great things": **Brutus and Cassius are raising armies** ("levying powers"); the triumvirs must gather their forces, combine their alliance, and decide in council how to uncover hidden dangers and meet open ones. Octavius agrees: they are like a bear tied to a stake and "**bay'd about with many enemies**", and "some that smile have in their hearts, I fear, / Millions of mischiefs."

### Scene 2, retold: before Brutus's tent near Sardis

#### 5. "A hot friend cooling"

At Brutus's camp near **Sardis**, **Lucilius** returns from Cassius's camp with **Pindarus**, Cassius's servant. Brutus says that Cassius, either through a change in himself or through bad officers, has given him cause to wish some things undone. Lucilius reports that Cassius received him politely, but not with the old warmth and friendly talk.

Brutus answers with a famous image:

> "Thou hast described
> **A hot friend cooling**: ever note, Lucilius,
> When love begins to sicken and decay,
> It useth an enforced ceremony.
> There are no tricks in plain and simple faith."

When friendship begins to die, it covers itself with forced politeness. False men ("hollow men") are like horses that are spirited at the start ("hot at hand") and make a fine show, but when they feel the "bloody spur" they drop their crests and "like deceitful jades, / Sink in the trial" (*jades* = worn-out horses).

#### 6. "Most noble brother, you have done me wrong"

Cassius arrives with his army. His first words are, "**Most noble brother, you have done me wrong.**" Brutus answers, "Judge me, you gods! wrong I mine enemies? / And, if not so, how should I wrong a brother?" He insists that they must not quarrel in front of their armies, "Which should perceive nothing but love from us." They go into Brutus's tent; officers guard the door (**Lucilius and Titinius** in most school editions).

### Scene 3, retold: the quarrel in Brutus's tent

#### 7. Lucius Pella and the "itching palm"

Cassius states his complaint: Brutus has **condemned and disgraced Lucius Pella** for **taking bribes from the Sardians**, and has **ignored Cassius's letters** pleading for Pella, whom Cassius knew. Brutus replies that Cassius wronged himself by writing for such a man. Cassius argues that in times of war it is not wise to criticise every small fault ("every nice offence").

Brutus strikes back: Cassius himself is accused of having "**an itching palm**" — a greedy hand eager for money — and of **selling offices for gold** to people who do not deserve them. Cassius is stung: if anyone but Brutus said this, it would be his last speech.

#### 8. "Remember March, the ides of March remember"

Brutus reminds him why they killed Caesar:

> "**Remember March, the ides of March remember:**
> Did not great Julius bleed for justice' sake? …
> What, shall one of us
> That struck the foremost man of all this world
> But for supporting robbers, shall we now
> Contaminate our fingers with base bribes …?
> **I had rather be a dog, and bay the moon,
> Than such a Roman.**"

They killed "the foremost man of all this world" for **justice**, partly because he protected robbers (corrupt officials); should they now dirty their own hands with bribes? Brutus would rather be a dog howling at the moon than such a Roman.

#### 9. The quarrel grows

The argument becomes personal and childish:

- Cassius: "I am a soldier, I, / **Older in practice, abler than yourself** / To make conditions." — Brutus: "Go to; you are not, Cassius."
- Brutus calls him "**slight man**" and mocks his "rash choler" (hot temper): "Shall I be frighted when a madman stares?" He will use Cassius "for my mirth, yea, for my laughter, / When you are waspish."
- Cassius: "I said, an **elder** soldier, not a **better**: / Did I say 'better'?" — Brutus: "If you did, I care not."
- Cassius: "When Caesar lived, he durst not thus have moved me." — Brutus: "**you durst not so have tempted him**."
- Cassius: "Do not presume too much upon my love; / I may do that I shall be sorry for." — Brutus: "You have done that you should be sorry for. / **There is no terror, Cassius, in your threats, / For I am arm'd so strong in honesty / That they pass by me as the idle wind.**"

#### 10. "I did send to you for certain sums of gold"

Now Brutus reaches his real grievance:

> "I did send to you
> **For certain sums of gold, which you denied me;**
> For I can raise no money by vile means:
> By heaven, I had rather coin my heart,
> And drop my blood for drachmas, than to wring
> From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash
> By any indirection."

Brutus asked Cassius for **gold to pay his legions**, and Cassius refused. Brutus himself **cannot raise money by dishonest means** — he would rather turn his heart into coins and his blood into drachmas than squeeze money from poor peasants by crooked methods. *(2025 specimen Q3.)*

Here is the **hypocrisy** that the 2027 specimen asks about: Brutus has just condemned Cassius for raising money by bribes, yet he is angry that Cassius did not **send him some of that money**. He wants clean hands while living on money raised by other people's unclean ones. *(2027 specimen Q1(iii).)*

Cassius protests, "**I denied you not.** … he was but a **fool** that brought / My answer back." He is deeply hurt: "**Brutus hath rived my heart: / A friend should bear his friend's infirmities, / But Brutus makes mine greater than they are.**" Brutus: "I do not like your faults." Cassius: "A friendly eye could never see such faults." Brutus: "A flatterer's would not, though they do appear / As huge as high Olympus."

#### 11. "Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come"

Cassius breaks down:

> "**Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come,
> Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius,**
> For Cassius is aweary of the world;
> Hated by one he loves; braved by his brother;
> Check'd like a bondman; all his faults observed,
> Set in a note-book, learn'd, and conn'd by rote,
> To cast into my teeth."

He is so **frustrated** and hurt that he invites his enemies to take their revenge on him alone. *(2027 specimen Q1(vi).)* He offers Brutus his dagger and his "naked breast": "**Strike, as thou didst at Caesar**; for, I know, / When thou didst hate him worst, thou lovedst him better / Than ever thou lovedst Cassius."

#### 12. Reconciliation

Brutus is moved: "Sheathe your dagger." He admits his own anger is like fire in a **flint** — "**yoked with a lamb / That carries anger as the flint bears fire**; / Who, much enforced, shows a hasty spark, / And straight is cold again." Cassius asks whether he has lived only to be mocked by Brutus; Brutus admits, "When I spoke that, I was ill-temper'd too." "**Give me your hand.**" "**And my heart too.**" Cassius asks Brutus to bear with the "rash humour which my mother gave me"; Brutus promises that when Cassius is too sharp, "He'll think your mother chides, and leave you so."

#### 13. The intruding Poet

A **Poet** forces his way past the guards, crying that the generals should "Love, and be friends, as two such men should be; / For I have seen more years, I'm sure, than ye." Cassius laughs ("how vilely doth this cynic rhyme!"), but Brutus angrily drives him out: "What should the wars do with these **jigging fools**?" The comic interruption relieves the tension.

#### 14. "Portia is dead"

Brutus calls for wine. Cassius says, "I did not think you could have been so angry." Brutus: "**O Cassius, I am sick of many griefs.**" Cassius reminds him of his **Stoic philosophy** (to bear misfortune calmly). Brutus: "**No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead.**"

Cassius is shocked — "How 'scaped I killing when I cross'd you so?" Brutus explains briefly: Portia, unable to bear his absence and grieving that Octavius and Antony had grown so strong, became distracted and, while her attendants were absent, "**swallow'd fire**" (burning coals). Brutus says, "Speak no more of her. Give me a bowl of wine. / **In this I bury all unkindness, Cassius.**" Cassius: "My heart is thirsty for that noble pledge."

The quarrel is now explained: Brutus had been carrying this private grief all along.

#### 15. News from Rome: the proscriptions and Philippi

**Titinius** and **Messala** arrive. Letters report that **Octavius and Antony** are marching with a mighty army towards **Philippi**. By **proscription**, the triumvirs have put to death **a hundred senators** (Messala's letters; Brutus's letters say seventy), among them **Cicero**.

Messala asks whether Brutus has heard from Portia; Brutus says he has not, and Messala tells him of her death. Brutus answers calmly: "**Why, farewell, Portia. We must die, Messala: / With meditating that she must die once, / I have the patience to endure it now.**" Messala admires him: "Even so great men great losses should endure." Cassius whispers that he knows the theory as well as Brutus, "But yet my nature could not bear it so." *(Many editors think Shakespeare's text keeps two versions of the news; others read Brutus's reply to Messala as a deliberate show of Stoic calm before his officers.)*

#### 16. The war council: "There is a tide in the affairs of men"

Brutus proposes marching **at once to Philippi**. Cassius disagrees:

> "'Tis better that the enemy seek us:
> So shall he waste his means, weary his soldiers,
> Doing himself offence; whilst we, lying still,
> Are full of rest, defence, and nimbleness."

Cassius's sound military plan is to **stay where they are** and let the enemy wear itself out marching to them. *(2025 specimen Q1(v).)*

Brutus overrules him. The people between Sardis and Philippi support them only by force and give unwillingly; the enemy, marching through them, would gather new soldiers and come "refresh'd, new-added, and encouraged." Their own legions are "brim-full", their cause "ripe"; the enemy grows every day, while they, at their height, can only decline:

> "**There is a tide in the affairs of men,
> Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune;
> Omitted, all the voyage of their life
> Is bound in shallows and in miseries.**
> On such a full sea are we now afloat;
> And we must take the current when it serves,
> Or lose our ventures."

Life is like the sea: there is a moment (the high tide) which, if seized, carries a man to success; if missed, his whole life is stuck in shallow water and misery. Cassius gives way once more: "Then, with your will, go on; / We'll along ourselves, and meet them at Philippi." This is Brutus's **last and fatal mistake**: every time he has overruled Cassius — sparing Antony, letting Antony speak, now marching to Philippi — he has been wrong.

They part as friends: "**Never come such division 'tween our souls!**" "Every thing is well." "Good night, good brother."

#### 17. Lucius, the music, and the Ghost

Brutus asks **Varro and Claudius** to sleep in his tent. He finds the book he had been looking for in his gown pocket and asks his young servant **Lucius** to play music. Lucius plays and falls asleep over his instrument; Brutus gently removes it so it will not break: "Gentle knave, good night; / I will not do thee so much wrong to wake thee." This small scene shows Brutus's **gentleness and kindness** to his servants.

As Brutus reads, the candle burns dim and the **Ghost of Caesar** appears:

> **Brutus:** Art thou some god, some angel, or some devil,
> That makest my blood cold and my hair to stare?
> Speak to me what thou art.
> **Ghost:** **Thy evil spirit, Brutus.**
> **Brutus:** Why comest thou?
> **Ghost:** **To tell thee thou shalt see me at Philippi.**
> **Brutus:** Well; then I shall see thee again?
> **Ghost:** Ay, at Philippi.
> **Brutus:** Why, I will see thee at Philippi, then.

The Ghost vanishes. Brutus wakes Lucius ("The strings, my lord, are false"), Varro and Claudius; none of them saw anything. Brutus sends word to Cassius to move his forces early. The Ghost is **Caesar's spirit "ranging for revenge"**, as Antony prophesied, and it **foreshadows Brutus's death at Philippi**.

### Conclusion: why this act matters

- It shows that the **civil war** Antony prophesied has come: the triumvirs coldly condemn their own relatives; a hundred senators die.
- It shows the **dark side of Antony**: cutting the people's legacies, despising Lepidus as a "property".
- The **quarrel scene** shows the friendship of Brutus and Cassius under strain, their different natures, Brutus's idealism and unconscious hypocrisy, and their touching reconciliation.
- **Portia's death** reveals Brutus's private suffering and Stoic self-control.
- The decision to march to **Philippi** is Brutus's final error; the **Ghost** foretells his doom.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The proscription (IV.1)

- **What happens:** Antony, Octavius and Lepidus mark the names of men to be killed; each gives up a relative.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**These many, then, shall die; their names are prick'd.**" — *prick'd* = marked with a pin-prick on a list.
  - "**He shall not live; look, with a spot I damn him.**" — Antony condemns his own nephew Publius with a mark of the pen.
  - "**How to cut off some charge in legacies**" — reduce the money Caesar left the people; the will was only a weapon for Antony.
- **Exam focus:** contrast with Antony's attitude in the Forum.

### Section 2 — Antony on Lepidus (IV.1)

- **What happens:** Antony belittles Lepidus as soon as he leaves; Octavius defends him mildly.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**This is a slight unmeritable man, / Meet to be sent on errands**" — insignificant, without merit, fit only to run errands. *(2025 specimen Q1(iv))*
  - "**He shall but bear them as the ass bears gold**" — simile; Lepidus will carry the blame and burden for them and then be turned out to graze.
  - "**So is my horse, Octavius**" — Antony compares Lepidus to his horse: trained, fed, useful, but without a will of its own.
  - "**He must be taught, and train'd, and bid go forth; / A barren-spirited fellow**" — without original ideas or spirit. *(2027 specimen Q1(iv))*
  - "**do not talk of him, / But as a property**" — think of him only as a tool.
  - "**we are at the stake, / And bay'd about with many enemies**" — imagery of bear-baiting: a bear tied to a stake and surrounded by barking dogs.

### Section 3 — Before the tent (IV.2)

- **What happens:** Lucilius reports Cassius's cooler manner; Cassius arrives with a complaint; they go inside to argue.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**A hot friend cooling**" — a friendship losing its warmth.
  - "**When love begins to sicken and decay, / It useth an enforced ceremony**" — failing friendship hides behind forced politeness.
  - "**hollow men, like horses hot at hand … like deceitful jades, / Sink in the trial**" — simile; false friends make a fine show but fail when tested.
  - "**Most noble brother, you have done me wrong.**" — Cassius's opening complaint.
  - "**Before the eyes of both our armies here … Let us not wrangle**" — Brutus's concern for appearances and discipline.

### Section 4 — The accusations (IV.3)

- **What happens:** Cassius complains about Lucius Pella; Brutus accuses Cassius of an itching palm; Brutus recalls the Ides of March.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**You have condemn'd and noted Lucius Pella / For taking bribes here of the Sardians**" — *noted* = publicly disgraced. *(2025 specimen Q3(iv))*
  - "**every nice offence should bear his comment**" — *nice* = trivial; Cassius thinks small faults should be overlooked in war.
  - "**you yourself / Are much condemn'd to have an itching palm**" — a greedy hand, always wanting money.
  - "**Remember March, the ides of March remember: / Did not great Julius bleed for justice' sake?**" — Brutus holds on to the ideal that justified the assassination.
  - "**I had rather be a dog, and bay the moon, / Than such a Roman.**" — he would rather be a howling dog than a corrupt Roman.

### Section 5 — The heat of the quarrel (IV.3)

- **What happens:** insults and threats are exchanged; Brutus is scornful, Cassius wounded.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**I am a soldier, I, / Older in practice, abler than yourself / To make conditions.**" — Cassius claims more military experience.
  - "**I said, an elder soldier, not a better**" — Cassius backs down on the word.
  - "**There is no terror, Cassius, in your threats, / For I am arm'd so strong in honesty / That they pass by me as the idle wind**" — Brutus's pride in his honesty; simile.

### Section 6 — The gold (IV.3)

- **What happens:** Brutus complains that Cassius refused him gold to pay his legions.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**I can raise no money by vile means**" — Brutus will not raise money dishonestly. *(2025 specimen Q3(iii))*
  - "**I had rather coin my heart, / And drop my blood for drachmas, than to wring / From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash / By any indirection**" — vivid metaphor; *vile trash* = money; *indirection* = crooked means.
  - "**he was but a fool that brought / My answer back**" — Cassius blames the messenger. *(2025 specimen Q3(ii))*
  - "**A friend should bear his friend's infirmities**" — friends should tolerate each other's weaknesses.
  - "**A flatterer's would not, though they do appear / As huge as high Olympus**" — hyperbole.
- **Exam focus:** Brutus's hypocrisy (2027 specimen Q1(iii)).

### Section 7 — Cassius's outburst and the reconciliation (IV.3)

- **What happens:** Cassius invites death; Brutus softens; they shake hands.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come, / Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius**" — frustration and despair. *(2027 specimen Q1(vi))*
  - "**Hated by one he loves; braved by his brother; / Check'd like a bondman**" — defied and scolded like a slave.
  - "**Strike, as thou didst at Caesar**" — emotional blackmail; he reminds Brutus that he loved Caesar more.
  - "**yoked with a lamb / That carries anger as the flint bears fire**" — Brutus's anger is like a spark from flint: quick, then cold.
  - "**Give me your hand. — And my heart too.**" — the reconciliation.

### Section 8 — Portia, Philippi and the Ghost (IV.3)

- **What happens:** Brutus reveals Portia's death; news of the proscriptions and the enemy's march; the council of war; the Ghost.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead.**" — Stoic restraint hides deep grief.
  - "**In this I bury all unkindness, Cassius.**" — the bowl of wine seals their friendship.
  - "**'Tis better that the enemy seek us**" — Cassius's plan. *(2025 specimen Q1(v))*
  - "**There is a tide in the affairs of men, / Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune**" — extended metaphor of the sea and tides.
  - "**Thy evil spirit, Brutus.**" / "**thou shalt see me at Philippi**" — the Ghost's prophecy.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- The Second Triumvirate of Antony, Octavius and Lepidus was formed in 43 BC; their proscriptions killed many senators and wealthy citizens, including the great orator **Cicero**.
- **Sardis** was the old capital of Lydia, in present-day western Turkey. **Philippi** was a city in Macedonia, in present-day northern Greece.
- Shakespeare compresses events: historically, more than a year separates Caesar's funeral from the battles of Philippi (42 BC).
- "There is a tide in the affairs of men" is often quoted to mean that opportunities must be seized when they come.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **The cost of civil war:** Antony's prophecy of "fierce civil strife" comes true; a hundred senators die by proscription.
- **Power corrupts:** the triumvirs bargain over lives and plan to cheat the people of Caesar's gifts.
- **Friendship under strain:** the quarrel tests the bond between Brutus and Cassius; love wins, but the cracks remain.
- **Idealism and practicality:** Brutus's ideals clash with the practical needs of war (money, strategy); Cassius is practical but morally flexible.
- **Private grief behind public faces:** Brutus hides Portia's death behind Stoic calm.
- **Fate and the supernatural:** the Ghost of Caesar signals that Caesar's spirit will have its revenge.

### 7.1 The stages of the quarrel

| Stage | What happens | Mood |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Complaint | Cassius: Lucius Pella condemned, his letters ignored | grievance |
| 2. Counter-charge | Brutus: Cassius has "an itching palm" and sells offices | anger |
| 3. Principle | Brutus: "Remember March"; "bay the moon" | moral scorn |
| 4. Insults | "slight man"; "rash choler"; "elder soldier, not a better" | personal, bitter |
| 5. Real grievance | Brutus: the gold denied for his legions | hurt pride |
| 6. Climax | Cassius: "Come, Antony …"; offers his dagger and heart | despair |
| 7. Reconciliation | the lamb and the flint; "Give me your hand." "And my heart too." | love restored |
| 8. Explanation | "Portia is dead"; the bowl of wine | grief, tenderness |

### 7.2 Irony in the act

1. Antony, who stirred the people with Caesar's will, now plans to **cut their legacies**.
2. The triumvirs, who called the conspirators murderers, condemn a hundred senators.
3. Brutus condemns bribery but is angry that Cassius did not send him the money (2027 specimen Q1(iii)).
4. Brutus, who says "There is no terror … in your threats", has just been the more aggressive of the two.
5. Brutus claims "No man bears sorrow better" — yet his sorrow explains his unusual anger.

### 7.3 Foreshadowing

- "We are at the stake, / And bay'd about with many enemies" — the coming war.
- Brutus's decision to march — the defeat at Philippi.
- "Never come such division 'tween our souls!" — their final parting in Act V.
- The Ghost: "thou shalt see me at Philippi" — Brutus's death in Act V.

**2025 specimen Q1(iv).** **(a) Lepidus.**

**2025 Q1(iv) / 2027 specimen Q1(iv).** **(d) Lepidus.**

**2025 specimen Q3.**

**(i) To whom are these words addressed? Why is Brutus angry with this person?** [3]
The words are addressed to **Cassius**. Brutus is angry because Cassius **refused to send him the gold** he had asked for — money Brutus needed to pay his legions. (Earlier, Brutus had also accused Cassius of having "an itching palm".)

**(ii) How does this person feel when he hears these words? What does he say to defend himself?** [3]
Cassius feels **hurt, grieved and betrayed** — "Brutus hath rived my heart." He defends himself by saying, "**I denied you not**": the messenger who brought back his answer was **a fool** — the message was wrongly delivered.

**(iii) Why did Brutus need 'certain sums of gold'? Why was he unable to raise these sums of money?** [3]
He needed the gold **to pay his legions** (his soldiers). He could not raise it himself because he **will not raise money by vile (dishonest) means** — he would rather "coin my heart" than **wring money from the hard hands of peasants** by crooked methods.

**(iv) Earlier in this scene, Brutus refers to Lucius Pella. What had he been accused of? Who had supported him and how?** [3]
Lucius Pella had been accused of **taking bribes from the Sardians**, and Brutus had **condemned and disgraced** him. **Cassius** had supported him by **writing letters** to Brutus pleading on his behalf, because he knew the man; Brutus ignored the letters.

**(v) Mention any two aspects of Brutus' character that are revealed in the above extract. What do you understand about the relationship between Brutus and the person he addresses?** [4]
**Character:** (1) Brutus is **idealistic and self-righteous** — proud of his honesty, he refuses to raise money by unjust means. (2) He shows **unconscious hypocrisy**: he condemns Cassius's methods of raising money, yet expects Cassius to send him money raised in exactly those ways. **Relationship:** the friendship between Brutus and Cassius is **strained and breaking down** — they quarrel bitterly, exchange insults and accusations, and only reconcile after Cassius offers his dagger and Brutus reveals Portia's death.

**2026 Q1(iv).** **(a) ruthless.** Coldly, the triumvirs mark their enemies for death — even Antony's own nephew.

**2026 Q1(v).** **(d) dismissive.** Brutus brushes Cassius aside as a man of no worth.

**2026 Improvement Q1(v).** **(d) Both 1 and 3.** "I an itching palm!" — he can hardly believe the charge, and angrily warns that from anyone but Brutus that speech would be his last.

**2026 Improvement Q1(vi).** **(a) Cassius.** Hearing from Lucilius how coldly Cassius received him, Brutus says, "Thou hast described / A hot friend cooling."

**2025 Q1(v).** **(c) Mark Antony, Octavius Caesar, Lepidus.** They meet in Act IV, Scene 1 to draw up the list of those who must die. (Option (a) is the First Triumvirate.)

**2025 Q1(vi).** **(d) frustrated.** In the quarrel scene Cassius feels that Brutus, his friend, now hates and scorns him, and in his frustration he offers his own life.

**2025 Improvement Q1(iv).** **(b) humiliating Lucius Pella.** "You have condemn'd and noted Lucius Pella / For taking bribes here of the Sardians." (It is Brutus who then accuses Cassius of selling offices.)

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> **Antony:** Octavius, I have seen more days than you:
> And though we lay these honours on this man,
> To ease ourselves of divers slanderous loads,
> He shall but bear them as the ass bears gold,
> To groan and sweat under the business,
> Either led or driven, as we point the way.

**(i)** Who is "this man"? Where are the speakers, and what have they been doing? [3]
**Answer:** "This man" is **Lepidus**, the third triumvir. Antony and Octavius are at a **house in Rome**, where the triumvirs have been **pricking the names of those to be put to death** (the proscription).

**(ii)** Where has Lepidus been sent, and why? [3]
**Answer:** To **Caesar's house** to fetch **Caesar's will**, so that the triumvirs can decide how to **cut down the legacies** Caesar left to the people.

**(iii)** Explain "He shall but bear them as the ass bears gold". [3]
**Answer:** Lepidus will carry the honours — and the blame for their harsh actions — as a donkey carries gold: he will **bear the burden but not enjoy the treasure**, being led or driven wherever they choose, and then turned out to graze when no longer needed.

**(iv)** How does Octavius defend Lepidus, and how does Antony reply? [3]
**Answer:** Octavius says Lepidus is "**a tried and valiant soldier**". Antony replies, "**So is my horse**": he feeds and trains his horse to fight and obey; Lepidus is no different — "a barren-spirited fellow", to be talked of only "as a property".

**(v)** What does this scene reveal about Antony? How is it different from the Antony of Act III? [4]
**Answer:** It reveals him as **ruthless, cynical and contemptuous**: he condemns his own nephew, plans to cut the people's legacies, and treats his partner as a beast of burden. In Act III he appeared as Caesar's grieving, loyal friend and the people's champion; now he is a cold **power-seeker** who uses people and discards them.

**Extract B.**
> **Brutus:** There is a tide in the affairs of men,
> Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune;
> Omitted, all the voyage of their life
> Is bound in shallows and in miseries.

**(i)** What decision are Brutus and Cassius discussing? [3]
**Answer:** Whether to **march to Philippi** to meet the armies of Octavius and Antony, or to wait where they are near Sardis. Brutus wants to march; Cassius wants to stay.

**(ii)** What is Cassius's plan, and why does he prefer it? [3]
**Answer:** "'Tis better that the enemy seek us": the enemy would **waste its supplies and tire its soldiers** marching to them, while their own army, resting, would be fresh, ready to defend and quick to act.

**(iii)** What reasons does Brutus give for marching? [3]
**Answer:** The people between Sardis and Philippi support them **only by force** and would **join the enemy**, who would come "refresh'd, new-added, and encouraged"; their own legions are **brim-full** and their cause ripe, while the enemy grows every day; they are at their height and can only **decline**.

**(iv)** Explain the lines in your own words. [3]
**Answer:** In human affairs, as at sea, there is a **high tide**: if a man seizes it, it carries him to success; if he misses it, the rest of his life's voyage is stuck in **shallow water and misery**. Opportunities must be taken when they come.

**(v)** Who wins the argument? Why is the decision a mistake? What happens later that night? [4]
**Answer:** **Brutus** wins; Cassius gives way ("Then, with your will, go on"). It is a mistake because Cassius's plan was **militarily sounder**; Brutus, a poor judge of practical matters, has overruled Cassius again, as he did over Antony. That night the **Ghost of Caesar** appears to Brutus as his "evil spirit" and says, "**thou shalt see me at Philippi**" — foreshadowing his defeat and death.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** What are the triumvirs doing at the beginning of Act IV? How do they treat their own relatives?
**Answer:** They are pricking the names of those to be put to death (a proscription).
Lepidus agrees to his brother's death.
Antony condemns his own nephew Publius: "with a spot I damn him."

**Q2.** Why is Lepidus sent to Caesar's house? What does this show about Antony?
**Answer:** To fetch Caesar's will.
Antony wants to cut down the legacies left to the people.
It shows Antony is cynical; he used the will in the Forum only to win the crowd.

**Q3.** How does Antony describe Lepidus? *(2025 and 2027 specimens)*
**Answer:** "A slight unmeritable man, / Meet to be sent on errands."
Like an ass bearing gold, or his own horse, taught and trained to obey.
"A barren-spirited fellow", to be talked of only "as a property".

**Q4.** What does Brutus mean by "a hot friend cooling"?
**Answer:** Lucilius reports that Cassius received him politely but without the old warmth.
Brutus says that friendship, when it begins to decay, uses forced politeness.
He senses that Cassius's friendship is cooling.

**Q5.** Why was Cassius angry with Brutus at the start of the quarrel? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** Brutus had condemned and disgraced Lucius Pella for taking bribes from the Sardians.
Cassius had written letters pleading for Pella.
Brutus had ignored those letters.

**Q6.** What does Brutus accuse Cassius of? How does Cassius react?
**Answer:** Of having "an itching palm".
And of selling offices for gold to undeserving men.
Cassius is outraged: if anyone but Brutus said so, it would be his last speech.

**Q7.** Explain "Remember March, the ides of March remember".
**Answer:** Brutus reminds Cassius that they killed Caesar for justice.
They struck "the foremost man of all this world" partly for supporting robbers.
They must not now dirty their own hands with bribes.

**Q8.** Why did Brutus need gold, and why could he not raise it himself? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** He needed it to pay his legions.
He will not raise money by vile means.
He would rather "coin my heart" than wring money from peasants by "indirection".

**Q9.** How does Cassius defend himself against the charge of denying Brutus gold? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** "I denied you not."
The messenger who brought back his answer was a fool.
He says Brutus has "rived" his heart and makes his faults greater than they are.

**Q10.** What does Cassius offer Brutus in his outburst, and why?
**Answer:** His dagger and his "naked breast" — "Strike, as thou didst at Caesar."
He feels hated by the friend he loves and treated like a slave.
He believes Brutus loved even Caesar more than him.

**Q11.** How did Portia die? How does Brutus speak of her death?
**Answer:** Distracted by Brutus's absence and the growing power of Octavius and Antony, she swallowed fire.
Brutus says, "No man bears sorrow better. Portia is dead."
He asks Cassius to speak no more of her.

**Q12.** What news do Titinius and Messala bring?
**Answer:** Octavius and Antony are marching with a mighty army towards Philippi.
The triumvirs have put to death a hundred senators (seventy, by Brutus's letters).
Cicero is among the dead.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** What aspects of Brutus's character are revealed in the quarrel scene? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** He is idealistic and proud of his honesty.
He is self-righteous and unconsciously hypocritical about the gold.
He is harsh and scornful in anger, but quickly forgiving.
He shows Stoic self-control in hiding his grief for Portia.

**Q14.** What does the quarrel reveal about the relationship between Brutus and Cassius? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** Their friendship is strained and close to breaking.
Cassius loves Brutus deeply and is easily hurt.
Brutus respects Cassius but does not fully trust his methods.
The reconciliation shows that their bond survives, though the cracks remain.

**Q15.** Compare Cassius's plan with Brutus's plan before Philippi. Who was right?
**Answer:** Cassius: stay put and let the enemy tire itself marching to them.
Brutus: march at once, as the local people would join the enemy and the enemy grows daily.
Brutus wins: "There is a tide in the affairs of men."
Cassius was militarily right; Brutus's decision leads to defeat.

**Q16.** Explain "There is a tide in the affairs of men … Or lose our ventures."
**Answer:** Life is like a sea voyage with a high tide.
Taken at the flood, the tide carries a man to fortune.
Missed, it leaves his life stuck in shallows and misery.
Brutus means they must strike now, at their strongest, or lose everything.

**Q17.** Describe the appearance of the Ghost and its significance.
**Answer:** As Brutus reads by a dim taper, the Ghost of Caesar appears.
It calls itself Brutus's "evil spirit" and says he will see it at Philippi.
Brutus bravely answers, "I will see thee at Philippi, then."
It is Caesar's spirit "ranging for revenge" and foreshadows Brutus's death.

**Q18.** How does Act IV, Scene 1 change our view of Antony?
**Answer:** In Act III he seemed a loyal, grieving friend of Caesar.
In Act IV he condemns his own nephew to death.
He plans to cut the people's legacies.
He despises his partner Lepidus as "a property" — a ruthless power-seeker.

**Q19.** "Brutus is a gentle and humane man even in the midst of war." Support this from the end of Act IV.
**Answer:** He asks Varro and Claudius to sleep in his tent rather than stand on watch.
He apologises to the sleepy Lucius for keeping him up.
When Lucius falls asleep playing, he will not wake him.
He removes the instrument so that it will not break.

**Q20.** How does the news of Portia's death explain Brutus's behaviour in the quarrel?
**Answer:** Brutus was unusually harsh and scornful during the quarrel.
He was carrying a deep private grief.
He says he is "sick of many griefs".
His anger was partly grief turned outward; when he confesses it, Cassius understands.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (16 marks each)

### Extract-based questions (16 marks each)

**Q1.** Read the extract and answer. **Cassius:** "That you have wrong'd me doth appear in this: / You have condemn'd and noted Lucius Pella / For taking bribes here of the Sardians; / Wherein my letters, praying on his side, / Because I knew the man, were slighted off." (i) Where are the speakers? Why have they gone inside? [3] (ii) What is Brutus's reply to this complaint? [3] (iii) Of what does Brutus accuse Cassius in return? [3] (iv) Explain "Remember March, the ides of March remember". [3] (v) Compare the attitudes of Brutus and Cassius to bribery as shown here. [4]
**Answer:** (i) In Brutus's tent, in his camp near Sardis; Brutus insisted that they must not quarrel before their armies, which should see nothing but love between them.
(ii) Brutus says that Cassius "wronged" himself by writing on behalf of such a man.
(iii) That Cassius himself is condemned for having "an itching palm" — greed for money — and for selling offices for gold to undeserving men.
(iv) Brutus reminds Cassius that they killed Caesar on the Ides of March for the sake of justice; having struck down "the foremost man of all this world" for supporting robbers, they cannot now dirty their own hands with bribes; he would "rather be a dog, and bay the moon, / Than such a Roman".
(v) Cassius is practical: in wartime, small faults ("every nice offence") should be overlooked, and friends protected; Brutus is idealistic and strict: their cause must stay pure, or the assassination loses its justification; yet Brutus also expects Cassius to supply money raised by such means — an unconscious hypocrisy.

**Q2.** Read the extract and answer. **Cassius:** "Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come, / Revenge yourselves alone on Cassius, / For Cassius is aweary of the world; / Hated by one he loves; braved by his brother; / Check'd like a bondman." (i) What has Brutus just said that leads to this outburst? [3] (ii) Which word best describes Cassius's state of mind? Explain. [3] (iii) What does Cassius offer Brutus next, and what does he say about Caesar? [3] (iv) How does Brutus respond? [3] (v) How is the quarrel finally ended, and what news explains Brutus's behaviour? [4]
**Answer:** (i) Brutus has said he does not like Cassius's faults; when Cassius says a friendly eye would never see such faults, Brutus replies that a flatterer's would not, "though they do appear / As huge as high Olympus".
(ii) Frustrated (2027 specimen Q1(vi)): Cassius feels hated by the friend he loves, defied by his brother, scolded like a slave, his faults noted and learnt by heart to be thrown in his face — he is weary of life.
(iii) His dagger and his naked breast, with a heart "richer than gold": "Strike, as thou didst at Caesar; for, I know, / When thou didst hate him worst, thou lovedst him better / Than ever thou lovedst Cassius."
(iv) He tells Cassius to sheathe his dagger and admits he is "yoked with a lamb / That carries anger as the flint bears fire" — quick to spark, quick to cool; he admits he too was ill-tempered.
(v) They shake hands — "Give me your hand." "And my heart too." — and later pledge friendship in a bowl of wine; Brutus reveals that Portia is dead, having swallowed fire, which explains why he was so unusually angry.

**Q3.** Read the extract and answer. **Brutus:** "How ill this taper burns! Ha! who comes here? / I think it is the weakness of mine eyes / That shapes this monstrous apparition. / It comes upon me. Art thou any thing? / Art thou some god, some angel, or some devil, / That makest my blood cold and my hair to stare?" (i) Where is Brutus, and what was he doing just before? [3] (ii) What does the apparition say it is, and why has it come? [3] (iii) How does Brutus react to the apparition's message? [3] (iv) What does Brutus do after it vanishes, and what do the others say? [3] (v) What is the dramatic significance of this appearance? [4]
**Answer:** (i) In his tent near Sardis, at night; he had been reading a book after Lucius fell asleep playing music, and Varro and Claudius were asleep in the tent.
(ii) It is the Ghost of Caesar; it says it is "Thy evil spirit, Brutus", and has come "To tell thee thou shalt see me at Philippi."
(iii) He is shaken but brave: "Well; then I shall see thee again?" — "Ay, at Philippi." — "Why, I will see thee at Philippi, then."
(iv) He wakes Lucius, Varro and Claudius and asks if they saw or cried out at anything; Lucius says only "The strings, my lord, are false", and none saw anything; Brutus sends word to Cassius to set on his forces early.
(v) It shows Caesar's spirit "ranging for revenge", as Antony prophesied; it foreshadows Brutus's defeat and death at Philippi; it adds supernatural fear and suggests that fate is against the conspirators; it may also reflect Brutus's own guilty conscience.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Friendship)** Cassius says, "A friend should bear his friend's infirmities." Do you agree? How should friends deal with each other's faults?
**Model answer:** Yes, within limits: true friends accept small weaknesses, but they should also speak honestly — kindly and in private — about serious faults, as Brutus tried (too harshly) to do.

**Q2. (Decision-making)** Brutus says opportunities must be "taken at the flood". When can acting quickly be a mistake? Use the play.
**Model answer:** When a careful, patient plan is better: Cassius's plan to let the enemy tire itself was wiser; acting too fast without weighing advice led Brutus to defeat.

**Q3. (Spot the error)** "Cassius wanted to march to Philippi, but Brutus persuaded him to wait." Correct this.
**Model answer:** Cassius wanted to wait near Sardis; Brutus insisted on marching to Philippi, and Cassius gave way.

**Q4. (Leadership)** How does Antony's treatment of Lepidus show a poor way of leading a team?
**Model answer:** He uses Lepidus for dirty work and plans to discard him, calling him "a property"; good leaders respect partners, share credit fairly and do not make others carry the blame.

**Q5. (Values)** Brutus will not "wring / From the hard hands of peasants their vile trash". Is it enough to keep one's own hands clean while benefiting from others' wrongdoing? Discuss briefly.
**Model answer:** No; accepting the benefits of someone else's dishonesty is also wrong — Brutus's demand for Cassius's money makes his honesty incomplete.

## 13. Assertion–Reason Questions

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) A is true, but R is false.
- (b) A is false, but R is true.
- (c) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each extract and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — The triumvirs

> **Antony:** These many, then, shall die; their names are prick'd.
> **Octavius:** Your brother too must die; consent you, Lepidus?

**(i)** Where are the speakers?
**Answer:** In a house in Rome (Antony's).

**(ii)** What does "prick'd" mean?
**Answer:** Marked on a list (of those to die).

**(iii)** On what condition does Lepidus consent?
**Answer:** That Antony's nephew Publius also dies.

**(iv)** Why is Lepidus then sent to Caesar's house?
**Answer:** To fetch Caesar's will.

### Case 2 — The gold

> Brutus reminds Cassius that he had asked for gold and been refused. He says he cannot raise money by dishonest means, and would sooner turn his own heart into coin than squeeze it out of poor farmers.

**(i)** Why did Brutus need the gold?
**Answer:** To pay his legions.

**(ii)** What is Cassius's defence?
**Answer:** The messenger was a fool; he did not refuse.

**(iii)** Which word, from the 2027 specimen, describes Brutus's attitude here?
**Answer:** Hypocritical.

**(iv)** Whom had Brutus condemned earlier for taking bribes?
**Answer:** Lucius Pella.

### Case 3 — The tide

> **Brutus:** There is a tide in the affairs of men,

**(i)** What plan had Cassius proposed?
**Answer:** To stay where they were and let the enemy come to them.

**(ii)** Where does Brutus want to march?
**Answer:** To Philippi.

**(iii)** What figure of speech is used here?
**Answer:** An extended metaphor.

**(iv)** Who appears to Brutus later that night?
**Answer:** The Ghost of Caesar.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Act at a glance
- **IV.1 (Rome):** the triumvirs prick names; Lepidus's brother and Antony's nephew Publius condemned; Lepidus sent for the will "to cut off some charge in legacies"; "a slight unmeritable man, / Meet to be sent on errands"; "as the ass bears gold"; "So is my horse"; "a barren-spirited fellow"; "a property"; Brutus and Cassius levying powers; "at the stake, / And bay'd about".
- **IV.2 (near Sardis):** "a hot friend cooling"; "enforced ceremony"; "hollow men … like deceitful jades"; "Most noble brother, you have done me wrong"; into the tent.
- **IV.3 (the tent):** Lucius Pella and the Sardians; "an itching palm"; "Remember March"; "bay the moon"; "elder soldier, not a better"; "arm'd so strong in honesty"; the gold for his legions — "coin my heart"; "I denied you not" — the messenger a fool; "Come, Antony, and young Octavius, come"; "Strike, as thou didst at Caesar"; the lamb and the flint; "Give me your hand." "And my heart too."; the Poet; "Portia is dead" — swallowed fire; wine; Messala: a hundred senators, Cicero dead, enemy to Philippi; Cassius: wait; Brutus: "There is a tide …"; Lucius's music; the Ghost: "thou shalt see me at Philippi".

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| "slight unmeritable man" = Octavius | It is Lepidus (2025 specimen). |
| Cassius wanted to march to Philippi | Cassius wanted to wait; Brutus wanted to march. |
| Cassius admitted refusing the gold | He blamed the messenger: "I denied you not." |
| Lucius Pella was Brutus's friend | He was defended by Cassius and condemned by Brutus. |
| Portia was killed by soldiers | She swallowed fire, in grief and distraction. |
| Everyone saw the Ghost | Only Brutus saw it. |
