## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*When Great Trees Fall* is a poem about **grief** by the American poet **Maya Angelou**. It begins with a scene from **nature** — an ancient, mighty tree crashing down in a forest, and the whole forest shaken by its fall. Then it turns to its real subject: what happens to **us** when a **great soul dies** — a person of great goodness or importance who has shaped our lives.

The poem describes grief honestly. First comes **shock**; then a painful, sharpened awareness and **regret** for things left unsaid and undone; then a deep sense of **emptiness**, as though part of ourselves had gone with the person. But the poem does not end in darkness. **Peace returns**, slowly and unevenly. We are changed, yet comforted by one thought: that person truly lived — and because they lived, we can go on and **become better**.

### About the poet

**Maya Angelou** (1928–2014), born Marguerite Annie Johnson in St. Louis, Missouri, was an American poet, memoirist, performer and **civil rights activist** who worked alongside Martin Luther King Jr. and Malcolm X. Her autobiography ***I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings*** (1969) made her famous, and poems such as *Still I Rise* and *Phenomenal Woman* are widely loved. She read her poem *On the Pulse of Morning* at President Bill Clinton's inauguration in 1993 and received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2011. *When Great Trees Fall* appeared in her collection *I Shall Not Be Moved* (1990).

### The poem's ideas, stanza by stanza

#### Stanzas 1 and 2 — Nature is shaken

The poem opens with the fall of a great tree. Its effect spreads far beyond the spot where it falls: the landscape itself seems to tremble, and **even the strongest animals are unsettled** — lions crouch low ("hunker down") and elephants move heavily away in search of safety. The **smaller creatures** are hit hardest: they shrink back into stunned stillness ("recoil into silence"), too shaken even to feel afraid. *(2025 specimen Q7(i); the specimen extract is from stanza 2.)*

**Idea:** the first stage of grief is **shock and numbness**. A great loss disturbs the strong and silences the weak.

#### Stanza 3 — When great souls die

Now the poet makes the comparison plain: the fall of great trees is like the **death of great souls**. In the first moments after such a death, those left behind find it **hard even to breathe** — grief seems to thin the very air. For a short while they see things with a painfully sharp clearness ("hurtful clarity"), and **memory** keeps returning to the loving things they never said ("kind words unsaid") and the plans they promised but never carried out. *(2025 specimen Q7(ii).)*

**Idea:** the second stage is **pain and regret** — awareness of missed chances to show love.

#### Stanza 4 — Emptiness

The poem then describes a deeper loss. The great soul had been so bound up with our lives that, when they die, our **sense of reality** seems to leave us. The parts of us that **grew because of them** — our inner life, our understanding — seem to **shrink and fade**. We are left not exactly mad, but lost in a darkness and ignorance too deep for words, as though shut in "dark, cold caves". *(2025 specimen Q7(iii).)*

**Idea:** the third stage is **emptiness and disorientation** — we struggle to cope without the person who nourished and guided us.

#### Stanza 5 — Peace returns

At last, **after a time**, peace returns — slowly, and never evenly. A quiet, healing energy seems to fill the empty spaces. Our senses recover, though we will **never be quite the same**. And they bring us a comforting truth: those great souls **existed**. Because they lived, we too can live — and live better: "Be and be better. For they existed." *(2025 specimen Q7(iv)(c) and Q7(v).)*

**Idea:** the final stage is **healing and hope**. The influence of great souls continues in us and inspires us to grow.

### Conclusion: the poet's message

- The death of a great soul affects everyone around them, as the fall of a great tree shakes a whole forest.
- Grief moves through **shock**, **regret**, **emptiness** and, slowly, **healing**.
- We are changed for ever, but not destroyed.
- The **memory and influence** of great people live on and inspire us to become better.

*(2025 specimen Q1(x): the theme is the impact of the death of a loved one on those left behind.)*

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Stanzas 1–2: nature's response

- **Points to note:**
  - The tree's fall affects even the distant landscape — **personification** and **hyperbole** show how far-reaching a great loss is.
  - **Lions** and **elephants** — the most powerful animals — are shaken; the strong are not immune to loss. *(2025 specimen Q7(i): lions hunker down, elephants lumber after safety, small things recoil into silence.)*
  - **Small creatures** fall silent, stunned beyond fear — **shock and numbness**. *(2025 specimen Q7(iv)(a))*
  - **2027 specimen Q1(ix):** the natural world reacts with **fear and uncertainty** (true), but the world is **not left permanently unstable** (false) — the poem ends with peace.

### Section 2 — Stanza 3: the immediate aftermath

- **Points to note:**
  - The comparison is revealed: **great trees = great souls**.
  - Grief makes it **hard to breathe** — we are choked with emotion.
  - **"hurtful clarity"** — a painful, sharpened understanding.
  - Memory dwells on **"kind words unsaid"** and **promises not kept** — **remorse and regret**. *(2025 specimen Q7(ii) and Q7(iv)(b))*
  - These sharp feelings are intense but **brief**.

### Section 3 — Stanza 4: the deeper loss

- **Points to note:**
  - Our **reality** was bound to the great soul; their death unsettles our whole world.
  - What **grew** in us because of them — understanding, inner strength — seems to **shrink**.
  - **"dark, cold caves"** — a metaphor for loneliness, confusion and despair. *(2025 specimen Q7(iii): disoriented; struggling to cope; an emotional void.)*

### Section 4 — Stanza 5: healing

- **Points to note:**
  - **Peace returns slowly and unevenly** — grief does not heal in a straight line.
  - A **soothing energy** fills the emptiness — a sense of renewal.
  - We are **changed for ever**, yet restored.
  - **"Be and be better. For they existed."** — the knowledge that they lived gives us strength and inspires us to grow. *(2025 specimen Q7(iv)(c): optimism, hope, joyful acceptance; Q7(v).)*

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Grief and loss:** the death of a great soul shakes all who knew them.
- **The stages of grief:** shock, regret, emptiness and, slowly, healing.
- **Regret:** a reminder to show love and keep promises while we can.
- **Interdependence:** our minds and spirits are shaped by those we love and admire.
- **Hope and renewal:** peace returns; the dead inspire us to be better.
- **Nature and humanity:** the natural world mirrors human feeling.

### 7.1 Tone and mood

| Part | Tone / mood |
|---|---|
| stanzas 1–2 | solemn, hushed, shocked |
| stanza 3 | painful, regretful |
| stanza 4 | dark, desolate |
| stanza 5 | gentle, healing, hopeful |

### 7.2 Significance of the title

The title introduces the **central metaphor**: a mighty tree that shelters many lives. "**Great**" tells us the poem is about exceptional people; "**fall**" suggests death and the shock it brings. Beginning with "**When**", the title makes us wait for the consequences — which the poem then unfolds stanza by stanza.

**Solutions — MCQs**

**2025 specimen Q1(x).** **(a) the impact of the death of a loved one on those left behind.**
**2027 specimen Q1(ix).** **(d) Statement I is false, but statement II is true.** Nature reacts with fear and uncertainty, but the world is not left permanently unstable — peace returns.

**2025 specimen Q7.**

**(i) What effect does the falling of a 'great tree' have on the creatures of the forest?** [3]
**Lions hunker down**; **elephants lumber** after safety; **small things recoil into silence**.

**(ii) How does the death of a great soul affect the lives of those left behind in the immediate aftermath of their passing?** [3]
It becomes **difficult to breathe** — the air seems rarefied and sterile, and one is **choked with emotion**; we **see with hurtful clarity**; and we **remember unfulfilled promises with regret**.

**(iii) What long-term effect does the death of a 'great soul' have?** [3]
We become **disoriented**; we **struggle to manage on our own** and cope with the loss and the **emotional void**; we feel unhinged — "reduced to the unutterable ignorance of dark, cold caves".

**(iv) What feeling is being expressed by: (a) small things recoil into silence (b) kind words unsaid (c) Be and be better. For they existed.** [3]
(a) **Shock / numbness / being stunned.** (b) **Remorse / regret.** (c) **Optimism / hope / joyful acceptance.**

**(v) What is a 'Great tree' a metaphor for? What is the central message of the poem?** [4]
A 'great tree' is a metaphor for **a great soul**. **Message:** the loss of a loved one is **traumatic yet inevitable**; the **memory and influence** of the great soul **linger** and continue to **shape our lives positively**; this brings **hope and healing**, helping us become **better versions of ourselves**.

**2026 Q1(xiii).** **(d) fear to hope.** Nature shudders and hides at the start; at the end peace blooms and we are urged to be better.

**2026 Q6.**

**(i) Three images from nature showing the effect of great trees falling.** [3]
**Rocks on distant hills shudder**; **lions hunker down** (crouch low) in the tall grasses; **even elephants lumber** away after safety. (The small creatures in the forest also shrink into silence.)

**(ii) Two ways the comparison of great trees with great souls is apt; the mood of the extract.** [3]
Any two: like great trees, great souls **tower above others**; they give **shelter, shade and support** to many; their **fall shakes everything around** them and leaves a great **empty space**; both take a lifetime to grow. The mood is one of **shock, fear and insecurity** — even the strongest creatures are shaken.

**(iii) Words describing the air when great souls die; the feeling in 'We breathe, briefly'.** [3]
The air around us becomes **light, rare, sterile** — thin and lifeless. "We breathe, briefly" expresses **shock and grief**: for a moment we can hardly breathe, as if **stunned and numb** with loss.

**(iv) The two things we remember and regret; what happens to our souls.** [3]
Our sharpened memory gnaws on **kind words left unsaid** and **promised walks never taken**. Our souls, which **depended on their nurture**, **shrink and wither (become wizened)**.

**(v) Explain the lasting influence of great souls; what appeals to you, with a reason.** [4]
At the end of the poem, after a period, **peace blooms** slowly; our senses are restored, though **never the same**, and they whisper that the great souls **existed**. Because they lived, **we can be, and be better**: their example continues to **shape and inspire** us after their death. *Personal response:* e.g. the **comparison with great trees** and the frightened animals, which makes an abstract grief vivid; or the **hopeful ending**, because it turns sorrow into a reason to live better.

**2026 Improvement Q1(xiv).** **(d) 'After a period peace blooms'.** "After a period" marks the passing of time; peace returns slowly.

**2026 Improvement Q7.**

**(i) How does a great soul's death affect us? Why?** [3]
**Our reality leaves us** — the world no longer seems real or secure — and **our souls shrink and wither**. This happens because our lives were **bound up with them**: our souls **depended on their nurture**, their care, guidance and love.

**(ii) The impact of the loss of their 'radiance'; our first reaction.** [3]
Our minds, which were **formed and informed by their radiance** (their light, wisdom and example), **fall away** — we feel lost and confused, unable to think clearly. Our first reaction is **shock**: the air seems thin and lifeless, and for a moment we **can barely breathe**.

**(iii) The regrets we may feel; the other emotion in 'We breathe, briefly'.** [3]
Regret for **kind words we never said** to them and for **walks we promised but never took** with them. "We breathe, briefly" suggests **shock and numbness** — a stunned, breathless **grief** in which we can hardly go on living.

**(iv) Three ways the poet offers comfort and hope.** [3]
**After a period, peace blooms**, slowly and irregularly; spaces fill with a **soothing vibration**; **our senses are restored**, though never the same; they whisper that the great souls **existed**; and because they existed, **we can be, and be better**. (Any three.)

**(v) The comparison at the start; its suitability; your own simile or metaphor.** [4]
She compares the death of a great soul to **the fall of a great tree**, which shakes the rocks on distant hills and sends even lions and elephants to safety. It is suitable because a great person, like a great tree, **towers above others**, gives **shelter and support** to many, and leaves a vast **empty space** when gone; their fall disturbs everyone around them. *Own simile or metaphor, e.g.:* "The death of a great soul is **like the setting of the sun** — the world goes dark and cold, yet we know light will return"; or "A great soul is **a lighthouse**; when it goes out, the ships are lost at sea."

**2025 Q1(ix).** **(a) (1) is false, but (2) is true.** Nature trembles and hides when great trees fall, but the poem ends with peace returning, so the world is not left unstable for ever.

**2025 Improvement Q1(ix).** **(d) hope.** After a period, peace returns, and we are urged to be, and be better, because the great souls existed.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A (paraphrased).** When great souls die, those left behind can hardly breathe; for a while they see with painful clearness, and their memories return again and again to loving words never spoken and promises never kept.

**(i)** What comparison does the poem make in its first three stanzas? [3]
**Answer:** The **fall of great trees** in a forest is compared to the **death of great souls**; the shaken creatures of the forest are compared to the **people left behind**.

**(ii)** Why does it become hard to breathe? [3]
**Answer:** Grief overwhelms us — we are **choked with emotion**, as though the air had become thin and lifeless.

**(iii)** What is meant by "hurtful clarity"? [3]
**Answer:** Grief makes us see the **truth sharply** — about the person, our relationship and our own failures — and this clear sight **hurts**.

**(iv)** What does memory dwell on, and what feeling does this show? [3]
**Answer:** On **kind words unsaid** and **promises not kept**; it shows **remorse and regret**.

**(v)** What lesson can readers draw from this stanza? [4]
**Answer:** We should **express our love and keep our promises** while people are still with us, because death can come suddenly; time spent together and small kindnesses are precious and should not be postponed.

**Extract B (paraphrased).** After a time, peace slowly returns, though never evenly. A soothing energy fills the emptiness. We are never quite the same, but our senses tell us that those we lost truly lived — and that we can live, and live better, because they did.

**(i)** What had the speaker and others felt before this healing? [3]
**Answer:** **Shock**, difficulty in breathing, **hurtful clarity**, **regret**, and a deep **emptiness** — as if lost in **dark, cold caves**.

**(ii)** Why does peace return "slowly" and unevenly? [3]
**Answer:** Because **grief does not heal in a straight line**; recovery takes time and has good and bad days.

**(iii)** What does "never quite the same" suggest? [3]
**Answer:** That we are **permanently changed** by the loss, even after we recover.

**(iv)** What feeling do the final lines express? [3]
**Answer:** **Optimism, hope and joyful acceptance** (2025 specimen Q7(iv)(c)).

**(v)** Explain "Be and be better. For they existed." Why is it the central message? [4]
**Answer:** Because the great soul **lived**, we can carry on — and live **as better people**, following their example. It is central because the poem moves from the darkness of loss to the recognition that the great soul's **memory and influence** continue to shape us positively, bringing **hope and healing**.

> I keep remembering the evening he taught us both to fly a kite on the hill behind your house. He was patient with our tangled strings and laughed louder than we did when the kite finally rose. He made everyone around him feel welcome.
>
> I can only imagine how strange and empty the house must feel now. Please don't feel you have to be strong for anyone. If you would like company, I can come over after school on Thursday, or we can simply go for a walk on Sunday.
>
> He gave you so much — his stories, his kindness and his love of learning. Those will stay with you. I hope, in time, remembering him will bring you more comfort than pain.
>
> With love,
> Asha

**Task B (speech).** Write a short speech to be delivered at your school assembly in tribute to a person whose life inspired you.

**Tips:**
- Begin with a **striking image or memory**, as Angelou begins with a falling tree.
- Explain **what the person gave others** — knowledge, courage, kindness.
- Acknowledge the **sense of loss**, but move towards **what we can learn and carry forward**.
- End with a **clear, hopeful sentence** that the audience will remember.

**Q1.** What effect does the falling of a great tree have on the creatures of the forest? *(2025 specimen)*

## 15. Revision Notes

### Poem at a glance
- **Poet:** Maya Angelou (1928–2014); collection *I Shall Not Be Moved* (1990); **free verse**, five stanzas; an **elegy**.
- **Central metaphor:** **great trees = great souls**.
- **St 1–2 (shock):** the landscape trembles; **lions hunker down**; **elephants lumber after safety**; **small things recoil into silence**, too stunned for fear.
- **St 3 (regret):** hard to breathe; **hurtful clarity**; memory returns to **kind words unsaid** and promises not kept.
- **St 4 (emptiness):** reality slips away; what grew in us shrinks; lost in **dark, cold caves**.
- **St 5 (healing):** peace returns **slowly and unevenly**; a soothing energy; never the same; **"Be and be better. For they existed."**
- **Theme (2025):** the impact of the death of a loved one on those left behind.
- **2027 statement pair:** world permanently unstable — **false**; nature reacts with fear and uncertainty — **true**.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The poem is about deforestation | It is about the death of great souls. |
| The world is left permanently unstable | Peace returns (2027 specimen). |
| "kind words unsaid" shows shock | It shows remorse/regret. |
| "Be and be better" shows regret | It shows optimism/hope. |
| The poem rhymes | It is free verse. |
| Healing is quick and complete | Slow, uneven; we are never quite the same. |
