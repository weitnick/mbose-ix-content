## 1. Complete Lesson

### What is waste?

**Waste** is any material that is **thrown away** because it is **no longer useful** to its owner. Growing **population, urbanisation, industrialisation** and a **"use-and-throw" lifestyle** produce ever more waste.

**Types of waste**

| On the basis of | Types | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature** | **Biodegradable** — broken down by micro-organisms | Kitchen waste, fruit peels, leaves, **paper**, dung |
| | **Non-biodegradable** — not broken down, or only after hundreds of years | **Plastic, polythene, glass, metals**, e-waste |
| **Source** | **Domestic (household)** | Food waste, paper, packaging |
| | **Industrial** | Chemicals, fly ash, slag, effluents |
| | **Agricultural** | Crop residue, dung, pesticide containers |
| | **Biomedical (hospital)** | Syringes, bandages, medicines |
| | **E-waste** | Old phones, computers, batteries |
| | **Radioactive (nuclear)** | Spent fuel from nuclear plants |

**Why is e-waste rising in India?** The **rapidly rising use of electronic gadgets** and a **modern lifestyle** (frequent replacement), with **no effective e-waste management system** and **little awareness** about proper disposal.

**Why is radioactive waste the most harmful?** It gives out **dangerous radiation for thousands of years**, causing **cancer and genetic defects**; it **cannot be destroyed** by ordinary means and must be **stored safely deep underground**.

### Impact of waste accumulation

**1. Spoilage of the landscape** — heaps of garbage are **ugly**, give off a **foul smell** and **spoil the scenic beauty** of towns, rivers and beaches.

**2. Pollution**
- **Air** — burning waste and rotting garbage release **harmful gases**, smoke and **greenhouse gases** (methane, carbon dioxide); they add to **smog**, **acid rain** and **air-borne diseases**.
- **Water** — **sewage**, chemicals and **leachate** (dirty liquid seeping from dumps) pollute **rivers, lakes and groundwater**; this causes **eutrophication**, kills **aquatic life** and leads to **biomagnification**.
- **Soil** — toxic chemicals, plastics and metals **reduce soil fertility**; plastics **block the soil**.

**3. Health hazards**
- Garbage is a **breeding ground for flies, mosquitoes, rats and other pests** that spread **malaria, dengue, cholera, typhoid, diarrhoea**.
- **Polluted air** causes **lung (respiratory) diseases**; **mercury poisoning** causes **Minamata disease**; polluted water spreads **infections**.

**Effect on living things**
- **Aquatic / marine life** — fish and other animals **die from polluted water**, lack of oxygen (eutrophication), and from **swallowing plastics** (turtles, whales).
- **Terrestrial life** — **foul smell, pests, diseases**; **polluted air, water and soil** harm plants and animals; **cattle die after eating plastic bags**.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2018, Q11(c))* Mention one way in which waste accumulation affects (i) aquatic life (ii) terrestrial life (iii) the landscape.
**Solution:** (i) **Fish and other aquatic animals may die** due to pollution created by waste. (ii) Waste **looks ugly**, gives a **foul smell**, **attracts insects and rodents**, **pollutes air and water** and **harms plants**. (iii) It makes the **landscape look ugly**.

### Need for waste management

1. To **prevent pollution** of **air, water and soil**.
2. To **protect health** — check the spread of **diseases**.
3. To **keep surroundings clean and beautiful** — protect the landscape.
4. To **conserve resources** — reuse and recycle materials; get **manure and energy** from waste.
5. To **reduce greenhouse gases** and **global warming**.
6. Because **population and urbanisation** produce **ever-larger amounts** of waste, and land for dumping is limited.

### Key terms (in brief)

| Term | Meaning | Causes and effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Global warming** | The **rise in the average temperature of the earth** | Caused by **greenhouse gases** (carbon dioxide, **methane**, CFCs, nitrous oxide) from **burning fuels, deforestation** and **rotting waste**; leads to **melting glaciers, rising sea level**, extreme weather |
| **Acid rain** | Rain containing **sulphuric and nitric acids** | Formed when **sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides** from **burning coal and oil** (factories, power plants, vehicles) mix with water vapour; it **damages buildings and monuments** (e.g. marble), **forests, soils, lakes and fish** |
| **Eutrophication** | The **depletion of oxygen in a water body** due to pollution by **excess nutrients** | **Fertilisers, sewage and detergents** add nitrates and phosphates → **algal bloom** → algae die and decompose → bacteria **use up the oxygen** → **fish die** |
| **Biomagnification** | The process in which **toxins become progressively more concentrated** in organisms at **higher levels of the food chain** | Pesticides (DDT), **mercury, lead** enter water → plankton → small fish → large fish → birds and humans, with **concentration rising at each level** |
| **Thermal pollution** | **Rise in the temperature of water** bodies | **Hot water** discharged by **power plants and industries** lowers **dissolved oxygen** and **kills aquatic life** |

**Other useful terms**
- **Smog** — **smoke + fog**; a thick, polluted haze caused by **air pollution**.
- **Sewage** — **waste water** from homes (toilets, kitchens, bathrooms) and drains, carrying **human waste, detergents and food waste**. It must be **treated before disposal** — untreated sewage **pollutes rivers**, spreads **diseases (cholera, typhoid)** and causes **eutrophication**.
- **Methane** — a **greenhouse gas** released by **decomposing organic waste in dumps and landfills**, **cattle and animal dung**, **paddy (rice) fields**, **sewage and wetlands**, and **coal mines and natural-gas leaks**.
- **Leachate** — the polluted liquid that seeps out of garbage dumps into soil and groundwater.

### Methods of safe disposal

#### 1. Segregation

**Segregation** is the **sorting and separating of waste at its source** — **biodegradable (wet)** from **non-biodegradable (dry)**, and **hazardous** waste kept apart — usually in **colour-coded bins**.

![Fig. 16.1 Segregation into separate bins](figures/g_ch16_fig01_segregation.png)

**Why must waste be segregated before disposal?**
1. It leads to **better, cleaner and safer disposal** — each kind goes to the right method (compost, recycle, special treatment) and causes **less pollution**.
2. It **encourages the 3Rs** — recyclable material is not spoilt by mixing.
3. It **keeps hazardous waste** (biomedical, plastic, e-waste) **apart**, preventing **fires, toxic smoke and health hazards**.
4. It **reduces the amount sent to dumps**.

**At home** — keep **separate bins**: kitchen waste (wet) in one, paper/plastic/glass/metal (dry) in another, batteries and medicines separately.

#### 2. Dumping

**Dumping** is the method in which waste is **collected and taken to large open, low-lying areas on the outskirts of cities**, where it is **thrown and left**. A **sanitary landfill** is an improved dump — a **lined pit** in which waste is spread, **compacted and covered with soil** daily.

| Advantages of dumping | Disadvantages (why it is harmful) |
|---|---|
| **Saves time and labour** | **Spreads diseases**; **breeding ground** for mosquitoes, flies and rodents |
| Needs **little investment** | **Foul smell**; **spoils the scenic beauty** |
| Easy for large quantities | **Leachate pollutes soil, surface water and groundwater** |
| | Releases **methane**; dumps catch **fire**, giving **toxic smoke** |

**Why are landfills set up far away from the city?** To **avoid spoilage of the landscape**, **foul smell** and the **spread of pollutants and greenhouse gases**; to **prevent health hazards and pest infestation** and **groundwater contamination** in inhabited areas; because they **need a large area**; and to **keep the city clean**.

#### 3. Composting

**Composting** is a **biological (aerobic) process** in which **micro-organisms decompose organic (biodegradable) waste** — kitchen waste, fruit and vegetable peels, leaves, dung — into **humus (compost)**, a **good fertiliser** for plants. **Vermicomposting** uses **earthworms** to speed up the process.

**Benefits of composting (how it converts "waste to wealth")**
1. **Converts waste into valuable manure** — nutrient-rich compost that **fertilises the soil** and **increases crop production** without expense.
2. **Considerably reduces the volume of waste**.
3. **Improves soil texture and fertility** and the **water-holding capacity** of the soil.
4. **Aids plant growth**; **suppresses plant diseases**; **reduces the need for chemical fertilisers**; prevents pollution.

**Not suitable for composting** — plastics, glass, metals and **chemical products** (for example **soap** — even "neem soap" — paints, medicines).

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q12(iv))* (a) What is composting? (b) Enumerate two advantages of composting.
**Solution:** (a) Composting is a **biological process** in which **micro-organisms convert organic waste into humus**, which is a good fertiliser for plants. (b) Any two: the waste is **converted into valuable fertiliser**; waste is **considerably reduced**; it **improves soil texture and fertility**; it **aids plant growth**; it **enhances the water-holding capacity** of the soil.

**Other methods** — **incineration** (burning waste at very high temperatures in closed incinerators, used for **biomedical waste**; open burning is harmful); **waste-to-energy** plants; **biogas** from organic waste.

### The 3Rs — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle

![Fig. 16.4 The waste hierarchy](figures/g_ch16_fig04_waste_hierarchy.png)

**1. Reduce** — **produce less waste** in the first place: buy only what is needed, **avoid wasting food**, carry **cloth or jute bags**, avoid **single-use plastics** and disposables, use **both sides of paper**, choose products with less packaging.
- **Significance of "Reduce"** — it **prevents waste accumulation** and its harmful effects, **saves natural resources**, **decreases pollution** and **reduces greenhouse-gas emissions**. It is the **best** of the 3Rs.

**2. Reuse** — **use an item again**, for the same or a different purpose, **without processing**: glass bottles and jars for **storage**, an empty can as a **pen stand**, old clothes as **dusters or doormats**, newspapers as **paper bags**, tin boxes for storage, passing textbooks to juniors.

**3. Recycle** — **collect discarded materials and process them into new products**: **waste paper → handmade paper / newsprint**; **plastic → new plastic goods**; **glass, metal cans and scrap → new glass and metal**. Materials like **plastic, paper, glass, metal and wood** are usually sent for recycling.
- **Benefits of recycling** — it **conserves natural resources** (trees, ores), **reduces waste accumulation** and the **disposal burden**, **reduces pollution** and **saves energy**.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2025, Q12(iii))* With reference to the 3Rs, give a term for each: (a) an empty soft-drink can is used as a pen stand (b) old scrap papers are used to manufacture handmade paper (c) no food is wasted in the household.
**Solution:** (a) **Reuse**. (b) **Recycle**. (c) **Reduce**.

### Making waste useful

| Sector | How waste can be made useful |
|---|---|
| **Household** | **Compost** kitchen waste; **recycle** newspapers, glass, metal scrap and plastic; **reuse** tins and bottles for storage; turn old clothes into **doormats, covers** |
| **Industry** | **Bagasse** → cardboard and paper; **molasses** → alcohol; **press mud** → wax for shoe polish and carbon paper; **fly ash** → bricks, cement, road-making; **steel scrap** → mini steel plants |
| **Agriculture** | Crop residue and dung → **compost**, **biogas**, **biofuel**; residue as **fodder**; straw for **cardboard** |

### What can individuals and students do?

1. **Segregate** waste at home and in school.
2. **Compost** kitchen and garden waste.
3. **Carry cloth or jute bags**; **avoid plastic** and **single-use** items.
4. **Reuse** bottles, jars, boxes; use **old notebooks** as rough books; **pass on textbooks**.
5. **Send waste for recycling**; dispose of **e-waste and batteries** properly.
6. **Do not litter**; take part in **clean-up drives**; **spread awareness**.
7. **Plant trees**; save **water and electricity**; use **public transport** or **car-pool**.

**Government initiatives** — **Swachh Bharat Abhiyan** (Clean India Mission, 2014); rules on **solid waste, plastic waste** (ban on many single-use plastics) and **e-waste**; **sewage treatment plants** and river-cleaning programmes; **waste-to-energy plants**; **wasteland management** and **afforestation**.

**Why should waste management be practised in every school?** To **teach the optimum use of resources**, to help students **understand the harm** that waste does to living things and the environment, and to **learn the 3Rs** through practice.

### Summary of the chapter

- **Waste** — biodegradable (kitchen, paper) and non-biodegradable (plastic, glass, metal); domestic, industrial, agricultural, biomedical, e-waste, radioactive (most harmful).
- **Impact** — **spoilt landscape**; **pollution** of air (gases, smog, acid rain), water (sewage, leachate, eutrophication, biomagnification) and soil; **health hazards** (pests, diseases, lung problems).
- **Need** — prevent pollution and disease, keep surroundings clean, conserve resources, reduce greenhouse gases.
- **Key terms** — **global warming**, **acid rain**, **eutrophication** (oxygen depletion), **biomagnification** (toxins concentrate up the food chain), **thermal pollution**; smog, sewage, methane.
- **Methods** — **segregation** (sorting at source; colour bins); **dumping** (cheap, quick, but harmful; landfills far from cities); **composting** (organic waste → humus; waste to wealth); incineration for biomedical waste.
- **3Rs** — **reduce** (best; no food waste, cloth bags), **reuse** (can as pen stand), **recycle** (scrap paper → handmade paper); waste made useful in homes, industry and farms; Swachh Bharat.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Rot or remain?
Biodegradable waste **rots** (peels, paper); non-biodegradable waste **remains** for centuries (plastic, glass). This one difference decides whether waste goes to a **compost pit** or a **recycling unit**.

### Section 2 — From the bin to the river
Waste dumped on land seeps into water; nutrients feed **algal blooms** (eutrophication); poisons climb the **food chain** (biomagnification) — the same waste harms land, water and people.

### Section 3 — Sort, then solve
**Segregation** is the first step: once sorted, wet waste becomes compost, dry waste is recycled, and hazardous waste is treated safely.

### Section 4 — The best waste is no waste
In the **3Rs**, **reduce** comes first — the waste we never create needs no disposal at all.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Wangari Maathai (1940–2011)
The Kenyan environmentalist who founded the Green Belt Movement, which planted tens of millions of trees, and won the **Nobel Peace Prize in 2004**. She believed that people must be informed and empowered to protect their own environment.

### 6.2 Almitra Patel
An engineer and campaigner whose petition to the Supreme Court led to India's **Municipal Solid Waste Rules (2000)**, making segregation, collection and safe disposal of city waste a legal duty of municipalities.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Waste is a resource in the wrong place** — sorted, it becomes compost, new paper, energy.
- **Pollution travels** — from dumps to groundwater, from rivers to fish to people.
- **Everyone is a waste manager** — individual habits add up to clean or dirty cities.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya:** Mawlynnong village in the East Khasi Hills, often called one of Asia's cleanest villages, uses bamboo dustbins, composting and community clean-ups.
- **Your school:** a compost pit for canteen waste, separate bins in each classroom and a "no plastic" rule put the chapter into practice.
- **Your phone:** an old phone or battery should go to an authorised e-waste collector, not the dustbin.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (The housing society)** A housing society of 200 flats sends all its mixed garbage to the city dump. Suggest a three-step plan to cut the waste sent out.
**Model answer:** (1) Give each flat two bins and segregate wet and dry waste at source. (2) Compost the wet waste in a society compost pit and use the manure in the garden. (3) Sell or give dry waste — paper, plastic, metal, glass — to recyclers; keep e-waste and batteries for authorised collectors.

**Q2. (The lake turns green)** A lake near farmland turns bright green each summer and dead fish float on it. Explain and suggest a remedy.
**Model answer:** Fertiliser run-off and sewage add nutrients, causing an algal bloom; dead algae decompose and use up oxygen (eutrophication), killing fish. Remedy: treat sewage before release, use organic manure and less chemical fertiliser, keep a green buffer strip around the lake.

**Q3. (The fish-eating bird)** A pesticide is found in tiny amounts in a river, but in large amounts in fish-eating birds. Why?
**Model answer:** Biomagnification — the pesticide is not broken down; each level of the food chain eats many organisms of the level below, so the poison concentrates as it moves up.

**Q4. (The old phone)** Your old mobile phone no longer works. What should you do with it, and why not throw it in the dustbin?
**Model answer:** Give it to an authorised e-waste collector or a take-back scheme; it contains metals and chemicals (lead, mercury) that pollute soil and water if dumped, and parts can be recovered by recycling.

**Q5. (The canteen)** The school canteen produces many disposable plates and food scraps daily. Suggest two changes.
**Model answer:** Use washable steel plates (reduce and reuse); compost food scraps in a school compost pit (waste to manure).

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — The fire at the dump

> A fire breaks out at a city dump of mixed garbage, plastics and hospital waste; toxic smoke covers the city.

**(i)** Which method of waste management would have reduced the hazard?
**Answer:** Segregation.

**(ii)** Why is the smoke toxic?
**Answer:** Burning plastics and biomedical waste release harmful gases.

**(iii)** Name a gas released by rotting garbage that helps such fires start.
**Answer:** Methane.

**(iv)** How should hospital waste be disposed of?
**Answer:** By incineration in closed incinerators (special treatment).

### Case 2 — The compost corner

> Canteen peels, tea leaves and dry leaves go into a school compost corner; weeks later, dark manure feeds the flower beds.

**(i)** Name this process.
**Answer:** Composting.

**(ii)** What turns the waste into manure?
**Answer:** Micro-organisms (bacteria and fungi; earthworms in vermicomposting).

**(iii)** Name one item that must NOT go in.
**Answer:** Plastic (also glass, metal, soap).

**(iv)** Give one benefit for the garden.
**Answer:** Improves soil fertility (also water-holding capacity).

### Case 3 — The three Rs at home

> A family stops buying bottled water, keeps glass jars for pickles and sells old newspapers to the scrap dealer.

**(i)** Which R is shown by not buying bottled water?
**Answer:** Reduce.

**(ii)** Which R is shown by keeping glass jars for pickles?
**Answer:** Reuse.

**(iii)** What happens to the newspapers, and which R is this?
**Answer:** They are made into new paper — recycle.

**(iv)** Which of the 3Rs is the most preferred?
**Answer:** Reduce.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Waste — biodegradable (peels, paper, dung) and non-biodegradable (plastic, glass, metal); domestic, industrial, agricultural, biomedical, e-waste, radioactive (most harmful — radiation for thousands of years).
- Impact — landscape spoilt (ugly, foul smell); air (gases, smog, acid rain, greenhouse gases), water (sewage, leachate, eutrophication, biomagnification), soil; health (pests, malaria, cholera, lung diseases, Minamata); aquatic and terrestrial life.
- Need — prevent pollution and disease, clean surroundings, conserve resources, reduce greenhouse gases.
- Key terms — global warming, acid rain, eutrophication (oxygen depletion), biomagnification (toxins concentrate up the food chain), thermal pollution; sewage (treat before disposal); methane (landfills, dung, paddy, sewage, coal mines).
- Segregation — sort at source; colour bins; safe disposal, 3Rs, no toxic fires.
- Dumping — open low-lying areas outside cities; saves time, labour, money; but disease, smell, leachate, methane, fires; landfills far from cities.
- Composting — micro-organisms turn organic waste into humus; waste to wealth; fertile soil, water retention, less waste; vermicomposting; not soap or plastic.
- 3Rs — reduce (best), reuse (can as pen stand), recycle (handmade paper); waste made useful in homes, industry (bagasse, molasses, fly ash), farms (biogas, compost); Swachh Bharat Abhiyan (2014).

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Calling paper non-biodegradable | Paper is biodegradable. |
| Mixing up eutrophication and biomagnification | Eutrophication — oxygen loss in water; biomagnification — poison builds up the food chain. |
| Writing "recycle" for a can used as a pen stand | Using it again without processing is reuse. |
| Calling dumping "safe because it is cheap" | Dumping is cheap but harmful. |
| Putting soap or plastic in a compost pit | Only organic waste can be composted. |
| Writing smog as "water pollution" | Smog is caused by air pollution. |
