## 1. Complete Lesson

### What is a topographical map?

A **topographical map** (toposheet) is a **large-scale map** that shows both the **natural features** of an area — **relief** (hills, valleys, slopes), **drainage** (rivers, streams, tanks), **natural vegetation** — and its **man-made features** — **settlements, roads, railways, wells, canals, post offices, temples** — using **conventional signs and colours**.

- In India toposheets are prepared by the **Survey of India**, the national mapping agency, founded in **1767** with its headquarters at **Dehradun**.
- ICSE uses **1 : 50,000** sheets, each covering **15′ of latitude × 15′ of longitude**.
- **Sheet numbers.** Older sheets carry numbers such as **45D/7** and **45D/10**. Under the **National Map Policy (2005)** the Survey of India issued **Open Series Maps (OSM)** with new numbers such as **G43S7** and **G43S10**, drawn on the **UTM grid**.
- Since 2017 every ICSE paper has used one of **two neighbouring sheets** of a **semi-arid region on the Rajasthan–Gujarat border**: **45D/7 (now G43S7)** or **45D/10 (now G43S10)**. That is why the same features — **seasonal streams, wells, tanks with embankments, sand dunes, causeways, scrub** — come up year after year.

**Marginal information** — read it before answering:

| Item | Where / what it tells you |
|---|---|
| **Sheet number** | Top of the extract (e.g. G43S7) |
| **Scale** | Below the extract — **RF 1 : 50,000**, statement **2 cm to 1 km**, and a **linear scale** |
| **Contour interval** | Below the extract — **20 metres** on these sheets |
| **Grid values** | Eastings along the top and bottom, northings along the sides (two-digit numbers in red) |
| **Latitude and longitude** | Values in degrees and minutes at the corners and edges; black lines across the map |
| **Legend and notes** | Conventional signs; notes such as "**tanks, shown dry, usually contain water from July to February**", "**a relative height against a well indicates its depth**" |

### The grid system

The map is covered by a network of **grid lines** forming **squares**.

- **Eastings** are the **vertical** lines. Their values **increase towards the east** (left to right) and are printed along the **top and bottom** margins.
- **Northings** are the **horizontal** lines. Their values **increase towards the north** (bottom to top) and are printed along the **left and right** margins.
- On a 1 : 50,000 sheet the lines are **2 cm apart**, i.e. **1 km apart on the ground**, so each grid square covers **1 km × 1 km = 1 km²**.

![Fig. 1.1 The grid system](figures/g_ch01_fig01_grid_system.png)

#### Four-figure grid reference

A **four-figure grid reference** identifies a **grid square**. It is written as the **easting** (first two figures) followed by the **northing** (last two figures) of the square's **south-west (bottom-left) corner**.

**Steps:** (1) Find the easting line on the **left (west) side** of the square. (2) Find the northing line along the **bottom (south) side** of the square. (3) Write them together — **easting first**.

In Fig. 1.1 the yellow square lies east of easting **45** and north of northing **27**, so its reference is **4527**.

> **Remember:** "**Along the corridor, then up the stairs**" — first move right along the eastings, then climb up the northings. A reference like **2706** therefore means easting **27**, northing **06**, not the other way round.

#### Six-figure grid reference

A **six-figure grid reference** identifies a **point** (a temple, a spot height, a well) inside a square. Divide the square **by eye** into **ten parts each way**.

1. Write the **easting** of the square, then the **number of tenths** the point lies **east** of that line → **three figures**.
2. Write the **northing** of the square, then the **number of tenths** the point lies **north** of that line → **three figures**.
3. Put the two together: **easting part first, northing part second**.

![Fig. 1.2 Six-figure grid reference](figures/g_ch01_fig02_six_figure.png)

In Fig. 1.2 the temple lies 6 tenths east of easting 64 (**646**) and 3 tenths north of northing 36 (**363**): six-figure reference **646363**.

**Tips.** The **tenth is an estimate**: examiners accept a difference of one tenth (for example the marking scheme of 2019 accepted both 201215 and 202215). Give the reference of the **symbol itself** (the dot of a spot height, the base of a temple), not of its name.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2024, Q1(i))* (a) Give the six-figure grid reference for spot height .522. (b) What is the pattern of settlement in the grid square 3591?
**Solution:** (a) **375966** — the spot is 5 tenths east of easting 37 and 6 tenths north of northing 96. (b) **Scattered / dispersed**.

### Scale

The **scale** of a map is the **ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground**. It is shown in **three ways**:

| Method | On ICSE sheets | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Representative Fraction (RF)** | **1 : 50,000** | 1 unit on the map = 50,000 of the **same** units on the ground. It has **no units**, so it is the **universally accepted** scale — it can be read in centimetres, inches or any unit, anywhere in the world. |
| **Statement scale** | **2 cm to 1 km** | 50,000 cm = 500 m, so **1 cm = 0.5 km** and **2 cm = 1 km**. |
| **Linear (graphical) scale** | A divided bar | Measured lengths can be read off directly; the scale stays correct even if the map is enlarged or reduced. |

![Fig. 1.3 Scales](figures/g_ch01_fig03_scales.png)

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2023, Q1(iii))* (a) What do you understand by 1 : 50,000 printed below the map extract? (b) What is the pattern of drainage seen in the grid square 2505?
**Solution:** (a) It is the **Representative Fraction (RF)** — the scale written as a ratio: **1 unit on the map represents 50,000 units on the ground** (2 cm to 1 km). It is the universal scale. (b) **Dendritic**.

### Measuring distance

**1. Straight-line (direct or "as the crow flies") distance**
- Measure the map distance between the two points with a **ruler** in centimetres.
- **Distance on the ground (km) = map distance (cm) ÷ 2**.

**2. Distance along a road, track, railway or river** (a curved route)
- Lay a **thread** (or step a **divider**) along every bend of the route, mark the end, straighten it and measure it — or measure short straight stretches and add them.
- Again, **km = cm ÷ 2**.

**3. Using distance stones** — along metalled roads and highways small **red numbers** show the **distance stones** (kilometre stones). The distance between two stones is the **difference of their numbers**: from stone **222** to stone **226** = **4 km** *(2027 specimen)*.

**4. Time, speed and distance** — **time = distance ÷ speed**. Each grid square is 1 km wide, so the distance across the map can often be counted in squares.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2018, Q1(h))* What is the direct distance in kilometres between the surveyed tree west of Rampura (9580) and the chhatri in Juvol (9282)?
**Solution:** Map distance = **6.7 cm**. Scale 2 cm = 1 km, so 6.7 ÷ 2 = **3.35 km** (the marking scheme accepted 3.25–3.45 km).

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2024 and 2025 specimens, Q1(ii))* Mohan goes on a bicycle from 02 northing to 12 northing in a straight line. If he moves at a speed of 10 km per hour, how much time will he take?
**Solution:** From northing 02 to 12 = **10 grid squares = 10 km**. Time = distance ÷ speed = 10 ÷ 10 = **one hour**.

### Calculating area

Every grid square is **2 cm × 2 cm** on the map = **1 km × 1 km** on the ground = **1 km²**.

- **Region enclosed by two eastings and two northings:** **Area = (difference of eastings) × (difference of northings) km²**.
- **Whole extract:** count the squares from the first to the last easting and from the first to the last northing, and multiply.
- **Irregular area** (a forest, a lake): count the **full squares**; add **squares more than half covered** as whole squares and **ignore those less than half covered**.

![Fig. 1.5 Area](figures/g_ch01_fig05_area.png)

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2023, Q1(v))* Calculate the area in square kilometres of the region enclosed between Easting 26 to 29 and Northing 05 to 11.
**Solution:** Length = 29 − 26 = 3 km; breadth = 11 − 05 = 6 km. Area = 3 × 6 = **18 grid squares**; each square is 1 km² (scale 2 cm to 1 km) ∴ area = **18 km²**.

### Direction

The **top of a toposheet is north**. Directions are given by the **points of the compass**: the **four cardinal points** (N, E, S, W), the **four intermediate points** (NE, SE, SW, NW) and, for finer work, **16 points** (NNE, ENE, ESE, SSE, SSW, WSW, WNW, NNW).

**"Direction of A from B"** — imagine you are **standing at B** and **looking towards A**. Draw a line from B to A and read the compass point.

![Fig. 1.4 Direction](figures/g_ch01_fig04_direction.png)

**Using grid references:** compare the eastings (larger easting = farther **east**) and the northings (larger northing = farther **north**). For **Malgarh (2414) from Sonpur (2111)**: easting 24 > 21 (east), northing 14 > 11 (north) → **north-east**. If one difference is much smaller than the other, the answer is nearer the main direction (for example **ENE** or simply **east**).

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2025 specimen, Q1(iii)(a))* What is the compass direction of Rampura (2709) from Kotda (3005)?
**Solution:** Stand at Kotda (3005) and look at Rampura (2709): the easting falls (30 → 27, so **west**) and the northing rises (05 → 09, so **north**) → **north-west**.

### Heights and contours

| Feature | How it is shown | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Contour** | **Brown line** with its value (e.g. **300**) | A line joining places of **equal height above mean sea level** |
| **Contour interval** | Printed below the extract | The **difference in height between two successive contours** — **20 m** on these sheets |
| **Spot height** | A **black dot** with a number, e.g. **.324** | The **exact height** of that point above mean sea level, in metres |
| **Triangulated height** | A **small triangle** with a number, e.g. **∆224** | Height of a **survey (triangulation) station**, usually on a hill-top |
| **Bench mark (BM)** | **BM** with a value, e.g. **BM 63.3** | A height marked on a permanent object (a wall, a pillar) by surveyors |
| **Relative height** | A number with **r**, e.g. **.8r**, **3r** | The **approximate height between the top and bottom of a steep slope**, an **embankment** or a **sand dune** |
| **Relative depth** | A number with **r** **against a well** (in blue), e.g. **30r** | The **total depth of the well** in metres |

- **Highest point of an extract** — look for the **largest spot or triangulated height**, usually inside the innermost closed contour of a hill.
- **Difference in altitude** between two points = higher value − lower value (in metres).
- Shapes of contours (hill, valley, ridge, slopes) are studied in **Chapter 2**.

**Example 7.** *(ICSE 2023, Q1(iv)(a))* What do you understand by .10r in 2709?
**Solution:** It is the **relative height** of a **sand dune**, which is **10 metres** (the height from its foot to its top).

### Colours and conventional signs

| Colour | Used for |
|---|---|
| **Red** | **Grid lines and their numbers**, **roads** (metalled and unmetalled), **settlements and huts**, **distance stones**; on the OSM sheets also **cart-tracks** |
| **Black** | **Names**, **railways**, **pack-tracks**, **dry (seasonal) streams**, **lines of latitude and longitude**, **boundaries**, **spot and triangulated heights**, **broken ground** |
| **Blue** | **Water** — perennial rivers and water channels, **wells**, **tanks**, canals |
| **Brown** | **Contours**, **sand dunes** and sandy features |
| **Green** | **Forests**, **scrub**, trees, orchards |
| **Yellow** | **Cultivated land** |
| **White** | **Uncultivated or barren land** |

![Fig. 1.6 Conventional signs](figures/g_ch01_fig06_conventional_signs.png)

**Lines that are not grid lines.** A **thin black line** running right across the extract, between two grid lines, is a **line of latitude** (if horizontal) or **longitude / meridian** (if vertical); its value in **degrees and minutes** is printed in the margin. A **black dash-and-dot line** is a **boundary** (district or tehsil).

### Summary of the chapter

- **Toposheet** — large-scale Survey of India map (1 : 50,000; 15′ × 15′); old numbers 45D/7, 45D/10, new OSM numbers G43S7, G43S10; read the **marginal information** first.
- **Grid** — eastings (vertical, increase eastwards) and northings (horizontal, increase northwards); squares of **1 km²**. **Four-figure** = square (SW corner); **six-figure** = point (tenths); **easting first**.
- **Scale** — **RF 1 : 50,000** (universal, no units), **statement 2 cm to 1 km**, **linear**. **km = cm ÷ 2**.
- **Distance** — ruler (straight), thread/divider (along a route), **distance stones** (difference of numbers); **time = distance ÷ speed**.
- **Area** — difference of eastings × difference of northings = **km²**; count half-squares carefully.
- **Direction** — "A **from** B": stand at B; compare eastings (east–west) and northings (north–south).
- **Heights** — contours (brown, equal height), **contour interval 20 m**, spot height (dot), triangulated height (triangle), bench mark, **relative height r** (slope, embankment, dune) or **depth of a well** (r in blue).
- **Colours** — red (grid, roads, settlements), black (names, railways, dry streams, latitude/longitude lines), blue (water), brown (contours, sand), green (forest), yellow (cultivated), white (barren).

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The map is a grid of kilometres
Every number on the margin is a **kilometre line**. Once you see the extract as rows and columns of **1 km squares**, references, distances and areas become counting exercises.

### Section 2 — Order matters
**Easting before northing** in references; **"from"** fixes where you stand in direction questions. Most lost marks in Question 1 come from reversing one of these.

### Section 3 — Numbers carry meanings
A number on a toposheet is never just a number: **.324** is a spot height, **∆224** a survey station, **300** in brown a contour value, **20** in red a distance stone, **.10r** the height of a dune, **30r** in blue the depth of a well. Read the **colour and the symbol** with the number.

### Section 4 — Always give units
Distances in **km**, areas in **km²**, heights and contour intervals in **metres**, time in **hours or minutes**.

### 5.2 Memory aids

- **"Along the corridor, then up the stairs"** — easting, then northing.
- **"Two centimetres make a kilometre"** — km = cm ÷ 2; one square = one km².
- **"FROM = stand there"** — direction of A from B: stand at B.
- **"Brown is for height, blue is for water, red is for people"** (contours; wells and tanks; roads and huts).

### 5.3 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- The **Great Trigonometrical Survey** of India (begun 1802) measured the subcontinent with chains and theodolites; it established the heights of Himalayan peaks, including Peak XV — Mount Everest.
- The OSM sheets use the **WGS-84** datum, the same reference used by GPS satellites, so a phone's coordinates can be plotted directly on them.
- A 1 : 50,000 sheet covering 15′ × 15′ shows about 28 km from north to south; from west to east it is a little narrower (about 25 km at this latitude), because meridians draw closer together away from the equator.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 William Lambton (c. 1753–1823)
A British infantry officer and surveyor who began the **Great Trigonometrical Survey** of India in **1802** near Madras (now Chennai). Measuring a long, precise base line and building a chain of triangles northwards, his work laid the foundation on which every later Survey of India map — including the toposheets in your exam — was built.

### 6.2 Radhanath Sikdar (1813–1870)
A mathematician from Bengal who worked as a "computer" for the Great Trigonometrical Survey. In **1852** his calculations from survey observations showed that **Peak XV** was the highest mountain in the world — the peak later named Mount Everest.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **A map is a scaled model** — every centimetre stands for half a kilometre.
- **Location needs a system** — the grid gives every point an exact, shareable address.
- **Symbols are a language** — colour and shape tell you what the number or line means.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya:** the Survey of India's **North Eastern Zone** office is in **Shillong**; its sheets of the Khasi and Jaintia Hills show closely packed contours, the steep slopes you can feel on the road from Guwahati.
- **Phones and GPS:** a map app gives your position as coordinates — the same idea as a grid reference.
- **Disaster response:** rescue teams use grid references to tell helicopters exactly where to land.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

*Where a question names places, use the synthetic practice extract, Fig. 1.7.*

### Part II parts (2 marks each)

**Q1.** Distinguish between a four-figure and a six-figure grid reference.
**Answer:** A four-figure reference identifies a grid square by its south-west corner (e.g. 4178).
A six-figure reference identifies a point inside a square by adding tenths (e.g. 443769).

**Q2.** Name the three ways of showing scale on a toposheet.
**Answer:** Representative Fraction (1 : 50,000) and statement scale (2 cm to 1 km).
Linear (graphical) scale — a divided bar.

**Q3.** Why is the Representative Fraction called the universal scale?
**Answer:** It is a ratio with no units.
It can be read in any unit of measurement anywhere in the world.

**Q4.** Define contour and contour interval.
**Answer:** A contour is a line joining places of equal height above mean sea level.
The contour interval is the difference in height between two successive contours.

**Q5.** What do (a) .324 (b) ∆224 indicate?
**Answer:** (a) A spot height — the exact height of that point, 324 m.
(b) A triangulated height — the height of a survey station, 224 m.

**Q6.** What do (a) .8r on a dune (b) 30r against a well indicate?
**Answer:** (a) Relative height — the dune is about 8 m high.
(b) Relative depth — the well is 30 m deep.

**Answer:** (45 − 42) × (79 − 75) = 3 × 4.
= 12 km².

**Q8.** The distance between two villages is 5.4 cm on the map. Find the ground distance.

**Q9.** Give the direction of (a) Dhani from Ranapur (b) Ranapur from Dhani.

(b) North-east.

**Q10.** What do the following colours show: (a) yellow (b) brown?

**Q11.** What do (a) a red number on a metalled road (b) a black vertical line across the map represent?

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Toposheet — Survey of India (1767, Dehradun); 1 : 50,000; 15′ × 15′; 45D/7 = G43S7, 45D/10 = G43S10; read marginal information.
- Grid — eastings (vertical, increase east), northings (horizontal, increase north); 2 cm = 1 km; square = 1 km². Four-figure = square (SW corner); six-figure = point (tenths); easting first.
- Scale — RF 1 : 50,000 (universal, no units); statement 2 cm to 1 km; linear bar (survives enlargement). km = cm ÷ 2.
- Distance — ruler (straight), thread/divider (route), distance stones (difference), time = distance ÷ speed.
- Area — difference of eastings × difference of northings; more than half a square = 1.
- Direction — "A from B": stand at B; compare eastings and northings; 8 or 16 points.
- Heights — contour (brown; equal height), contour interval (20 m), spot height (dot), triangulated height (triangle), bench mark (BM), relative height r (slope, embankment, dune), r against a well (depth).
- Colours — red (grid, roads, settlements, distance stones), black (names, railways, dry streams, latitude/longitude), blue (water), brown (contours, sand), green (forest), yellow (cultivated), white (barren).

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Writing the northing first (0627 for 2706) | Easting first, then northing. |
| Giving the NE corner for a four-figure reference | Use the south-west corner. |
| Forgetting units (18, 3.35) | Write 18 km², 3.35 km. |
| Multiplying cm by 2 to get km | Divide: km = cm ÷ 2. |
| Reversing "from" in direction questions | Stand at the place after "from". |
| Calling .10r "height above sea level" | It is a relative height (foot to top). |
| Calling a black line across the map a grid line | Grid lines are red; the black line is a latitude or longitude. |
