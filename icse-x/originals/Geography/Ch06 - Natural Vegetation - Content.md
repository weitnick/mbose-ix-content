## 1. Complete Lesson

### What is natural vegetation?

- **Natural vegetation** is the **plant cover that grows by itself, without human help**, and has been **left undisturbed for a long time**. It is also called **virgin vegetation**.
- Crops, orchards and gardens are **not** natural vegetation — people plant and look after them.
- Natural vegetation may be **forests** (dense growth of trees), **grasslands** or **scrub** (bushes and thorny shrubs).
- Plants that are **native to India** are called **endemic** (indigenous); plants brought from outside are **exotic**.

**Why does India's natural vegetation vary so greatly?**
1. **Vast size and latitudinal extent** — India stretches from about **8° N to 37° N**, from the hot tropics to the cold Himalayas.
2. **Varied relief** — high mountains, plateaus, plains, deserts, coasts and islands.
3. **Varied climate** — rainfall ranges from over **1,000 cm** (Mawsynram, Meghalaya) to **under 15 cm** (western Rajasthan); temperature varies with latitude and altitude.
4. **Variety of soils** — alluvial, black, red, laterite, desert, mountain soils.
5. **Altitude** — on mountains, temperature and rainfall change with height, so vegetation changes too.

> The single most important control is **rainfall**. Where rain is heavy, forests are dense and evergreen; as rain decreases, forests become deciduous, then thorny scrub, then desert vegetation.

### Importance of forests

Forests are called **"green gold"** because they are as valuable to a country as gold. Their importance can be learnt in two groups.

**A. Environmental importance**
1. **Supply oxygen and purify the air** — trees give out oxygen and **absorb carbon dioxide** during photosynthesis; forests are **carbon sinks** and **reduce global warming**. They also trap dust and smoke.
2. **Moderate the climate** — they control **temperature and humidity**; **transpiration** from leaves adds moisture to the air and **helps bring rain**.
3. **Check soil erosion** — roots **bind the soil**; the canopy breaks the force of raindrops.
4. **Control floods** — forests **slow down run-off**, so rivers do not swell suddenly.
5. **Recharge groundwater** — water **percolates** slowly into the ground under a forest cover.
6. **Add humus** — fallen leaves decay and make the soil **fertile**.
7. **Habitat for wildlife** — forests shelter countless species of plants and animals and **maintain biodiversity and the ecological balance**.
8. **Check the spread of deserts** — belts of trees stop sand dunes from advancing.

**B. Economic importance**
1. **Timber** — for furniture, buildings, railway sleepers, boats and ships (teak, sal, shisham, deodar, sundari).
2. **Fuelwood and fodder** — most rural homes still use firewood; forests provide grass and leaves for cattle.
3. **Raw materials for industries** — **paper** (bamboo, softwood), **plywood**, **matchboxes**, **sports goods**, **rayon**, **packing cases**, **furniture**.
4. **Minor forest products** — **lac, gum, resin, honey, wax, tendu leaves** (for bidis), **cane**, **katha** (from khair), **tannin**, **sandalwood oil**, and **medicinal plants** (cinchona gives **quinine**).
5. **Employment and livelihood** — millions of tribal and forest-dwelling people depend on forests; forest-based industries employ many more.
6. **Tourism** — national parks and wildlife sanctuaries earn income.
7. **Foreign exchange** — from exports such as sandalwood products, lac and handicrafts.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q6(iv))* Forests are "green gold". Give three reasons in support of this statement.
**Solution:** Any three: forests **provide oxygen and purify the air**; they **absorb carbon dioxide** and reduce global warming; they allow **percolation of water** and recharge groundwater; they **control floods**; they are a **habitat** for numerous plants and animals; their **roots hold the soil** and check erosion; **transpiration induces rainfall**; they provide **raw materials to forest-based industries**.

### Types of natural vegetation in India

The ICSE syllabus studies **five types**. Each is closely **correlated with its environment** — mainly rainfall and temperature, and for mountain forests, altitude.

| Type (belt) | Annual rainfall | Temperature | Typical areas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical evergreen (rain forest) | **over 200 cm** | 25–27 °C average (20–30 °C) | Western slopes of the Western Ghats, North-East hills, Andaman & Nicobar Islands |
| Tropical deciduous (monsoon) | **100–200 cm** (dry type 70–100 cm) | 20–30 °C | Himalayan foothills, Chota Nagpur plateau, eastern slopes of the Western Ghats, Odisha |
| Tropical desert (thorn and scrub) | **under 50 cm** | 25–35 °C | Western Rajasthan, Kutch, south-west Punjab and Haryana, rain-shadow Deccan |
| Littoral (tidal / mangrove) | over 200 cm, with **tidal salt water** | 26–29 °C | Deltas — Ganga–Brahmaputra (Sundarbans), Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri; Andaman & Nicobar |
| Mountain (montane) | varies with altitude | falls with height | Himalayas; Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palni hills |

#### 1. Tropical evergreen forests (tropical rain forests)

**Climatic conditions**
- **Rainfall: over 200 cm** a year (150–350 cm), spread over most of the year.
- **Temperature: high**, about **25–27 °C** on average (between 20 °C and 30 °C).
- **Humidity: very high** — 70 to 95 per cent.
- A **short dry season**, if any.

**Distribution**
- The **western (windward) slopes of the Western Ghats** — western Maharashtra, Goa, western Karnataka and Kerala.
- The **hills of North-East India** — Meghalaya, Assam hills, Arunachal Pradesh, Nagaland, Manipur, Mizoram, Tripura.
- The **sub-montane (terai) region of West Bengal**.
- The **Andaman and Nicobar Islands** (and Lakshadweep).

**Characteristics**
1. **Dense** forests with **very tall trees** — **40 to 60 metres** high.
2. **Layered (multi-storeyed)** structure — emergent trees, a closed **canopy**, an **under-storey** and **shrubs** on the ground.
3. The canopy is so thick that **sunlight cannot reach the ground** — the **forest floor is dark**.
4. **Thick undergrowth** of climbers, creepers (lianas), canes, ferns and epiphytes.
5. **Evergreen** — the trees **shed their leaves at different times of the year**, so the forest **never looks bare**. There is **no single dry season** forcing all trees to drop leaves together.
6. **Mixed stands** — many species grow together; trees of **one kind do not grow in pure stands**.
7. **Hardwood** — the timber is **very hard, heavy and durable**.
8. **Broad leaves**, often with **drip tips** to shed heavy rain.

![Fig. 6.1 Layers (storeys) of a tropical evergreen forest](figures/g_ch06_fig01_evergreen_layers.png)

**Important trees** — **rosewood, ebony, mahogany, ironwood**, **cinchona**, **rubber**, **bamboo**, **cane**, toon, jamun, palms.

**Why are they difficult to exploit commercially?**
1. **Thick undergrowth** and **dense forest** — difficult to enter.
2. **Mixed stands** — useful trees are **scattered** among many others; no pure stands of one species.
3. **Hardwood** is **difficult to cut**, and too **heavy to float** down rivers.
4. **Heavy rainfall**, **marshy, waterlogged ground** and dark, damp conditions.
5. **Inaccessible** — **lack of roads and transport**.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q6(i))* While visiting the Andaman Islands, Sneha comes across tropical evergreen forests characterised by dark forest floors. (a) Why are the forest floors dark? (b) State the rainfall requirements for this type of forest.
**Solution:** (a) The **thick canopy blocks sunlight**. (b) **More than 200 cm** of annual rainfall.

#### 2. Tropical deciduous forests (tropical monsoon forests)

These are the **most widespread forests of India**. They are called **monsoon forests** because they grow in the areas of **monsoon climate**, and **deciduous** because their trees **shed their leaves** in the dry season.

**Climatic conditions**
- **Rainfall: 100–200 cm** a year, mostly in the rainy (south-west monsoon) season, followed by a long dry season.
- **Temperature: 20–30 °C** (hot summers).
- **Humidity: moderate** — 50 to 70 per cent.

**Two sub-types**
| Moist deciduous | Dry deciduous |
|---|---|
| Rainfall **100–200 cm** | Rainfall **70–100 cm** |
| Foothills of the Himalayas (Shiwaliks, bhabar and terai), **Chota Nagpur plateau** (Jharkhand), **Odisha**, Chhattisgarh, **eastern slopes of the Western Ghats** | Rainier parts of the peninsular plateau, plains of **Uttar Pradesh and Bihar**, parts of Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu |
| Sal, teak, shisham, sandalwood, bamboo | Teak, sal, peepal, neem, tendu, khair |

**Distribution** (any region) — the **foothills of the Himalayas (Shiwalik range)**, the **Chota Nagpur plateau**, the **eastern slopes of the Western Ghats**, and large parts of **Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Odisha, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh and Bihar**.

**Characteristics**
1. Trees **shed their leaves for about 6–8 weeks** in the **dry summer (March–April)** to **conserve moisture** — they reduce the loss of water through **transpiration**. The forest then looks **bare**.
2. Trees grow in **pure stands** — large patches of **one species** (sal or teak).
3. **Less dense** than evergreen forests, with **less undergrowth**; trees are 30–45 m high.
4. **Hardwood** that is **durable**, **valuable** and **easy to work**.
5. **Broad leaves**.

**Important trees** — **teak, sal, shisham, sandalwood, mahua, khair, arjun, palas, tendu, myrobalan (harra), bamboo**.

**Why are they commercially the most important (most exploited) forests?**
1. Trees grow in **pure stands** — easy to locate and cut.
2. They yield **valuable, durable hardwood** — **teak, sal, shisham** — for furniture, construction, railway sleepers and ship-building.
3. They are **easily accessible** — **less undergrowth**, flatter land, **easy transport**.
4. They yield **many other products** — **lac, gum, resin, tendu leaves, honey, bamboo for paper, sandalwood oil**.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2019, Q5(d)(iii))* Explain why the trees in monsoon deciduous forests shed their leaves for about 6–8 weeks during March and April.
**Solution:** There is **not enough moisture** for the leaves to withstand the **dry weather**; the trees shed their leaves **to conserve moisture** and **reduce the loss of water through transpiration**, and so survive the **heat and drought** of spring and early summer.

#### 3. Tropical desert vegetation (thorn and scrub forests)

**Climatic conditions**
- **Rainfall: less than 50 cm** a year (in the driest parts **under 25 cm**); rain is **scanty, seasonal and unreliable**, with **long dry spells**.
- **Temperature: high**, **25–35 °C** (very hot summers).
- **Humidity: low** — under 50 per cent.

**Distribution**
- **Western Rajasthan** (the **Thar Desert**).
- **Kutch and Saurashtra** (Gujarat).
- **South-western Punjab and Haryana**.
- The **rain-shadow interior of the Deccan plateau** (parts of Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh) and parts of Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh.

**Characteristics and adaptations** — the plants are **xerophytes** (plants adapted to dry conditions):
1. **Scattered** plants, **stunted** (short) trees and **thorny bushes** — not enough water for tall growth.
2. **Long, deep roots** — to **draw water from deep underground**, since rainfall is scanty.
3. **Small, thick, waxy leaves**, or leaves **modified into spines/thorns** — to **reduce transpiration** (loss of water).
4. **Thick, fleshy stems** — to **store water** (succulents such as cactus).
5. **Sunken stomata**, rolled-up leaves, a woody stem — all reduce water loss.
6. Many are **drought-tolerant**; some acacias live in partnership with stinging ants that protect them.

![Fig. 6.3 How desert plants and mangroves are adapted to their environment](figures/g_ch06_fig03_adaptations.png)

**Important plants** — **babool (acacia), kikar, khair, khejri, cactus, euphorbia, date palm, ber, phog**, and coarse grasses.

**Uses** — fuelwood; fodder for camels, goats and sheep; **gum** (from babool); khejri pods and ber fruit for food; date palms in oases.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q6(iii))* (a) Why are long and deep roots a typical characteristic of desert vegetation? (b) State the rainfall condition required for this type of vegetation to evolve. (c) Mention one region in India where this vegetation is predominant.
**Solution:** (a) The long roots help to **draw water from deep underneath**, as rainfall is **scanty**. (b) **Less than 25 cm** of rain per annum. (c) Any one: parts of **Rajasthan** / interior parts of the **Deccan plateau** / **south-western Punjab** / the **Saurashtra** region of Gujarat / **Kutch**.

#### 4. Littoral forests (tidal forests / mangrove forests)

**Littoral** means **of the sea-shore**. These forests grow in the **deltas and coastal marshes** that are **flooded by the tides** twice a day.

**Climatic and other conditions**
- **Temperature: 26–29 °C**; **rainfall: over 200 cm**; a hot, humid **coastal climate**.
- **Tidal influence** — the land is **flooded with salt water** at high tide.
- **Soft, compact, waterlogged, salty mud** with **little oxygen**.

**Distribution**
- The **Sundarbans** — the delta of the **Ganga and Brahmaputra** in **West Bengal** (named after the **sundari** tree).
- The deltas of the **Mahanadi** (Odisha), **Godavari** and **Krishna** (Andhra Pradesh) and **Kaveri** (Tamil Nadu).
- Coastal marshes of **Gujarat** (Gulf of Kutch) and the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**.

**Characteristics and adaptations**
1. Trees have **stilt (prop) roots** that arch down into the soft mud — they **support the tree against the tides**.
2. Many have **breathing roots (pneumatophores)** that **grow upward above the mud** to **absorb oxygen**, because the **waterlogged, compact, salty soil** has hardly any air.
3. Stilt roots are **under water at high tide** and exposed at low tide.
4. Trees are **salt-tolerant** (halophytes), with **thick, leathery leaves**.
5. Seeds often **germinate while still on the tree** (vivipary), so seedlings can root quickly in the mud.
6. The forest is **dense** and the roots are **tangled**, and the ground is **marshy** — so it is **very difficult to move through tidal forests**.

**Important trees** — **sundari** (hard, durable wood used for **boat-making** and construction), **mangrove** (rhizophora), **keora, goran, agar, hogla, nipa palm**, casuarina.

**Uses and importance**
1. **Timber and fuelwood** — sundari wood is **hard and durable**, used for **boats and buildings**.
2. **Tannin** from mangrove bark (used in leather tanning).
3. **Protect the coast** from **cyclones, storm surges, tsunamis and coastal erosion**.
4. **Breeding ground for fish, prawns and crabs**; the Sundarbans are the home of the **Royal Bengal tiger**.

#### 5. Mountain forests (montane forests)

On mountains, **temperature falls with height** (about 6.4 °C per 1,000 m) and **rainfall changes with altitude** and with the side of the slope. So the vegetation changes in **belts**, from tropical at the foot to alpine near the snow — just as it changes from the Equator to the Poles.

**A. Himalayan mountain forests**

| Altitude (approx.) | Type | Trees |
|---|---|---|
| Foothills up to ~1,000 m | Tropical moist deciduous | Sal, teak, shisham, bamboo |
| 1,000–2,000 m | **Wet temperate** (evergreen broad-leaved) | **Oak, chestnut**, laurel, maple |
| 2,000–3,000 m | **Temperate coniferous** | **Pine, deodar, silver fir, spruce, cedar**, blue pine |
| 3,000–4,000 m | **Alpine** | **Birch, juniper, rhododendron**, silver fir; **alpine meadows (grasslands)** used for summer grazing |
| Above ~4,000 m | Tundra-like | **Mosses and lichens**; then permanent snow |

![Fig. 6.2 Mountain vegetation changes with altitude (Himalayas, schematic)](figures/g_ch06_fig02_mountain_belts.png)

- The **Eastern Himalayas** get **more rain** than the Western Himalayas, so their forests are denser and the evergreen belts reach higher.
- **Coniferous trees** — **conical in shape**, with **sloping branches** and **needle-shaped leaves** — so that **snow slides off easily** without breaking the branches; the needle leaves also **reduce transpiration** in the cold, dry winter. Their **softwood** is used for **paper, pulp, matchboxes, packing cases** and building.

**B. Mountain forests of the south (Peninsular hills)**
- Found in the **Nilgiri, Anaimalai and Palni hills** and the higher Western Ghats.
- The vegetation **varies with altitude**: **tropical deciduous and evergreen** forests on the lower slopes, **temperate forests** higher up.
- The temperate forests of the Nilgiris are called **sholas** — patches of dense, stunted evergreen forest in the valleys, surrounded by **rolling grasslands**. Trees include **magnolia, laurel, cinchona and wattle**; **eucalyptus** has been planted.
- Their vegetation is therefore **mixed** — deciduous, evergreen and temperate/coniferous types.

**Why do mountains have varied vegetation?** Because **temperature and rainfall vary with altitude**: they are both **high at lower altitudes**, which therefore have **denser vegetation**; higher up, the cold allows only conifers, then alpine plants and mosses.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2024, Q6(iii))* Give a reason: (a) Mountains have varied vegetation. (b) Stilt-like / aerial roots are a typical feature of littoral forest. (c) Tropical evergreen forests do not look bare at any time of the year.
**Solution:** (a) In a mountain, **temperature and rainfall vary with altitude**; both are high at lower altitudes, which therefore have denser vegetation. (b) The marshy ground of tidal forests has **very compact soil inundated with salt water**; the roots grow **above the ground to absorb oxygen**. (c) Rain falls **throughout the year**, so the trees **do not shed their leaves at the same time** — the forest is never bare.

### The five belts compared

| Feature | Evergreen | Deciduous | Desert | Littoral | Mountain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rainfall | > 200 cm | 100–200 cm | < 50 cm | > 200 cm + tides | varies with height |
| Density | very dense, layered | less dense | scattered, stunted | dense, tangled roots | changes in belts |
| Leaves | broad, shed at different times | broad, all shed in dry season | small, waxy, spines | thick, leathery | needles (conifers) |
| Stands | mixed | **pure** | scattered | mixed | conifers often pure |
| Wood | very hard | hard, durable | thorny, little timber | hard (sundari) | **softwood** (conifers) |
| Trees | rosewood, ebony, mahogany | teak, sal, shisham | babool, khejri, cactus | sundari, mangrove, keora | oak, pine, deodar, fir, birch |
| Commercial value | low (hard to exploit) | **highest** | low | local (boats, fuel) | high (softwood, paper) |
| Key adaptation | drip-tip leaves; layers | leaf-fall in summer | long roots, spines | stilt and breathing roots | conical shape |

### Deforestation — why the forest cover is shrinking

**Deforestation** is the **cutting down or clearing of forests** on a large scale. Its causes:
1. **Rising population** — forests are cleared for **farmland, houses and villages**.
2. **Urbanisation and industrialisation** — land for **towns, factories** and roads.
3. **Mining and quarrying** — forests above mineral deposits are removed.
4. **Multipurpose projects and dams** — large areas are **submerged**.
5. **Roads and railways** cut through forests.
6. **Demand for timber, fuelwood and furniture**.
7. **Shifting (jhum) cultivation** — "slash and burn" farming cleared large forest areas in the past, especially in the North-East.
8. **Overgrazing** — animals eat young plants, so the forest cannot regenerate.
9. **Forest fires** and **illegal felling (smuggling)**.

**Effects** — soil erosion, floods, falling groundwater, less rainfall, loss of wildlife and biodiversity, spread of deserts, global warming, loss of livelihood for forest dwellers.

### Forest conservation

**Forest conservation** means the **protection, careful use and renewal of forests** so that they last for the future. The National Forest Policy aims at keeping **one-third (33 per cent)** of India's area under forests (two-thirds in the hills).

**Methods named in the syllabus**

| Method | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Afforestation** | **Planting trees on land that had no forest** before — barren land, wasteland, desert margins | Planting trees along the edge of the Thar |
| **Reforestation (re-afforestation)** | **Replanting trees where forests have been cut**; in India, **two saplings are planted for every tree felled** | Replanting a felled forest coupe |
| **Social forestry** | **Planting trees on public and community land** — **along roads, railway lines, canal banks**, village commons, school compounds — **with the help of local people**, to meet their **fuelwood, fodder and timber** needs | Strip plantations along a highway |
| **Farm forestry** (part of social forestry) | Farmers plant trees **on their own farmland** — field boundaries, unused corners | Eucalyptus or poplar on field edges |
| **Agroforestry** | A **sustainable system** of growing **trees together with agricultural crops and animal rearing** on the **same piece of land** | Fruit trees with pulses and goats |
| **Van Mahotsav** | The **national tree-planting festival**, held every year in the **first week of July** (started in **1950** by **K. M. Munshi**) | Schools and offices plant saplings |

**How social forestry helps to conserve forests** — it **involves local communities** in planting and protecting trees; by **providing fuelwood, fodder, timber and minor products** to rural people it **reduces the pressure on natural forests**; it **educates and raises awareness**; it supports **local cottage industries** and so **lessens dependence** on natural forests.

**Why is Van Mahotsav significant?** It **expands the green cover**, **restores degraded land**, and **creates awareness** about protecting and conserving forests.

**Other conservation measures**
- **Controlled grazing** and **fire lines** (cleared strips that stop forest fires spreading).
- **Checking shifting cultivation**; giving jhum farmers settled land.
- **Protected areas** — **national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves**.
- **Silviculture** — scientific raising and management of forests.
- **Joint Forest Management** (1990) — the Forest Department and village communities protect forests together and share the produce.
- **People's movements** — the **Chipko movement** (1973, Uttarakhand), in which villagers **hugged trees** to stop them being felled.
- **Using substitutes** for wood (LPG, biogas, solar cookers; steel and aluminium furniture) and **recycling paper**.

**Why has forest cover increased gradually in recent times?** — There is now a **check on deforestation**; **shifting agriculture** has been restricted; and **government initiatives** such as **agroforestry, farm forestry, social forestry, Van Mahotsav, afforestation and re-afforestation** have added tree cover.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q6(iv))* (a) Name a forest conservation method that involves the local community. (b) Which primitive system of agriculture led to large-scale destruction of forest in the past? (c) Name the process which involves planting two saplings for each tree that is felled.
**Solution:** (a) **Social forestry**. (b) **Jhum cultivation** / shifting agriculture / the slash-and-burn method of farming. (c) **Reforestation** / afforestation.

### Summary of the chapter

- **Natural vegetation** — plants growing on their own, undisturbed; varies because of India's **size, relief, climate (rainfall above all), soils and altitude**.
- **Importance** — oxygen, carbon sink, climate, rain, soil, floods, groundwater, humus, wildlife; timber, fuel, fodder, raw materials, minor products, jobs, tourism — **"green gold"**.
- **Evergreen** — > 200 cm, 25–27 °C; western Ghat slopes, NE, A&N; dense, layered, dark floor, mixed stands, hardwood, never bare; rosewood, ebony, mahogany; **hard to exploit**.
- **Deciduous (monsoon)** — 100–200 cm; foothills, Chota Nagpur, eastern Ghat slopes; shed leaves 6–8 weeks (March–April) to save water; **pure stands**, teak, sal, shisham; **commercially most important**.
- **Desert** — < 50 cm, 25–35 °C; western Rajasthan, Kutch, SW Punjab; xerophytes — **long roots, spines, waxy leaves, fleshy stems**, stunted; babool, khejri, cactus.
- **Littoral** — tides, salt water, deltas (**Sundarbans**, Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna); **stilt and breathing roots**; sundari (boats), mangrove; protect coasts.
- **Mountain** — vegetation changes with altitude because temperature and rainfall change: deciduous → oak/chestnut → **conifers (conical)** → alpine → mosses → snow; Nilgiri **sholas**.
- **Deforestation** — population, farming, towns, industry, mining, dams, timber, jhum, overgrazing, fires.
- **Conservation** — **afforestation, reforestation, social forestry, farm forestry, agroforestry, Van Mahotsav**; controlled grazing, fire lines, protected areas, Chipko, JFM.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Rainfall decides the forest
Follow the rain down from the Western Ghats to the Thar: over 200 cm → **evergreen**; 100–200 cm → **moist deciduous**; 70–100 cm → **dry deciduous**; 50–70 cm → **thorn and scrub**; under 50 cm → **desert**. The trees change from tall, dense and green to short, thorny and scattered.

### Section 2 — Why leaves fall, or do not
Leaves lose water through **transpiration**. Where it rains all year (**evergreen**), there is no need to drop them all together — each tree sheds at its own time. Where a long **dry season** comes (**deciduous**), all trees drop their leaves in March–April to save water. In the **desert**, leaves shrink to **spines**.

### Section 3 — Roots tell the story
**Desert**: long, deep roots go **down** to find water. **Littoral**: roots come **up** (breathing roots) to find **air**, and arch out (stilt roots) to **stand firm** in soft mud against tides.

### Section 4 — Mountains are like a journey to the Poles
Climbing a Himalayan slope is like travelling from the tropics to the Arctic: deciduous → broad-leaved temperate → coniferous → alpine → mosses → snow. **Conical** conifers shed snow.

### Section 5 — Exploit or conserve?
Deciduous forests are the easiest to exploit, so they are the most depleted. Conservation methods differ by **where** trees are planted: new land (afforestation), cut land (reforestation), public land with the people (social forestry), the farmer's own land (farm forestry), or among crops and animals (agroforestry).

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 K. M. Munshi (1887–1971)
Kanaiyalal Maneklal Munshi was a lawyer, writer and freedom fighter. As Union Minister for Agriculture and Food he started **Van Mahotsav** in July **1950**, turning tree-planting into a national festival.

### 6.2 Gaura Devi and the Chipko movement
In **1974**, at **Reni** village in the Garhwal Himalayas (now Uttarakhand), **Gaura Devi** led the women of the village to **hug the trees** and stop contractors from felling them. The **Chipko movement** ("chipko" = to cling), begun in 1973 with Chandi Prasad Bhatt and later carried forward by Sunderlal Bahuguna, made forest conservation a people's cause.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Vegetation mirrors climate** — read the leaves and roots of a plant and you can tell how much rain falls there.
- **Forests are both a resource and a shield** — they give timber and income, and they protect soil, water, air and coasts.
- **What is easiest to use is quickest to lose** — the accessible deciduous forests have been cut the most.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Meghalaya and the North-East:** sacred groves such as Mawphlang in the Khasi Hills are protected by village tradition; jhum with short cycles has cleared many hill forests.
- **Cyclones on the east coast:** villages behind thick mangroves suffered less damage in past cyclones — one reason mangrove planting is now encouraged.
- **Your school:** Van Mahotsav planting is real social forestry when the saplings are cared for after the photograph is taken.

#### Exercise 6A — Natural vegetation and forests

**Q1.** What is natural vegetation?
**Answer:** Plant cover that grows by itself, without human help, and has been left undisturbed for a long time.

**Q2.** Give two reasons for the variety of natural vegetation in India.
**Answer:** Variety of climate (especially rainfall) and relief; also the vast size and latitudinal extent, and the variety of soils.

**Answer:** Lac and gum (also resin, honey, tendu leaves, cane, katha).

#### Exercise 6B — Vegetation belts

**Q4.** Why is the floor of an evergreen forest dark?
**Answer:** The thick canopy of tall trees blocks the sunlight.

**Q5.** Name two trees of the tropical deciduous forest.
**Answer:** Teak and sal (also shisham, sandalwood).

**Q6.** Why do desert plants have small, waxy leaves?
**Answer:** To reduce the loss of water by transpiration.

**Q7.** Name the forest belt of the Sundarbans and one adaptation of its trees.
**Answer:** Littoral (tidal / mangrove) forest; stilt or breathing roots.

**Q8.** Why are conifers conical in shape?
**Answer:** So that snow slides off the sloping branches without breaking them.

#### Exercise 6C — Conservation

**Q9.** Distinguish between afforestation and reforestation.
**Answer:** Afforestation is planting trees on land that had no forest; reforestation is replanting trees where a forest was cut.

**Q10.** What is agroforestry?
**Answer:** Growing trees together with crops and livestock on the same piece of land.

**Q11.** When is Van Mahotsav celebrated?
**Answer:** In the first week of July every year.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (2 marks) Distinguish between social forestry and agroforestry.
**Answer:** Social forestry — trees on public/community land (roadsides, canal banks, village commons) planted with the people for their fuel and fodder. Agroforestry — trees grown together with crops and animals on the same farm.

**Q2.** (3 marks) Name the vegetation belt: (a) teak and sal in pure stands (b) babool and khejri (c) deodar and pine.
**Answer:** (a) Tropical deciduous. (b) Tropical desert. (c) Mountain (coniferous belt).

**Q3.** (3 marks) Give reasons: (a) the Sundarbans protect the West Bengal coast (b) the Western Himalayas have fewer evergreen forests than the Eastern Himalayas (c) deciduous forests have been reduced the most.
**Answer:** (a) Dense mangroves break the force of cyclones, storm surges and waves and hold the mud. (b) The Western Himalayas get less rainfall. (c) They are accessible and in pure stands of valuable timber, so they were cut the most.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part II parts (2 marks each)

**Q1.** State the rainfall and temperature conditions of tropical evergreen forests.
**Answer:** Rainfall over 200 cm a year.
Temperature about 25–27 °C (20–30 °C), with high humidity.

**Q2.** Why is the floor of an evergreen forest dark, and why is the forest never bare?
**Answer:** The thick canopy blocks sunlight.
Trees shed their leaves at different times, as rain falls all year.

**Q3.** Mention two adaptations of tropical desert plants.
**Answer:** Long, deep roots to reach groundwater.
Small, waxy leaves or spines to reduce transpiration (also fleshy stems).

**Q4.** State two conditions for the growth of littoral forests.
**Answer:** Tidal influence — salty, waterlogged soil of deltas and coasts.
Temperature 26–29 °C and rainfall over 200 cm.

**Q5.** How does social forestry help to conserve forests? (Two ways)
**Answer:** It involves local communities in planting and protecting trees.
It meets their needs of fuelwood and fodder, reducing pressure on natural forests.

**Q6.** State two reasons why Van Mahotsav is significant.
**Answer:** It expands the green cover and restores degraded land.
It creates awareness about protecting forests.

**Q7.** Name two trees each of the deciduous and the evergreen forests.
**Answer:** Deciduous — teak, sal (also shisham).
Evergreen — rosewood, ebony (also mahogany).

**Q8.** Give two reasons for the great diversity of natural vegetation in India.
**Answer:** Diverse climate, especially rainfall.
Varied relief and soils, and vast latitudinal extent.

**Q9.** Identify the methods: (a) trees + crops + animals on one plot (b) trees along roads and railways.
**Answer:** (a) Agroforestry.
(b) Social forestry.

**Q10.** Mention two causes of the decrease in India's forest cover.
**Answer:** Clearing land for farming, housing and industry as population rises.
Mining and dams (also timber demand, shifting cultivation, overgrazing).

**Q11.** How is the tropical monsoon forest useful to the economy? (Two points)
**Answer:** It yields durable hardwood (teak, sal) for furniture and construction.
It supplies lac, gum, resin, honey and tendu leaves to industries.

**Q12.** How do forests help maintain air quality?
**Answer:** They give out oxygen and absorb carbon dioxide (a carbon sink).
They trap dust and smoke and purify the air.

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Give reasons: (a) desert vegetation has long roots (b) trees at high altitudes are conical (c) monsoon forests are called deciduous.
**Answer:** (a) To draw water from deep underground, as rain is scanty.
(b) Snow slides off the sloping branches.
(c) Their trees shed their leaves in the dry season.

**Q14.** With reference to littoral forests: name one tree, one adaptation and one region.
**Answer:** Sundari (also mangrove, keora).
Stilt roots / breathing roots.
The Sundarbans (also Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna deltas).

**Q15.** State three differences between tropical deciduous and tropical desert vegetation.
**Answer:** Deciduous: tall trees in pure stands; desert: stunted, scattered plants.
Deciduous: broad leaves shed in summer; desert: small waxy leaves or spines.
Deciduous: 100–200 cm rain; desert: under 50 cm.

**Q16.** Give reasons: (a) evergreen forests are hard to exploit (b) deciduous trees are most exploited (c) tidal forests have aerial roots.
**Answer:** (a) Thick undergrowth, mixed stands, hardwood, poor access.
(b) Pure stands of valuable timber, easy access.
(c) To take in oxygen from the air in waterlogged soil.

**Q17.** Why is it essential to conserve forests? (Three reasons)
**Answer:** They give oxygen and absorb carbon dioxide.
They check soil erosion and floods and recharge groundwater.
They shelter wildlife and give livelihood and raw materials.

**Q18.** (a) Name a conservation method involving the local community. (b) Name the farming that destroyed forests in the past. (c) Name the process of planting two saplings for each tree felled.
**Answer:** (a) Social forestry.
(b) Jhum (shifting) cultivation.
(c) Reforestation.

**Q19.** Give reasons: (a) mountains have varied vegetation (b) evergreen forests are never bare (c) deciduous forests look bare in summer.
**Answer:** (a) Temperature and rainfall change with altitude.
(b) Trees shed leaves at different times; rain all year.
(c) Trees shed leaves to save moisture in the dry season.

**Q20.** Name the natural vegetation of: (a) western slopes of the Western Ghats (b) the Nilgiris (c) western Rajasthan.
**Answer:** (a) Tropical evergreen.
(b) Mountain (shola forests and grasslands).
(c) Tropical desert (thorn and scrub).

**Q21.** Mention three ways in which forests help the economic development of India.
**Answer:** Timber for construction and furniture.
Raw materials for paper, plywood and match industries.
Minor products, employment and tourism.

**Q22.** Explain: (a) afforestation (b) agroforestry (c) farm forestry.
**Answer:** (a) Planting trees on land that had no forest.
(b) Growing trees with crops and animals on the same land.
(c) Farmers planting trees on their own farmland.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (A school trip)** Your class visits a forest in Jharkhand in April and finds the trees almost bare. A friend thinks the trees are dead. Explain.
**Model answer:** They are tropical deciduous trees such as sal and teak, which shed their leaves for 6–8 weeks in the dry season to save water; new leaves come before the monsoon.

**Q2. (A timber merchant)** A merchant wants teak in large quantity at low cost. Which forest should the merchant go to, and why not the Kerala rain forest?
**Model answer:** The tropical deciduous forests of Madhya Pradesh or Maharashtra — teak grows in pure stands and is accessible. In the rain forest, trees are mixed, undergrowth is thick and transport is difficult.

**Q3. (A cyclone-prone coast)** A coastal district in Odisha wants to reduce cyclone damage. Suggest one vegetation measure and explain.
**Model answer:** Plant and protect mangroves along the coast and creeks; their dense roots break waves and storm surges and hold the soil.

**Q4. (A dry village)** A village in Kutch needs fuelwood and fodder but has little forest. Suggest a plan.
**Model answer:** Social and farm forestry with drought-hardy trees such as khejri and babool along field edges, roads and the village common, managed by the village; controlled grazing to let saplings grow.

**Q5. (A Himalayan trek)** On a trek from Dehradun to a high pass, you pass sal, then oak, then deodar, then birch and grassy meadows. Explain the change.
**Model answer:** Temperature falls and rainfall changes with height, so the vegetation changes from deciduous to wet temperate, coniferous and alpine.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Natural vegetation — grows on its own, undisturbed; diversity from size, relief, climate (rain above all), soils, altitude.
- Forests: oxygen, carbon sink, climate, rain, soil, floods, groundwater, humus, wildlife; timber, fuel, fodder, raw materials, minor products, jobs, tourism.
- Evergreen: > 200 cm, 25–27 °C; Western Ghat west slopes, NE, A&N; tall, layered, dark floor, mixed stands, hardwood, never bare; rosewood, ebony, mahogany; hard to exploit.
- Deciduous: 100–200 cm; foothills, Chota Nagpur, east slopes of Western Ghats; leaf-fall March–April; pure stands; teak, sal, shisham; most exploited.
- Desert: < 50 cm, 25–35 °C; W Rajasthan, Kutch, SW Punjab; xerophytes — long roots, spines, waxy leaves, fleshy stems, stunted; babool, khejri, cactus.
- Littoral: tides, salt water, 26–29 °C; Sundarbans, Mahanadi–Godavari–Krishna deltas, A&N; stilt and breathing roots; sundari, mangrove; coastal shield.
- Mountain: changes with altitude (temperature, rain); sal → oak → conifers (conical) → alpine → mosses → snow; Nilgiri sholas.
- Deforestation: population, farms, towns, industry, mining, dams, timber, jhum, overgrazing, fires.
- Conservation: afforestation, reforestation (2 saplings per tree), social and farm forestry, agroforestry, Van Mahotsav (July); fire lines, controlled grazing, protected areas, Chipko, JFM.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| "Sundarbans" when asked for the belt | Write **Littoral / tidal forest**. |
| Listing states when a region is asked | Name the region — e.g. western slopes of the Western Ghats. |
| "Evergreen trees never shed leaves" | They shed leaves **at different times**, so the forest is never bare. |
| "Mountains have varied vegetation because of altitude" only | Say **temperature and rainfall change with altitude**. |
| Giving climatic conditions when asked about stilt roots | Explain the **waterlogged, airless, salty soil** and the need for oxygen and support. |
| Naming a forest policy as a "method" | Give a method — afforestation, social forestry, agroforestry. |
| Writing only characteristics when asked for economic usefulness | Give **uses** — timber, raw materials, minor products, jobs. |
| Mixing up afforestation and reforestation | Afforestation: new land; reforestation: replanting cut forest. |
