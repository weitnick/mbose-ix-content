## 1. Complete Lesson

### Cash crops

**Cash crops** are grown **for sale** — as **raw materials for industries** (sugar, textiles, oil mills, jute mills) or for **export** (tea, cotton, oilseed cake). They earn **money and foreign exchange**, but many need **heavy inputs** (fertilisers, irrigation, labour).

![Fig. 12.3 Climatic needs of the cash crops (approximate ranges)](figures/g_ch12_fig03_cash_crop_climate.png)

### 1. Sugarcane

**Importance** — the source of **sugar, gur (jaggery) and khandsari**; India is the **second-largest producer** of sugarcane in the world.

**Climatic conditions**
- A **tropical crop** — **hot and humid**; **temperature 20–30 °C**.
- **Rainfall 100–200 cm**; **irrigation** where rain is less (north India).
- A **long growing season** — **10 to 18 months** (it is in the field for about a year).
- **Frost is harmful**; the hot, dry **loo** of the north harms it; **bright sunshine** at ripening raises the sugar content.

**Soil** — **deep, fertile, well-drained loamy alluvium** (Ganga plains) or **black soil** (Maharashtra); sugarcane **exhausts the soil**, so it needs **heavy manuring and fertilisers**.

**Methods of cultivation**
- Propagated by **setts** — **pieces of stem with buds**, planted in **furrows**.
- Needs much **water, fertiliser and labour** (planting, earthing up, harvesting).
- **Ratooning** — after the cane is cut, the **stubble left in the ground sprouts again** and gives a **second (ratoon) crop** without fresh planting.
  - **Why popular?** The ratoon crop **matures faster**, **saves time and money** (no new setts or ploughing) and needs **less labour** — it is **cost-effective**.
  - **Why not more than two ratoons?** The **yield declines**; the **risk of pests** increases; **roots may be infected with diseases**; the **sucrose (sugar) content falls**; sugarcane **exhausts the soil**.

![Fig. 12.4 Sugarcane — setts, ratooning and by-products](figures/g_ch12_fig04_sugarcane.png)

**Distribution** — **Uttar Pradesh** (the **largest producer** — Ganga–Yamuna doab, Rohilkhand), **Maharashtra**, **Karnataka**, **Tamil Nadu**, **Bihar**, **Gujarat**, **Andhra Pradesh**.

**Why has Maharashtra emerged as an important sugarcane state?** **Black soil** that holds moisture; a **tropical, maritime climate** (20–30 °C, no frost, **no loo**); a **longer crushing season**; **irrigation**; **scientifically managed cooperative farming** and sugar mills **close to the plantations**, with **capital investment** from the sugar cooperatives. Peninsular India gets **higher yields and sugar content** than the north.

**By-products of the sugar industry** — **bagasse** (crushed cane fibre — **fuel in sugar mills**, paper, board), **molasses** (alcohol, yeast), **press mud** (manure).

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2025, Q9(ii))* Give any two reasons why Maharashtra has emerged as an important sugarcane-producing state.
**Solution:** Any two: **black soil**; a **tropical / maritime climate** — **temperature 20–30 °C**, **rain 100–200 cm**, **frost-free days**; **scientifically managed / cooperative farming**; **capital investment** from the sugar lobby; the **sugar industry located close to the plantations**; **absence of loo**.

### 2. Oilseeds

India is one of the largest producers of oilseeds, yet it **imports edible oil**. The main oilseeds are **groundnut, mustard (rapeseed) and soybean** (also sesame, sunflower, linseed, castor). Oilseeds give **edible oil**, **vanaspati**, oil for **soap, paints and lubricants**, and **oil cake** — a rich **cattle feed and manure**.

| Feature | **Groundnut** | **Mustard (rapeseed)** | **Soybean** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Season | **Kharif** (also rabi in the south) | **Rabi** | **Kharif** |
| Temperature | **20–30 °C**; **cannot withstand frost** | **10–25 °C** (cool) | 20–30 °C |
| Rainfall | **50–75 cm** (about 70 cm; 50–100 cm) | **25–40 cm** (or irrigation) | 50–75 cm |
| Soil | **Light sandy loam**, red, black — **well-drained, loose** (pods form **underground**) | **Light loamy** soils | **Well-drained black and loamy** soils |
| Leading states | **Gujarat** (Saurashtra) — **largest**; Rajasthan, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka | **Rajasthan** — **largest**; Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Madhya Pradesh, Gujarat | **Madhya Pradesh** ("Soya State") — **largest**; Maharashtra, Rajasthan |
| Special uses | Oil, **roasted nuts**; residue (**oil cake**) is **cattle feed and manure** | **Cooking oil** of north and east India; leaves as greens | **Protein (about 40 %) and oil (about 20 %)**; soy foods, cattle feed; fixes nitrogen |

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2025, Q9(i))* Mention two climatic conditions that favour the cultivation of groundnut.
**Solution:** Any two: **temperature 20–30 °C** (about 25 °C); it **cannot withstand frost**; **rainfall 50–100 cm** (about 70 cm).

### 3. Cotton

**Importance** — the raw material of India's **largest traditional industry (cotton textiles)**; India is among the **largest producers of cotton** in the world. Its seed gives **cottonseed oil** and **cattle-feed cake**.

**Climatic conditions**
- A **kharif** crop of **tropical and sub-tropical** areas.
- **Temperature 21–30 °C** — **uniformly high** during the growing period.
- **Rainfall 50–100 cm** (light, well-distributed); **irrigation** in drier parts (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan).
- At least **200 frost-free days**.
- **Bright sunshine** and **dry weather** when the **bolls open** and at **picking** — rain then spoils the fibre.

**Why is cotton grown as a kharif crop?** It needs **uniformly high temperatures (21–30 °C)** and about **200 frost-free days** during its growth — conditions of the **tropical summer**; frost is harmful; rain is less likely at harvest.

**Soil** — **black (regur) soil** of the Deccan — **moisture-retentive** and **rich in lime and potash** — is ideal; also **alluvial** soils (north) and **red** soils with irrigation.

**Cotton is a labour-intensive crop** — **seeds are sown by hand** on the farm; the crop must be **protected against bollworms and weevils**, so **pesticides are sprayed**; it **exhausts the soil**, so **fertilisers** are applied; **picking** cannot be mechanised easily and goes on by hand for about **three months**; then **ginning and baling**.

**Ginning** — the process of **separating the cotton fibre (lint) from the seeds** (and short linters), done in **ginning mills**; the fibre is then pressed into **bales**.

**Staple** — the **length of the fibre**: **long staple** (finest; 27 mm and over), **medium** and **short staple**.

**Distribution** — **Gujarat** (usually the **largest producer**), **Maharashtra** (the **largest area** under cotton — Vidarbha, Khandesh), **Telangana**, **Rajasthan**, **Karnataka**, **Madhya Pradesh**, **Haryana**, **Punjab**, Andhra Pradesh.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q9(iv))* Rohan, a farmer, hired some labourers to help him with "ginning". (a) Name the crop grown by Rohan. (b) Mention any two climatic conditions suitable for this crop.
**Solution:** (a) **Cotton**. (b) Any two: **temperature 20–32 °C**; **rainfall 50–120 cm**; **at least 200 frost-free days**.

### 4. Jute — the "golden fibre"

**Importance** — jute is called the **golden fibre** for its **golden-brown colour and high value**. It is used for **gunny bags (sacks), hessian, carpets, ropes, mats, wall coverings** and **eco-friendly, biodegradable bags** — a good substitute for plastic. India is the **largest producer of raw jute**.

**Climatic conditions**
- **Hot and humid** — **temperature 25–35 °C**; **high humidity (about 90 %)**.
- **Heavy rainfall — 150–200 cm** or more.

**Soil** — **rich, well-drained alluvial soil**, **renewed every year by floods** (the delta of the Ganga–Brahmaputra); jute **exhausts the soil** quickly, so the flood silt is valuable.

**Other needs** — **plenty of clean, soft water** for **retting**; **cheap labour** for harvesting, retting and stripping.

**Retting** — **bundles of harvested jute stalks** are **submerged in clean, gently flowing water** for about **2–3 weeks**; **bacteria (a micro-biological process) soften the stalk** and loosen the bark, so that the **fibre can be stripped** easily. The fibre is then **washed, dried and baled** for the mills.

![Fig. 12.2 Jute — from field to mill; retting](figures/g_ch12_fig02_jute_retting.png)

**Distribution** — **West Bengal** (the **largest producer** — about three-quarters of India's jute), **Bihar**, **Assam**, **Odisha**, Meghalaya. The **jute mills** stand along the **Hooghly river** near Kolkata.

**Why is West Bengal the leading producer of jute?** A **warm (25–35 °C), humid (about 90 %) climate**; **heavy rainfall (more than 150–200 cm)**; **rich alluvial soil** renewed by floods in the delta; **plenty of soft water from the Hooghly** for retting; **cheap labour**; mills nearby.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2024, Q9(iii)(a))* Give a reason: West Bengal is a leading producer of jute.
**Solution:** Any one: a **warm** climate (average **25–35 °C**); a **humid** climate (about **90 % humidity**); **heavy rainfall** (more than **150–200 cm**); **rich alluvial soil**; **plenty of soft water from the Hooghly** river.

### 5. Tea

**Importance** — India is one of the **largest producers and consumers of tea**, and a major exporter. Tea is a **plantation crop**: a single crop on large **estates (gardens)** with **factories** for processing and a large **labour force**.

**Climatic conditions**
- **Temperature 20–30 °C** (the hill gardens of West Bengal: **10–35 °C** through the year).
- **Rainfall 150–250 cm**, **well distributed throughout the year**; **high humidity**, **frequent showers** that encourage **fresh leaves**.
- **No frost**; tea is **sensitive to direct sunlight** when young, so **shade trees** (silver oak) are planted.

**Soil and land**
- **Deep, well-drained, friable loamy soil** — **forest / mountain soil** rich in **humus and nitrogen**, slightly **acidic**; also **laterite**.
- **Sloping land (hill slopes)** — tea **cannot bear stagnant water** at its roots; slopes **drain the rain quickly**. Altitude about **900–2,000 m** in the hills (Darjeeling); in Assam it grows on the **gently sloping land of the Brahmaputra valley**.

![Fig. 12.1 A tea garden on a hill slope](figures/g_ch12_fig01_tea_garden.png)

**Methods of cultivation**
1. **Propagation** — from **seeds** in nurseries or by **vegetative propagation**: a **cutting from a superior (high-yielding) mother plant** is planted, giving uniform, better-yielding bushes.
2. Seedlings are planted **in rows** on slopes, with **shade trees**.
3. **Pruning** — bushes are **cut back regularly** to keep them **low (about a metre, waist height)** so that **plucking is easy**, and to encourage **fresh leaves and new shoots** that branch sideways.
4. **Plucking** — by hand, mostly by women, every week or two in the growing season. **Fine pluck** = **two leaves and a bud** — gives the **best quality** tea; **coarse pluck** takes more leaves.
5. **Processing** in the estate factory — **withering, rolling, fermenting (oxidising), drying (firing), sorting and grading** (black tea; green tea is not fermented).

**Distribution** — **Assam** (the **largest producer** — Brahmaputra and Surma valleys), **West Bengal** (Darjeeling hills, Jalpaiguri — the Dooars), **Tamil Nadu** (Nilgiris), **Kerala** (Munnar), Himachal Pradesh (Kangra), Tripura, Karnataka.

![Fig. 12.5 Leading states for sugarcane, cotton, jute and tea](figures/g_ch12_fig05_cash_crops_map.png)

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q9(iii))* (a) Name the crop that predominantly grows in the hilly regions of West Bengal. (b) Give two climatic conditions present in this state that favour its cultivation. (c) Which other state is the leading producer of this crop?
**Solution:** (a) **Tea**. (b) Any two: **temperature 10–35 °C**; **rainfall 150–250 cm**, well distributed throughout the year; **shade** — it is sensitive to direct sunlight; **deep, well-drained, friable loamy soil** rich in nitrogen; **sloping land** to avoid stagnation of water at the root zone; **900–2,000 m** altitude. (c) **Assam**.

### Coffee (in papers up to 2026)

*Coffee is not named in the 2027 syllabus, but it was asked in 2024–2026; learn these key points.*

- **Plantation crop** of the **hill slopes of the south** — **Karnataka** (the **largest producer** — **Kodagu (Coorg)**, **Chikkamagaluru** — the **Baba Budan hills**, where coffee was first planted in India — and Hassan), **Kerala** (Wayanad), **Tamil Nadu** (Nilgiris, Shevaroys).

![Fig. 12.6 Coffee and the oilseeds (groundnut, mustard, soybean): leading states](figures/g_ch12_fig06_coffee_oilseeds_map.png)

- **Temperature 15–28 °C**; **rainfall 150–250 cm**; **no frost**, **no strong direct sun**; **well-drained, loamy (red / laterite) soil** rich in humus on **sloping land** (600–1,600 m).
- **Shade trees** (**silver oak**, with **cardamom** and **pepper** grown alongside) are planted between the rows to **give shade**, since coffee is sensitive to direct sunlight, and to give the planter **extra income**.
- Varieties: **Arabica** and **Robusta**. The berries are dried and hulled, and the **beans are roasted** to **bring out the flavour and aroma** (and remove moisture) before grinding.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2025, Q9(iii))* Give one reason each: (a) Trees are planted between the rows of coffee plants. (b) Cotton cultivation requires plenty of cheap labour. (c) Hill slopes are best suited for tea cultivation.
**Solution:** (a) To provide **shade** / **extra income**. (b) For **picking, ginning, manuring** and **harvesting**. (c) **Stagnant water is harmful** for the crop — it requires **well-drained** soil.

### Problems of cash-crop farming and their remedies

| Crop | Problem | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Sugarcane** | Heavy use of **water**; **delays in payment** by mills; low sugar content in the north | **Drip irrigation**, better varieties, timely payment, cooperative mills |
| **Cotton** | **Bollworm** attacks; **dependence on rain** in dry areas; price swings | **Pest-resistant varieties**, integrated pest management, irrigation, support prices |
| **Jute** | Competition from **cheap synthetic (plastic) bags**; soil exhaustion; shortage of retting water | Promote **jute bags and diversified products**; better retting methods; flood-silt and fertilisers |
| **Tea** | **Old bushes** and low yields; rising costs; competition abroad | **Replanting with high-yield clones**, better processing, branding (GI tags) |
| **Oilseeds** | Grown mostly on **rain-fed, poor land**; low yields; India **imports** edible oil | Better seeds, irrigation, the edible-oil mission, support prices |

### Summary of the chapter

- **Sugarcane** — tropical; 20–30 °C; 100–200 cm; loamy alluvium / black soil; setts; **ratooning** (saves time and money; not more than two ratoons); **Uttar Pradesh**, Maharashtra (black soil, maritime climate, cooperatives); bagasse, molasses, press mud.
- **Oilseeds** — **groundnut** (kharif, 20–30 °C, 50–75 cm, sandy loam, **Gujarat**, oil cake = cattle feed); **mustard** (rabi, cool, 25–40 cm, **Rajasthan**); **soybean** (kharif, black soil, **Madhya Pradesh**, protein and oil).

- **Tea** — plantation; 20–30 °C, 150–250 cm well distributed; shade; well-drained forest soil on **slopes**; vegetative propagation; **pruning**; **fine pluck = two leaves and a bud**; **Assam**, West Bengal (Darjeeling).
- **Coffee** (to 2026) — 15–28 °C, 150–250 cm, shade trees, slopes; **Karnataka**; beans **roasted**.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — One process, one crop
**Ratoon** grows from sugarcane stubble; **ginning** cleans cotton; **retting** rots jute stalks to free the fibre; **pruning** keeps tea low; **roasting** flavours coffee. Learning the pairs earns easy marks.

### Section 2 — Water: a friend and a foe
Jute and sugarcane love water; tea needs heavy rain but **hates waterlogging**, so it climbs the slopes; cotton needs rain while growing but **dry sunshine** at picking.

### Section 3 — The Deccan's black gift
**Black soil** holds moisture and is rich in lime and potash — perfect for **cotton**, good for **sugarcane** and **soybean**.

### Section 4 — Plantations
Tea (and coffee) are grown on estates with factories, shade trees and a large, skilled labour force — **plantation farming** from Chapter 10.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Robert Bruce and Maniram Dewan
In 1823 the Scottish trader Robert Bruce learned of tea growing wild in upper Assam from the Singpho people; **Maniram Dewan**, an Assamese noble, helped the early tea gardens and later set up his own — one of the first Indians to do so. Assam became the largest tea-growing region in the world.

### 6.2 Baba Budan
According to tradition, the Sufi saint Baba Budan brought seven coffee beans from Yemen in the 17th century and planted them in the hills of Chikkamagaluru (Karnataka) — now called the **Baba Budan hills** — beginning India's coffee story.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Cash crops link farm and factory** — sugar mills, textile mills, jute mills and oil mills depend on them.
- **Each crop has a niche** — black soil for cotton, deltas for jute, slopes for tea.
- **Processing adds value** — ginning, retting and tea manufacture turn raw crops into trade goods.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **North-East India:** tea gardens of Assam and Tripura employ lakhs of workers; small growers now sell leaf to "bought-leaf" factories.
- **Your school bag:** a jute bag replaces many plastic bags and rots harmlessly.
- **Your kitchen:** mustard oil in the east, groundnut oil in the west and south, soybean oil everywhere.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (3 marks) Name the leading state of: (a) mustard (b) soybean (c) tea.
**Answer:** (a) Rajasthan. (b) Madhya Pradesh. (c) Assam.

**Q2.** (2 marks) Why do sugar mills have to be close to the cane fields?
**Answer:** Cane is bulky and loses its sugar content within about 24 hours of cutting, so it must be crushed quickly.

**Q3.** (3 marks) Name three by-products of the sugar industry and one use of each.
**Answer:** Bagasse — fuel and paper; molasses — alcohol; press mud — manure.

**Q4.** (2 marks) Why do cotton bolls need dry, sunny weather at picking time?
**Answer:** Rain at that stage stains and spoils the fibre; sunshine helps the bolls open.

**Q5.** (3 marks) Give the season and leading state of: (a) groundnut (b) mustard (c) cotton.
**Answer:** (a) Kharif — Gujarat. (b) Rabi — Rajasthan. (c) Kharif — Gujarat (Maharashtra largest area).

**Q6.** (2 marks) Why is the jute industry facing competition today? Suggest one remedy.
**Answer:** Cheap synthetic (plastic) bags have replaced jute sacks; promoting eco-friendly jute bags and diversified jute products is a remedy.

**Q7.** (3 marks) Name the cash crop: (a) "golden fibre" of the Bengal delta (b) "white gold" of the black-soil Deccan (c) the plantation crop of Assam.
**Answer:** (a) Jute. (b) Cotton. (c) Tea.

**Q8.** (2 marks) Why is sugarcane yield and sugar content higher in peninsular India than in the north?
**Answer:** A frost-free maritime climate without the loo, black soil, a longer growing and crushing season and better irrigation and management.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (A farmer's choice)** A farmer in Vidarbha (Maharashtra) has black soil and 80 cm of rain. Suggest a cash crop and two reasons.

**Model answer:** Fine pluck (two leaves and a bud), regular pruning to keep bushes low, shade trees, and good drainage on the slopes.

**Model answer:** Jute is biodegradable and renewable, while plastic takes centuries to degrade; it also supports jute farmers.

**Q5. (An oilseed for a dry state)** Rajasthan has cool winters and little rain. Which oilseed suits it, and in which season?
**Model answer:** Mustard (rapeseed), a rabi crop needing only 25–40 cm or light irrigation.
