## 1. Complete Lesson

### Water — a precious resource

- Water is needed for **drinking, farming, industry, power generation, transport** and for all living things.
- India has about **18 per cent of the world's people** but only about **4 per cent of its fresh water**.
- Almost all of India's water comes from **rainfall and snowfall**, and most of the rain falls in **four months (June–September)** of the south-west monsoon.
- **Agriculture uses the largest share** of India's water (about 80 per cent), mainly for irrigation.

### Sources of water

India's fresh water comes from two sources.

**1. Surface water**
- **Surface water** is the water found **on the surface of the earth** — in **rivers, streams, lakes, ponds, tanks and reservoirs**.
- It comes directly from **rainfall** and **melting snow**.
- The **Himalayan rivers** (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra and their tributaries) are **perennial** — fed by **rain and melting snow**, they flow all year.
- The **peninsular rivers** (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri, Narmada, Tapi) are **rain-fed and seasonal** — their flow falls sharply in the dry season.
- Surface water is easily **polluted** by sewage, factory waste and farm chemicals.

**2. Groundwater**
- **Groundwater** is the water found **below the surface** of the earth, filling the **pores and cracks of soil and rocks**. It comes from **rainwater and surface water that percolates (seeps) down**.
- A layer of rock or soil that holds and yields groundwater is called an **aquifer**. The upper level of the water-filled zone is the **water table**.
- Groundwater is tapped by **wells and tube wells**, and it comes out naturally as **springs** — so a **spring is a groundwater source**.
- It is usually **cleaner** than surface water, because the soil filters it.
- The **Northern Plains** (soft, porous alluvium) are **rich in groundwater**; the **peninsular plateau** of hard, impermeable rocks has **less groundwater**, found only in cracks.

![Fig. 7.1 Surface water and groundwater; a dug well, a tube well and a spring](figures/g_ch07_fig01_surface_ground_water.png)

| Surface water | Groundwater |
|---|---|
| On the surface — rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks | Below the surface — in aquifers (pores and cracks of rock) |
| Fed directly by rain and snowmelt | Fed by percolation of rain and surface water |
| Easily polluted; lost by evaporation | Usually purer (filtered); little evaporation |
| Used through canals, tanks, dams | Used through wells, tube wells; comes out as springs |

**Why is there a scarcity of surface water in India?**
1. Rainfall is **seasonal** — most of it falls in four months; many rivers shrink in the dry season.
2. Rainfall is **unevenly distributed** — heavy in some regions, scanty in others (Rajasthan).
3. The **peninsular rivers are seasonal**.
4. Much rainwater **runs off quickly** into the sea; a lot is lost by **evaporation** in the hot climate.
5. **Pollution** makes much surface water unfit for use.
6. **Over-use** for irrigation, industry and cities.

### Water scarcity and the need for conservation

**Water scarcity** means that the **demand for usable water is more than its supply**. India faces it even though it gets plenty of rain, because:
1. **Rising population** — more water is needed for drinking, food production and homes.
2. **Urbanisation and industrialisation** — cities and factories need huge amounts of water.
3. **Expansion of irrigation** — high-yielding crops need assured water.
4. **Over-exploitation of groundwater** — too many tube wells have **lowered the water table** sharply (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan, Delhi).
5. **Pollution** — sewage, industrial effluents, fertilisers and pesticides make water unusable.
6. **Wastage** — leaking pipes, flood irrigation, careless use.
7. **Seasonal, erratic and uneven rainfall**; recurring **droughts**.

**Water conservation** is the **careful use, saving, protection and recycling of water** so that enough is available now and in the future.

**Why is water conservation necessary?**
1. To **meet the increasing demand** of the growing population, agriculture and industry.
2. To **check the falling groundwater table**.
3. To **reduce water scarcity**, especially in the dry season and drought years.
4. To **prevent pollution** and keep water clean for use.
5. To **reduce wastage** of a limited resource.
6. To **increase crop production** and ensure **food security**.

**Methods of water conservation**
- **Rainwater harvesting** — collecting and storing rain, and recharging groundwater.
- **Recharging groundwater** — percolation pits, recharge wells, percolation tanks, check dams, plugging gullies.
- **Watershed management** — conserving soil and water in a whole river catchment.
- **Efficient irrigation** — **drip and sprinkler** instead of flooding fields.
- **Recycling and reuse** — treating sewage and industrial waste water for gardens, industry and flushing.
- **Controlling pollution** — treating effluents before release.
- **Saving in daily life** — repairing leaks, installing **water meters**, avoiding wastage, spreading **awareness**.
- **Reviving traditional systems** — johads, tankas, stepwells, tanks.
- **Afforestation** — forests help water percolate.

### Rainwater harvesting

**Rainwater harvesting** is the **collecting and storing of rainwater where it falls** (or capturing the run-off in one's own village or town) for **later use**, or **channelling it into the ground to recharge the groundwater** — while **keeping it clean** by not allowing polluting activities in the catchment.

**Rooftop rainwater harvesting** — the **roof** of a building acts as the **catchment area**. Rain on the roof is led through **gutters and pipes**, passed through a **filter**, and either **stored in a tank** for use or sent into a **recharge pit / well** to **recharge the groundwater**.

![Fig. 7.2 Rooftop rainwater harvesting — store for use, and recharge the groundwater](figures/g_ch07_fig02_rooftop_rwh.png)

**Methods of rainwater harvesting**
1. **Rooftop rainwater harvesting** — into storage tanks or recharge pits.
2. **Percolation pits / recharge pits** — pits filled with gravel and sand that let water seep down.
3. **Recharge wells** — directing run-off into **dug wells or old bore wells**.
4. **Percolation tanks** and **check dams** across streams — they hold water so it soaks into the ground.
5. **Contour bunds and trenches** on slopes.

**Traditional rainwater harvesting systems of India**

| System | Region | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Johad** | Rajasthan, Haryana | Small **earthen check dams** that collect rainwater and recharge groundwater |
| **Tanka (kund, kundi)** | Rajasthan (Thar) | **Underground tanks** in courtyards or houses, filled from rooftops |
| **Khadin** | Jaisalmer (Rajasthan) | Earthen embankment across a slope; water soaks into a field |
| **Kul / gul** | Himachal Pradesh, Jammu | **Diversion channels** carrying glacier and stream water to fields |
| **Zing** | Ladakh | **Small tanks** that collect **glacier melt-water** |
| **Bamboo drip irrigation** | Meghalaya | **Bamboo pipes** carry spring water downhill to betel and black-pepper plants |
| **Eri (tank)** | Tamil Nadu | Village **tanks** storing rain for irrigation |
| **Bawari / baoli** (stepwell) | Rajasthan, Gujarat, Delhi | **Stepwells** reaching down to groundwater |
| **Zabo** | Nagaland | Ponds that collect run-off from forested slopes, used with cattle-rearing and rice |

**Importance (objectives) of rainwater harvesting**
1. **Conserves water** and **meets the increasing demand** for water.
2. **Recharges groundwater** and **raises the water table**.
3. **Reduces water scarcity** — supplements the supply in the **dry season / summer**.
4. **Reduces surface run-off**, so it **reduces flooding** (especially of city streets) and **checks soil erosion**.
5. Provides **clean water** for drinking and irrigation, and **checks groundwater pollution**.
6. Gives an **independent water supply** to a house, school or village.
7. **Cheap and simple**; anyone can do it.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2024, Q7(iv))* [A picture of a house whose sloping roof drains rainwater through a pipe into an underground tank.] (a) Name the water conservation practice. (b) How does this system help to reduce floods? (c) Why is water conservation necessary? (Two reasons)
**Solution:** (a) **Rooftop rainwater harvesting**. (b) It **collects rainwater** and so **reduces run-off and flooding**. (c) Any two: the **demand for water has increased tremendously** with the rising population; much water is **polluted** or **wasted**; demand has risen with **industrialisation and urbanisation**; **over-exploitation of groundwater** has lowered the water table considerably; rain is **erratic, sporadic and seasonal**.

### Irrigation — meaning and importance

**Irrigation** is the **artificial supply of water to crops** from wells, tube wells, tanks, canals or modern systems, when rainfall is not enough.

**Why does India need irrigation?** (Why is it crucial for Indian farming?)
1. **Rainfall is seasonal** — it falls mainly in **June–September**; the rest of the year is dry, so **rabi crops** and **year-round cropping** need irrigation.
2. **Rainfall is erratic and uncertain** — the monsoon may **arrive late, end early or break** for weeks; droughts and floods both occur.
3. **Rainfall is unevenly distributed** — **Rajasthan, Gujarat** and the **rain-shadow Deccan** get very little.
4. **Some crops need a lot of water** — **rice, sugarcane, jute**; **annual crops** like sugarcane need water all through their long growing period.
5. **High-yielding varieties (HYV)** of the Green Revolution need **assured, regular water** with fertilisers.
6. **High evaporation** in the tropical heat dries the soil quickly.
7. India has a **long growing season** (warm all year) — with irrigation, **two or three crops** a year can be grown, raising production for a **large population**.

**How has irrigation changed the cropping pattern?**
- **Multiple cropping** — two or three crops a year on the same land.
- **Rabi crops** such as **wheat** in the dry winter.
- More area under **water-loving and cash crops** — rice in Punjab and Haryana, sugarcane, cotton.
- **Success of the Green Revolution** and **self-sufficiency in food grains**.
- Cultivation in **dry areas** — e.g. the Indira Gandhi Canal has made farming possible in parts of the Thar.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q7(i))* The need for irrigation in India is, to a large extent, influenced by the rainfall pattern of the country. Explain.
**Solution:** Any two: rainfall is **erratic**; it is **unevenly distributed**; it is **seasonal**; and some crops like **rice and sugarcane** need more water than the rain provides.

### Means (methods) of irrigation

| Means | Share of irrigated area (approx.) |
|---|---|
| Wells and tube wells (groundwater) | **Over 60 per cent** — the most important means today |
| Canals | About **one-quarter** |
| Tanks | Only a **few per cent** |
| Others (springs, small channels, drip, sprinkler) | The rest |

**Traditional means** — **wells, canals and tanks** — are still important because they have a **low initial cost**, are **easy to operate** and **easily accessible** to farmers. **Modern means** — **drip and sprinkler** — save water.

#### 1. Wells

A **well** is a **pit dug into the ground** down to the **water table** to draw groundwater. **Dug wells (open wells)** are shallow. Water is lifted by the **Persian wheel (rahat)**, a **leather bucket pulled by bullocks (charas / mot)**, a **lever (dhenkli)**, or a small **pump**.

**Conditions (requirements) for well irrigation**
1. A **high water table** — enough groundwater near the surface.
2. **Soft soil** (alluvium) that is **easy to dig**.
3. **Sufficient rainfall** to **recharge** the groundwater.
4. Water that is **fresh**, not salty.

**Areas** — the **Northern Plains** (**Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Punjab, Haryana**), **Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu**.

**Why are wells popular in northern India?** Wells are the **simplest and cheapest** source; the **soft alluvial soil** is easy to dig and the **water table is high**; a well is an **independent source** in the farmer's control and can be **dug at any convenient place**.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Cheap**, simple and **easy to dig and operate** | Irrigate only a **small area** |
| An **independent source** in the farmer's hands; used when needed | May **dry up** in droughts or when the water table falls |
| Can be **dug at a convenient place**; occupies **little land** | Lifting water by hand or animals is **slow and tiring** |
| Well water contains **salts (nitrates, etc.)** useful for crops; little evaporation | Water may be **polluted**; time-consuming |

#### 2. Tube wells

A **tube well** is a **deep, narrow bore** (often 15 m to over 100 m) fitted with a **pipe** through which groundwater is **pumped up by an electric or diesel pump**.

**Conditions for tube well irrigation**
1. A **high groundwater table** — plenty of groundwater.
2. **Soft soil / soft rocks** — easy to bore.
3. A **regular, cheap supply of electricity** (for example **hydel power** in Punjab and Haryana).
4. **Fertile, productive land** — the **costly** tube well must be **recovered** from good harvests.
5. Farmers able to afford the cost.

**Areas** — **Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh** (largest area under tube wells), **Bihar, West Bengal**, Gujarat, Tamil Nadu.

**Why are tube wells preferred over surface (dug) wells?** They draw water from **deeper, more reliable** layers, **irrigate a much larger area**, give water **quickly with a pump** and **do not dry up** easily.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Irrigate a **large area**; water available **whenever needed** | **Expensive** to install and run |
| **Regular supply**; little loss by evaporation or seepage | Need a **regular, cheap supply of electricity** |
| Can be used where canals cannot reach | **Over-use depletes groundwater** — the water table falls; in places water becomes saline |

**Why have many states restricted tube wells?** Excessive pumping, especially for rice, has **lowered the groundwater table rapidly**; states such as **Punjab and Haryana** control new tube wells and **delay paddy transplanting** to save groundwater.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q7(iii))* (a) Name one state where tube wells are commonly used. (b) Mention two geographical conditions that favour tube-well irrigation in this state.
**Solution:** (a) Any one: **Punjab / Haryana / Uttar Pradesh / Bihar / West Bengal**. (b) Any two: a **high groundwater table**; a **steady supply of electricity**; **soft soil**; **fertile, productive land** that makes this expensive method viable.

#### 3. Tanks

A **tank** is a **natural or man-made hollow (depression)** in which **rainwater is collected and stored**; often a small **earthen or stone embankment (bund)** is built across a seasonal stream to make one. Water is let out to the fields through channels.

**Where?** Tank irrigation is most widely used in **peninsular India (the Deccan plateau)** — **Tamil Nadu** (the leading state), **Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana**, and parts of Odisha, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra and Rajasthan.

**Why is tank irrigation popular in peninsular India (e.g. Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu)?**
1. The land is **uneven / undulating** with **many natural depressions** where rain collects.
2. The **hard, impermeable rocks** do not let water soak away — and make **digging wells and canals difficult**.
3. The **rivers are seasonal**; rain falls in one season, so it must be **stored**.
4. Population and farms are **scattered**; small tanks serve each village.

**Why is tank irrigation NOT common in northern India?** The **soft, porous soil** absorbs the water; there are **few natural depressions** on the flat plain; and **better sources** — **perennial rivers, canals and plentiful groundwater** — are available.

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| **Simple, cheap** to build; last long | **Dry up in summer**, when water is needed most (seasonal) |
| **Store surplus rainwater** that would otherwise run off and go waste | **Silting** — tanks fill up with mud and need desilting |
| Used for **fish rearing (pisciculture)** and **domestic use** | **Occupy large areas** of land that could be farmed |
| **Recharge the groundwater** of the surrounding area | **Loss of water by evaporation** as tanks are shallow |
| Managed by the farmers / village | Lifting water to fields needs labour; irrigate a limited area |

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2023, Q7(iii))* (a) Where in India is tank irrigation most widely used? (b) Give one factor that has led to its popularity there. (c) Mention one advantage of tank irrigation.
**Solution:** (a) The **Deccan / peninsular plateau** (Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Telangana). (b) Any one: rainwater collects in **natural pits and depressions**, which are common there; the region is made of **hard, impermeable rock**; the **rivers and rain are seasonal**. (c) Any one: rainwater is **easily collected in quantity**; it is **cheaper** than other types since natural hollows are used; it is used in the **dry season** and recharges the **water table** of the surrounding area.

#### 4. Canals

A **canal** is an **artificial channel** that carries water from a **river, reservoir or barrage** to the fields.

**Types of canals**

| Inundation canals | Perennial canals |
|---|---|
| Taken out **directly from rivers without any regulating system** (weir or barrage) at their head | Taken out from **perennial rivers** with the help of a **barrage, weir or dam (head works)**, or from **reservoirs** |
| Carry water **only when the river is in flood** (rainy season) | Supply water **throughout the year** |
| **Seasonal and unreliable**; irrigate only **low-lying** areas | **Reliable**; let crops be grown **round the year**, even in the dry season |
| Few today; many converted into perennial canals | Most canals of India today |

![Fig. 7.3 Inundation canal and perennial canal (schematic plan)](figures/g_ch07_fig03_canals.png)

**Areas** — the **Northern Plains**: **Punjab, Haryana, Uttar Pradesh**, **Rajasthan** (Indira Gandhi Canal), **Bihar**; and the **coastal deltas** of the **Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri** (Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu).

**Important canals**
- **Indira Gandhi Canal** — from the **Harike barrage** (Sutlej–Beas), carries water into the **Thar desert** of Rajasthan; the longest canal of India.
- **Sirhind Canal** (from the Sutlej at Ropar) and the **Bhakra canals** — Punjab, Haryana.
- **Western Yamuna Canal** (Haryana) and **Eastern Yamuna Canal** (Uttar Pradesh).
- **Upper Ganga Canal** (from Haridwar) and **Lower Ganga Canal** (from Narora) — Uttar Pradesh.
- **Sharda Canal** — Uttar Pradesh.
- **Nagarjuna Sagar canals** (Krishna), **Mettur canals** (Kaveri), **Mahanadi canals** (Hirakud), **Sardar Sarovar (Narmada) canal** in Gujarat and Rajasthan.

![Fig. 7.5 Irrigation: the tube-well and tank states and the main canals](figures/g_ch07_fig05_irrigation_map.png)

**Why are canals more suitable in the Northern Plains (e.g. Punjab)?**
1. The land is **flat and level** — canals are easy to build and water flows over long distances.
2. There are **many perennial (snow-fed) rivers** that supply water all year.
3. The **soft alluvial soil** is **easy to dig**.
4. The land is **fertile and agriculturally important**, so it needs water and **recovers the cost**.

**Why are most South Indian states unsuitable for canals?** The terrain is **uneven and rocky**; **hard rocks** make digging difficult; and the **rivers are seasonal** (except in the deltas).

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Irrigate **very large areas** | **Waterlogging** from over-irrigation and seepage |
| **Perennial supply**; crops all year | **Salinity / alkalinity** — salts rise to the surface (**efflorescence**, white crust called **reh / kallar**), making land infertile |
| Bring **fertile silt** to the fields | **Seepage and evaporation** losses |
| **Cheap for farmers** once built; recharge groundwater | **Costly to build**; stagnant water breeds **mosquitoes** |

**Why does canal irrigation often lead to soil efflorescence?** Too much canal water and seepage **raise the water table**; as water evaporates from the surface, **salts are drawn up by capillary action** and left as a **white crust**, making the soil **saline and infertile**.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2019, Q6(d))* Briefly explain: (i) inundation canal (ii) rooftop rainwater harvesting (iii) surface water.
**Solution:** (i) Canals taken out from rivers **without any regulating system like weirs** at their head — they are **filled with water only during floods**. (ii) Rainwater is **collected over the rooftop** and the collected water is **channelled through small pipes into underground pits, wells**, etc. (iii) Water found **on the surface of the earth** in the form of **rivers, lakes, ponds**, etc.

#### 5. Modern methods — drip and sprinkler irrigation

**Drip (trickle) irrigation**
- Water is carried through a **network of pipes** with small **emitters** that release it **drop by drop at the root zone** of each plant.
- **Advantages:** **conserves water** (the water reaches the roots where it is needed); **little loss by evaporation, run-off or seepage**; fertilisers can be given with the water; **weeds are fewer**; suits **dry areas, uneven land and orchards** (grapes, bananas, sugarcane, cotton, vegetables, coconut); saves labour.
- **Disadvantages (why not used on a large scale):** **expensive to install**; needs **proper infrastructure**; **high maintenance cost**; **pipes and emitters clog**.

**Sprinkler (spray) irrigation**
- Water is pumped through pipes and **sprayed into the air through rotating nozzles**, falling on the crops **like rain**. It is also called **spray irrigation**.
- **Advantages:** **even (uniform) distribution** of water over a large field; **no loss by seepage** and less run-off, so it **conserves water**; suits **uneven land and sandy, porous soils**; ideal for **arid and semi-arid regions**; prevents wastage.
- **Disadvantages:** costly; strong **wind** and heat cause some **evaporation** and uneven spraying; not suited to paddy.

![Fig. 7.4 Drip irrigation and sprinkler irrigation](figures/g_ch07_fig04_drip_sprinkler.png)

**Why are modern methods gaining popularity?** There is **no loss of water by evaporation and leakage**; they **use water economically**; they **do not cause soil erosion**; they suit **areas of low rainfall** and give **high efficiency** — and governments now **subsidise** them ("**more crop per drop**").

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q7(i))* Drip irrigation is a powerful solution to prevent crop failure, especially during critical dry periods. Give two reasons in support of this statement.
**Solution:** (1) It **waters the crops at the root zone** and hence **conserves water**. (2) As the water is provided **in drops and released slowly**, it **prevents wastage** of water due to **evaporation and run-off**.

### All means of irrigation compared

| Means | Needs | Main areas | Key advantage | Key disadvantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Well | High water table, soft soil | UP, Bihar, Punjab, Gujarat | Cheap, independent | Small area; dries up |
| Tube well | Groundwater, soft soil, cheap power, fertile land | Punjab, Haryana, UP | Large area, any time | Costly; lowers water table |
| Tank | Natural hollows, hard rock, seasonal rain | Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, AP, Telangana | Stores rain cheaply | Dries up in summer; silting |
| Canal | Level land, perennial rivers, soft soil | Punjab, Haryana, UP, Rajasthan, deltas | Very large area, all year | Waterlogging, salinity |
| Drip | Pipes, pump | Maharashtra, Karnataka, AP | Saves most water | Costly; clogging |
| Sprinkler | Pump, pipes | Rajasthan, Haryana, Gujarat | Even spread; for sandy, uneven land | Costly; wind loss |

### Summary of the chapter

- **Sources** — **surface water** (rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks; Himalayan rivers perennial, peninsular rivers seasonal) and **groundwater** (in aquifers; tapped by wells and tube wells; comes out as springs; rich in the plains).
- **Scarcity** — population, cities, industry, irrigation, over-pumping, pollution, wastage, seasonal and uneven rain. **Conservation** — rainwater harvesting, recharge, drip/sprinkler, recycling, meters, awareness, traditional systems.
- **Rainwater harvesting** — collect rain where it falls; store it or recharge groundwater; rooftop, recharge pits, recharge wells, check dams; johad, tanka, kul, zing, bamboo drip; reduces scarcity, floods and erosion.
- **Irrigation needed** — rain seasonal, erratic, uneven; thirsty crops; HYV seeds; evaporation; multiple cropping.
- **Wells** — cheap, independent; high water table and soft soil; northern plains. **Tube wells** — groundwater + soft soil + cheap power + fertile land; Punjab, Haryana, UP; costly, deplete groundwater.
- **Tanks** — hollows in hard-rock peninsula; Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, AP; cheap; dry up, silt up.
- **Canals** — inundation (flood season, no weir) vs perennial (barrage, all year); northern plains and deltas; waterlogging and salinity.
- **Drip** — drops at the roots, saves water, costly; **sprinkler** — spray like rain, even spread, arid and sandy areas.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Two kinds of water
Water you can see (rivers, lakes) is surface water; water stored in the rocks beneath your feet is groundwater. Rain feeds both — directly, or by percolating down.

### Section 2 — The land decides the method
On the **soft, level, river-rich plains** of the north, canals and tube wells work best. On the **hard, uneven, seasonal-river plateau** of the south, rain is stored in tanks. In **dry, sandy or uneven areas**, sprinklers and drips save water.

### Section 3 — Plenty of rain, yet thirsty
India's rain is **seasonal, erratic and uneven**. So even a rainy country needs irrigation, and must **harvest** its rain while it falls.

### Section 4 — Too much of a good thing
Over-pumping lowers the water table; over-irrigating with canals waterlogs the land and brings salts to the surface. Good irrigation is **efficient** irrigation.

## 4. Vocabulary (Key Terms)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Surface water | Water on the earth's surface — rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks. |
| Groundwater | Water below the surface, in the pores and cracks of rocks. |
| Aquifer | A layer of rock or soil that holds and yields groundwater. |

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Rajendra Singh (born 1959) — "the Waterman of India"
Working with the Tarun Bharat Sangh in Alwar district, Rajasthan, Rajendra Singh helped villages rebuild thousands of **johads**. Dry streams such as the Arvari began to flow again. He received the Ramon Magsaysay Award (2001) and the Stockholm Water Prize (2015).

### 6.2 Anupam Mishra (1948–2016)
A Gandhian environmentalist whose book *Aaj Bhi Khare Hain Talaab* ("The ponds are still relevant") described India's traditional tanks and the communities that built them, and inspired many villages to revive them.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Water follows the rocks and the rain** — soft alluvium stores groundwater; hard rock needs tanks.
- **Store the monsoon** — India's challenge is not too little rain but rain at the wrong time and place.
- **Efficiency is conservation** — every drop saved by drip or sprinkler is water left in the aquifer.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Chennai:** after Tamil Nadu made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for buildings, groundwater levels in many parts of the city rose.
- **Meghalaya:** the 200-year-old bamboo drip system carries spring water across slopes to betel-leaf and pepper gardens — drip irrigation long before plastic pipes.
- **Your home:** a leaking tap can waste many litres a day; a water meter and a repaired washer are conservation too.

### 8.1 Graded exercises — solutions

#### Exercise 7A — Sources and conservation

**Q1.** Differentiate between surface water and groundwater.
**Answer:** Surface water is found on the surface in rivers, lakes and ponds; groundwater is found below the surface in the pores and cracks of rocks (aquifers).

**Q2.** Name two causes of water scarcity in India.
**Answer:** Rising population and over-exploitation of groundwater (also pollution, urbanisation, seasonal rain).

**Q3.** What is an aquifer?
**Answer:** A layer of rock or soil that holds and yields groundwater.

#### Exercise 7B — Rainwater harvesting

**Q4.** What is rooftop rainwater harvesting?
**Answer:** Collecting rain from roofs through pipes into storage tanks or recharge pits.

**Q5.** Name two traditional rainwater harvesting systems.
**Answer:** Johads and tankas of Rajasthan (also kuls, zings, bamboo drip).

**Q6.** How does rainwater harvesting reduce floods?
**Answer:** It collects rainwater and reduces surface run-off.

#### Exercise 7C — Irrigation

**Q7.** Give two reasons why India needs irrigation.
**Answer:** Rainfall is seasonal and erratic; crops like rice and sugarcane need much water.

**Q8.** Distinguish between inundation and perennial canals.
**Answer:** Inundation canals have no weir and carry water only in floods; perennial canals take water from barrages or reservoirs and flow all year.

**Q9.** Why is tank irrigation common in Tamil Nadu?
**Answer:** Hard rock and uneven land with natural depressions; seasonal rivers; rain must be stored.

**Q10.** Give one advantage of drip irrigation.
**Answer:** It conserves water by delivering it to the roots with little evaporation.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part II parts (2 marks each)

**Q1.** Give two reasons why India needs irrigation.
**Answer:** Rainfall is seasonal, erratic and unevenly distributed.
Crops like rice and sugarcane need much water (also HYV seeds).

**Q2.** Differentiate between inundation and perennial canals.
**Answer:** Inundation — no weir; water only during floods; seasonal.
Perennial — from barrages or reservoirs; water all year.

**Q3.** Mention one advantage and one disadvantage of well irrigation.
**Answer:** Advantage — cheap, independent, easy to dig.
Disadvantage — irrigates a small area; may dry up.

**Q4.** Mention one advantage and one disadvantage of tank irrigation.
**Answer:** Advantage — stores surplus rainwater cheaply; fish rearing.
Disadvantage — dries up in summer; silting; evaporation loss.

**Q5.** State two reasons why drip irrigation prevents crop failure in dry periods.
**Answer:** It waters the crops at the root zone and conserves water.
Slow release in drops prevents loss by evaporation and run-off.

**Q6.** Why is canal irrigation suitable in the northern plains? (Two reasons)
**Answer:** The land is flat and level.
Many perennial rivers and soft soil.

**Q7.** Name a method that gives uniform water distribution over a large field, and how it saves water.
**Answer:** Sprinkler (spray) irrigation.
It sprays only the required water evenly; no seepage, little run-off.

**Q8.** State the requirements for well irrigation.
**Answer:** A high water table with enough groundwater.
Soft soil easy to dig, and rainfall to recharge it.

**Q9.** Suggest two measures to overcome water scarcity in metropolitan cities.
**Answer:** Rainwater harvesting and recharge pits.
Recycling waste water and installing water meters.

**Q10.** Why are traditional means of irrigation still important? Name them.
**Answer:** Low initial cost; easy to operate and accessible.
Wells, tanks and canals.

**Q11.** Differentiate between surface water and groundwater.
**Answer:** Surface water — in rivers, lakes, ponds; easily polluted.
Groundwater — below the surface in aquifers; purer.

**Q12.** Why is drip irrigation not used on a large scale in India? Give its advantage.
**Answer:** Expensive, needs infrastructure; pipes clog.
It conserves water by reaching the roots directly.

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** (a) Name a state where tube wells are common. (b) Give two conditions that favour them there.
**Answer:** (a) Punjab (also Haryana, Uttar Pradesh).
(b) High groundwater table; soft soil.
Cheap power and fertile land also help.

**Q14.** (a) Where is tank irrigation most used? (b) One factor for its popularity. (c) One advantage.
**Answer:** (a) Peninsular India (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, AP).
(b) Natural depressions in hard, impermeable rock.
(c) Cheap storage of rainwater for the dry season.

**Q15.** Give reasons: (a) tanks are not common in northern India (b) canals are important in Punjab (c) all farmers do not use tube wells.
**Answer:** (a) Soft soil, no hollows, better sources available.
(b) Perennial rivers, level land, soft soil.
(c) Tube wells are expensive and need electricity.

**Q16.** State three objectives of rainwater harvesting.
**Answer:** Recharge groundwater and conserve water.
Reduce flooding, run-off and soil erosion.
Supplement supply in the dry season.

**Q17.** (a) How is groundwater different from surface water? (b) Why is it declining? (c) One recharge measure.
**Answer:** (a) Groundwater is below the surface; surface water is in rivers and lakes.
(b) Over-pumping by tube wells for irrigation and cities.
(c) Percolation pits / rainwater harvesting.

**Q18.** Give reasons: (a) wells are common in northern India (b) states restrict tube wells (c) tanks are popular in Karnataka.

(b) Flat land and perennial rivers.
(c) Uneven, rocky terrain and seasonal rivers.

**Q20.** (a) What is rainwater harvesting? (b) Name two methods. (c) How does it reduce floods?

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (A school building)** Your school has a large flat roof and the borewell runs dry each May. Design a simple plan.
**Model answer:** Rooftop rainwater harvesting — gutters and pipes from the roof, a filter, a storage tank for use, and an overflow to a recharge pit beside the borewell to raise the water table.

**Q2. (Two farmers)** One farmer in Haryana has cheap electricity and fertile alluvium; another on the Karnataka plateau has hard rock and a hollow near the field. Suggest a means of irrigation for each.
**Model answer:** Haryana — a tube well (high water table, soft soil, cheap power, fertile land). Karnataka — a tank in the natural hollow to store monsoon rain.

**Q3. (A salty field)** Land beside a canal in Punjab shows a white crust and poor crops. Explain and suggest a remedy.
**Model answer:** Over-irrigation and seepage raised the water table; evaporation left salts (reh/kallar). Remedy: use water carefully, improve drainage, line canals, add gypsum and grow salt-tolerant crops.

**Q4. (A dry orchard)** A grape grower in Maharashtra has little water. Which irrigation method should be used, and why?
**Model answer:** Drip irrigation — it delivers water drop by drop at the roots with almost no loss.

**Q5. (A desert village)** Families in the Thar collect every drop of rain. Name two traditional systems they might use.
**Model answer:** Tankas (underground household tanks) and johads/khadins (earthen check dams).

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Surface water: rivers, lakes, ponds, tanks; Himalayan rivers perennial, peninsular seasonal; easily polluted.
- Groundwater: in aquifers below the water table; wells, tube wells, springs; rich in the alluvial plains; purer.
- Scarcity: population, cities, industry, irrigation, over-pumping, pollution, wastage, seasonal and uneven rain.
- Conservation: rainwater harvesting, recharge pits and wells, check dams, drip and sprinkler, recycling, meters, awareness, traditional systems.
- Rainwater harvesting: catch rain where it falls; rooftop → filter → tank / recharge pit; johad, tanka, khadin, kul, zing, bamboo drip, eri; recharges groundwater, reduces floods and erosion, supply in the dry season.
- Irrigation needed: seasonal, erratic, uneven rain; thirsty crops; HYV seeds; evaporation; multiple cropping.
- Wells: cheap, independent; high water table, soft soil; northern plains. Tube wells: groundwater, soft soil, cheap power, fertile land; Punjab, Haryana, UP; costly; deplete groundwater.
- Tanks: peninsula (Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, AP, Telangana); hard rock, hollows, seasonal rivers; cheap; dry up, silt up, evaporation.
- Canals: inundation (floods only, no weir) vs perennial (barrage, all year); northern plains and deltas; Indira Gandhi Canal; waterlogging, salinity (reh, kallar).
- Drip: drops at roots; saves most water; costly, clogs. Sprinkler: spray irrigation; even spread; sandy, uneven, arid lands.
