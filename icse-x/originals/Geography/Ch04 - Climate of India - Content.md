## 1. Complete Lesson

### India's climate — the tropical monsoon type

- **Weather** is the state of the atmosphere at a place for a short time (a day or a week); **climate** is the average weather of a place over a long period (about 30 years).
- India has a **tropical monsoon climate**. The word **monsoon** comes from the Arabic *mausim*, meaning "**season**". A monsoon wind is a **seasonal (periodic) wind** that **reverses its direction** between summer and winter.
- In summer the winds blow **from sea to land** (south-west) and bring rain; in winter they blow **from land to sea** (north-east) and are mostly dry. About **75 per cent** of India's rain falls in the four months of the **south-west monsoon** (June to September).

### Factors affecting the climate of India

**1. Latitude**
- The **Tropic of Cancer** (23°30′ N) passes through the middle of India — through Gujarat, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram.
- The area **south** of it lies in the **tropical zone** — it is **hot throughout the year**, with little difference between summer and winter (e.g. Kochi, Chennai, Kanyakumari).
- The area **north** of it lies in the **sub-tropical/warm temperate zone** — hot summers and **cold winters** (e.g. Delhi, Amritsar).

**2. Altitude (height above sea level)**
- Temperature **decreases with height** — by about **1 °C for every 165 metres** (6.5 °C per kilometre).
- Therefore **hill stations** such as **Shimla, Nainital, Darjeeling, Shillong, Ooty and Kodaikanal** are **cooler** than the plains below; places high in the Himalayas receive **snowfall** in winter.

**3. Distance from the sea**
- The sea warms and cools **slowly**; land warms and cools **quickly**. Places **near the sea** therefore have an **equable (maritime) climate** — a **small annual range of temperature** (e.g. **Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Visakhapatnam, Kanyakumari**).
- Places **far from the sea** have an **extreme (continental) climate** — very hot summers, cold winters and a **large annual range** (e.g. **Delhi, Jaipur, Kanpur, Amritsar, Lucknow**).

**4. Winds**
- The **reversal of monsoon winds** gives India a **wet summer** and a **dry winter**.
- The **Western Disturbances** (temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean region) bring **winter rain** to north-west India; **local winds** such as the **Loo** and **Kalbaisakhi** affect summer weather.

**5. The Himalayas (relief)**
- The Himalayas **block the cold, dry winds** from **Siberia and Central Asia** in winter, so north India stays warmer than places at the same latitude in Central Asia.
- They **trap the south-west monsoon winds** and force them to **shed their moisture** over north India; without them the northern plains would be a desert.
- Thus the Himalayas act as a **climatic divide** between India and Central Asia — **cold and dry to their north**, **warm and humid to their south**.
- Other relief features matter too: the **Western Ghats** make the **west coast very wet** (windward side) and the **interior Deccan dry** (leeward, **rain-shadow** side); the **Aravallis**, lying **parallel** to the Arabian Sea branch, do not stop it, so **western Rajasthan** remains a desert.

**6. Jet streams**
- **Jet streams** are fast **westerly or easterly winds** blowing in a narrow belt high in the troposphere (about 9–14 km up).
- In **winter**, a **westerly jet stream** blows **south of the Himalayas**; it guides the **Western Disturbances** into north-west India.
- In **summer**, the westerly jet **shifts north of the Himalayas** and an **easterly jet** develops over peninsular India; this shift helps the **sudden onset ("burst") of the monsoon**.

**7. El Niño**
- **El Niño** is the periodic **warming of the ocean surface off the coast of Peru** in the eastern Pacific Ocean (every 3–7 years).
- It changes air pressure and wind patterns across the tropics and usually **weakens the Indian monsoon**, causing **droughts** (e.g. 2002, 2009). Its opposite, **La Niña** (cooler water), often brings a **good monsoon**.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2024, Q4(i))* "Himalayas act as a climatic divide." Justify the statement giving two reasons.
**Solution:** The Himalayas **block the cold Siberian winds** coming from the north, and they also **intercept the monsoons**, forcing them to bring rain to the country. Thus to the **north of the Himalayas** it is **cold and dry**, and to the **south** it is **warm and humid**.

### The monsoon and its mechanism

1. **Differential heating of land and sea** — in summer the land heats up far more than the sea. An intense **low-pressure area** forms over the **north-western plains** (Thar and Punjab), while **high pressure** lies over the **Indian Ocean**. Winds blow from sea to land — the **south-west monsoon**. In winter the pressure is **reversed**: high over the cold land, low over the warmer sea, and winds blow from land to sea — the **north-east monsoon**.
2. **Shift of the pressure belts (ITCZ)** — the belt of low pressure near the Equator (the **Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone**) moves north with the sun to about **20°–25° N** in July (the **monsoon trough**). The **south-east trade winds** of the Southern Hemisphere cross the Equator and are **deflected to the right** by the **Coriolis force** (Ferrel's law), so they blow **from the south-west**.
3. **Heating of the Tibetan Plateau** — the high plateau becomes a source of heat in summer and strengthens the low pressure and the **easterly jet** over India.
4. **Jet streams** — the northward shift of the **westerly jet** in early June allows the monsoon to **burst** suddenly.
5. **El Niño and the Southern Oscillation** — affect the **strength** of the monsoon from year to year.

**Characteristics of the Indian monsoon**
1. It is **seasonal** — about three-fourths of the annual rain falls in **June–September**.
2. It is **erratic and uncertain** — it may come early or late, and be heavy or weak.
3. It is **unevenly distributed** — over 1,000 cm at Mawsynram but less than 15 cm in parts of western Rajasthan and Ladakh.
4. It is mostly **orographic (relief) rain** — it falls heavily where moist winds are forced up mountains.
5. It has **"breaks"** — dry spells of days or weeks in the middle of the rainy season.

### The seasons of India

| Season | Months | Main feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Cold weather season (Winter)** | **December – February** | Cold, dry; NE (offshore) winds; Western Disturbances in the north-west; NE monsoon rain in Tamil Nadu |
| **Hot weather season (Summer)** | **March – May** | Hot, dry; low pressure over the north-west; Loo; pre-monsoon storms |
| **Advancing monsoon / Rainy season** | **June – September** | South-west monsoon: onshore, moist, rain over most of India |
| **Retreating monsoon season** | **October – November** | Monsoon withdraws; "October heat"; cyclones; rain on the Coromandel coast |

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2023, Q4(i))* Name the seasons of India and mention the duration of each season.
**Solution:** **Summer** — the hot and dry (hot weather) season, **March to May**; **Rainy season** — hot and wet, **south-west monsoon / advancing monsoon** season, **June to September**; **Retreating monsoon** season, **October and November**; **Winter** — cold and dry season, **north-east monsoon** season, **December to February**.

#### 1. The hot weather season (March – May)

**Temperature** — the sun moves north towards the Tropic of Cancer; temperatures rise from south to north. By May the **north-west** (Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana) records **45 °C or more**; the south remains a little cooler because of the sea.

**Pressure** — an intense **low-pressure area** develops over the **north-west** and **central India**; by **May** it covers the whole northern plains, and it begins to draw in moist winds from the sea.

**Winds and local storms**
| Local wind / storm | Where | Nature and effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Loo** | Northern plains — Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar | **Hot, dry, dusty** westerly winds blowing in the afternoon in May–June; cause **heatstroke** |
| **Dust storms (andhi)** | Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana | Bring slight relief from heat |
| **Kalbaisakhi / Nor'westers** | **West Bengal, Assam**, Odisha (called **Bardoli Chheerha** in Assam) | Violent **thunderstorms** with rain in the evening (April–May); good for **rice, jute and tea** |
| **Mango showers** | **Kerala, coastal Karnataka** (and Tamil Nadu coast) | Pre-monsoon showers that help the **early ripening of mangoes** |
| **Cherry (coffee) blossoms** | Karnataka | Showers that help **coffee plants to flower** |

#### 2. The advancing monsoon / rainy season (June – September)

**Onset and the "burst"**
- The south-west monsoon strikes the **Kerala coast** around **1 June**. It arrives **suddenly**, with heavy rain, thunder and lightning — this is called the **"burst of the monsoon"**. By about **mid-July** it covers the whole country.

**The two branches**
1. **The Arabian Sea branch**
   - It strikes the **Western Ghats** almost at right angles. The **windward (western) slopes** and the **west coast** — **Konkan and Malabar** (Mumbai, Goa, Mangaluru, Kochi) — receive **very heavy rain** (over 250 cm).
   - After crossing the Ghats, the winds descend and become **dry**; the **interior Deccan** (Pune, interior Maharashtra and Karnataka, Marathwada) lies in the **rain-shadow area** and receives little rain.
   - A part moves north into **Gujarat and Rajasthan**; since the **Aravallis lie parallel** to its path, it does not shed rain there — the **Thar** remains a desert.
2. **The Bay of Bengal branch**
   - It strikes **Myanmar** and **north-east India**. The moist winds are trapped in the **funnel-shaped valleys** of the **Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills** (Meghalaya) and rise sharply: **Mawsynram** and **Cherrapunji (Sohra)**, on the **windward** side, receive the **heaviest rainfall in the world** (over 1,100 cm); **Shillong**, on the **leeward** side, gets much less.
   - Blocked by the **Himalayas**, this branch turns **westward** up the **Ganga plains**. As it moves west it **loses moisture**, so rain **decreases from east to west** — Kolkata receives more than Patna, Patna more than Lucknow, and Lucknow more than Delhi.
   - It runs **parallel to the east coast**, so **Tamil Nadu (Coromandel coast)** gets **little rain** in this season; it also lies in the **rain shadow** of the Arabian Sea branch.

![Fig. 4.2 Relief (orographic) rainfall: heavy rain on the windward side of the Western Ghats and a rain-shadow area on the leeward side](figures/g_ch04_fig02_relief_rainfall.png)

**Temperature and pressure** — clouds and rain **lower the temperature** after the onset (June/July are cooler than May), but **humidity is high**. A **low-pressure trough** lies over the north.

#### 3. The retreating monsoon season (October – November)

- As the sun moves south, the **low pressure over the north weakens**; the monsoon winds **withdraw** from the north-west from September and from most of India by November.
- **Weather:** **clear skies**, a **rise in temperature** with **high humidity** — the uncomfortable "**October heat**"; nights become cooler.
- **Tropical cyclones** form over the **Bay of Bengal** and strike the east coast — **Odisha, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and West Bengal** — causing heavy rain and damage.
- The retreating winds (the **north-east monsoon**), after crossing the **Bay of Bengal**, pick up moisture and bring rain to the **Coromandel coast of Tamil Nadu** in **October–December**. Tamil Nadu receives most of its rain at this time.
- **Kerala**, the first state to receive the monsoon, is also among the last from which it withdraws.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2024, Q4(ii))* Differentiate between the Southwest Monsoon and the Retreating Monsoon (any two differences).
**Solution:** Any two:

| South-west Monsoon | Retreating Monsoon |
|---|---|
| **Onshore** — blows from **sea to land** | **Offshore** — blows from **land to sea** |
| **Low pressure over land**, high pressure over the sea | The **reverse** pressure condition |
| **June – September** | **October – November** |
| The **advancing** monsoon | The **transition** between the rainy and winter seasons |
| Brings **rain to most of India** | Brings rain to the **Coromandel coast**; "October heat" elsewhere |

#### 4. The cold weather season (December – February)

**Temperature** — the sun is over the Southern Hemisphere; temperatures **decrease from south to north**. North India is **cold** (January averages 10–15 °C in the plains); **cold waves**, **frost** and **fog** occur; the Himalayas receive **snow**. The south remains **warm** (Chennai and Kochi about 24–27 °C) because it is near the **Equator** and the **sea**.

**Pressure and winds** — **high pressure** develops over the cold north-western plains; **dry, offshore north-east winds** (the winter monsoon) blow from land to sea.

**Rainfall**
1. **North-west India** — **Western Disturbances** (temperate cyclones from the **Mediterranean**, guided by the westerly jet stream) bring **light winter rain** to **Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and western Uttar Pradesh**, and **snowfall** to **Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh**. This rain is very useful for the **rabi crops**, especially **wheat**.
2. **South-east India** — the **north-east monsoon**, after crossing the Bay of Bengal, gives rain to **Tamil Nadu**.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2018, Q3(a))* How is the winter rainfall of the north-west part of India different from the winter rainfall of the south-east part of India?
**Solution:** Rainfall in the **north-west** occurs due to **temperate cyclones** (Western Disturbances), whereas in the **south-east** it is due to the **winter monsoon / retreating (north-east) monsoon**.

### Distribution of rainfall in India

| Annual rainfall | Regions |
|---|---|
| **Over 200 cm (very heavy)** | **West coast** and windward Western Ghats; **north-eastern states**; sub-Himalayan West Bengal; Andaman and Nicobar |
| **100–200 cm (heavy)** | Eastern Ganga plains (Bihar, eastern UP), Odisha, Chhattisgarh, eastern Madhya Pradesh, eastern Maharashtra, coastal Tamil Nadu |
| **50–100 cm (moderate)** | Western Uttar Pradesh, Delhi, Haryana, Punjab (east), interior Deccan, Gujarat |
| **Below 50 cm (scanty)** | **Western Rajasthan (Thar)**, Kutch, **Ladakh**, the **rain-shadow** parts of the Deccan |

### Interpreting climate data

A climate-data table gives **monthly temperature** and **rainfall** for a station "X". Use these steps:

1. **Annual range of temperature** = **highest monthly temperature − lowest monthly temperature**. Always write the **unit** (°C).
2. **Total annual rainfall** = **sum of the 12 monthly figures** (in cm); for the **rainy season**, add **June–September**.
3. **Coastal or interior?**
   - **Small annual range (below about 10 °C)** and mild winter → **coastal** (maritime) station.
   - **Large annual range (above about 15–20 °C)** with a cold winter → **continental interior** station.
4. **Which wind brings the rain?**
   - Most rain in **June–September** → **south-west monsoon** (and the station is probably on the west coast or in the north).
   - Most rain in **October–December** → **north-east (retreating) monsoon** → station on the **Coromandel (east) coast**, e.g. Chennai.
   - Some rain in **January–February** with a cold winter → **Western Disturbances** — north-west India.
5. **Temperature drops in June/July** after a hot May → **onset of the monsoon** (clouds and rain).
6. **Hottest month** in **May/June** → north India; hottest in **April/May** with little change → south India.

![Fig. 4.3 A climograph: rainfall bars and a temperature line for a practice station](figures/g_ch04_fig03_climograph.png)

**Worked example (Practice Station P, Fig. 4.3).** Temperatures (°C): 14.3, 17.2, 22.9, 29.0, 33.4, 33.6, 30.6, 29.7, 29.0, 25.9, 20.1, 15.4. Rainfall (cm): 2.1, 1.8, 1.3, 0.9, 1.6, 7.3, 21.0, 22.8, 12.1, 1.5, 0.3, 1.0.
- Annual range = 33.6 − 14.3 = **19.3 °C** — large, so the station is in the **continental interior**.
- Total rainfall = **73.7 cm**; rainiest month **August**; the rain comes in **June–September** from the **south-west monsoon** (Bay of Bengal branch), with a little winter rain from the **Western Disturbances**.
- Temperature falls in **July** after the onset of the monsoon.

### Summary of the chapter

- **Monsoon** = seasonal wind that reverses direction; India has a **tropical monsoon climate**.
- **Factors:** latitude (Tropic of Cancer), altitude (1 °C per 165 m), distance from the sea (equable vs extreme), winds, **Himalayas** (climatic divide), **jet streams**, **El Niño**.
- **Mechanism:** differential heating, ITCZ shift and Coriolis force (SE trades become SW monsoon), Tibetan Plateau, jet streams, El Niño.
- **Seasons:** winter **Dec–Feb**; summer **Mar–May**; rainy **Jun–Sep**; retreating **Oct–Nov**.
- **Summer:** 45 °C in NW; low pressure; **Loo**, **Kalbaisakhi**, **mango showers**, dust storms.
- **Rainy:** burst at **Kerala ~1 June**; **Arabian Sea branch** — west coast heavy, Deccan rain shadow, Aravallis parallel; **Bay of Bengal branch** — Mawsynram, Ganga plains east → west decrease; Tamil Nadu dry.
- **Retreating:** clear skies, **October heat**, Bay of Bengal **cyclones**, **NE monsoon rain in Tamil Nadu**.
- **Winter:** cold north, warm south; high pressure, offshore NE winds; **Western Disturbances** (rain for wheat, snow in Kashmir); Tamil Nadu rain.
- **Climate data:** range = max − min; total = sum; small range → coastal; rain in Oct–Dec → NE monsoon.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The five keys
Most reason questions compare two places. Decide which key applies: latitude, altitude, distance from the sea, windward/leeward, or which wind. Write one sentence for each place.

### Section 2 — Two branches, two coasts
The **Arabian Sea branch** soaks the **west coast** and leaves the **Deccan** dry; the **Bay of Bengal branch** soaks the **north-east** and the **Ganga plains**, decreasing westward. **Tamil Nadu** waits for the **NE monsoon** in October–December.

### Section 3 — Winter rain has two sources
**North-west → Western Disturbances** (good for wheat, snow in Kashmir). **South-east → NE monsoon** (Tamil Nadu). Don't mix them.

## 4. Vocabulary (Key Terms)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Weather | Atmospheric conditions at a place over a short period. |
| Climate | The average weather of a place over a long period (about 30 years). |
| Monsoon | A seasonal wind that reverses its direction between summer and winter. |
| Onshore wind | A wind blowing from sea to land. |
| Offshore wind | A wind blowing from land to sea. |
| Equable climate | A climate with a small annual range of temperature (near the sea). |
| Continental (extreme) climate | A climate with very hot summers and cold winters (away from the sea). |
| Annual range of temperature | Highest monthly temperature minus lowest monthly temperature. |
| Windward side | The side of a mountain facing the rain-bearing winds. |
| Leeward side / rain shadow | The side away from the winds, which gets little rain. |
| Orographic (relief) rainfall | Rain caused when moist air is forced to rise over mountains. |
| Burst of the monsoon | The sudden onset of the monsoon with heavy rain and thunder. |
| Western Disturbances | Temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean that bring winter rain to north-west India. |
| Jet stream | A fast, narrow belt of wind high in the troposphere. |
| El Niño | Periodic warming of the eastern Pacific Ocean that weakens the Indian monsoon. |
| Loo | A hot, dry wind of the northern plains in summer. |
| Kalbaisakhi (Nor'wester) | Violent evening thunderstorms of West Bengal and Assam in April–May. |
| Mango showers | Pre-monsoon showers in Kerala and Karnataka that help mangoes ripen. |
| October heat | Hot, humid weather after the monsoon withdraws. |

### 5.2 Formulas for climate data

- **Annual range of temperature = Maximum monthly temperature − Minimum monthly temperature** (°C).
- **Total annual rainfall = Sum of all 12 monthly values** (cm).
- **Average temperature for a season = Sum of the months' temperatures ÷ number of months** (e.g. summer = March, April, May).

### 5.3 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- "Sohra" is the local name of Cherrapunji.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Unity in diversity of climate** — the same monsoon gives Mawsynram floods and Jaisalmer drought.
- **Relief shapes rain** — mountains decide which side is wet and which is dry.
- **A monsoon economy** — Indian farming, water supply and even prices depend on the monsoon.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **North-East India:** Meghalaya's heavy rain feeds its rivers and waterfalls; the same funnel-shaped hills make Shillong pleasant and Mawsynram the wettest place on Earth.
- **Weather alerts:** the IMD issues heavy-rain and cyclone warnings that save lives along the coasts.

#### Exercise 4A — Factors

**Q1.** Why are hill stations cooler than the plains?
**Answer:** Temperature falls with height — about 1 °C for every 165 m — so places at high altitude are cooler.

**Q2.** Why does Mumbai have an equable climate?
**Answer:** It is near the sea, which keeps summers and winters moderate.

**Q3.** How do the Himalayas protect India in winter?
**Answer:** They block the cold, dry winds from Siberia and Central Asia.

#### Exercise 4B — Seasons and winds

**Q4.** Name the hot dry wind of the northern plains in summer.
**Answer:** The Loo.

**Q5.** Which state first receives the south-west monsoon, and when?
**Answer:** Kerala, around 1 June.

**Q6.** What brings winter rain to Punjab?
**Answer:** The Western Disturbances (temperate cyclones from the Mediterranean).

#### Exercise 4C — Climate data

**Q7.** A station's highest monthly temperature is 33 °C and lowest is 26 °C. What is its annual range, and is it coastal or interior?
**Answer:** 7 °C; coastal (small range).

**Q8.** A station gets most of its rain in October–December. Which wind brings it, and where is it?
**Answer:** The north-east (retreating) monsoon; on the Coromandel coast (Tamil Nadu).

**Q9.** Why does temperature fall in June/July at most north Indian stations?
**Answer:** The onset of the monsoon brings clouds and rain, which lower the temperature.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (2 marks) What is El Niño? How does it affect India?
**Answer:** The periodic warming of the eastern Pacific Ocean off Peru; it usually weakens the Indian monsoon and can cause drought.

**Q2.** (3 marks) Give a reason for each: (a) Chennai has rain in November. (b) Jaisalmer is very dry. (c) June is cooler than May in Delhi.
**Answer:** (a) The NE monsoon picks up moisture over the Bay of Bengal. (b) It lies in the Thar, beyond the reach of rain-bearing winds and parallel to the Aravallis. (c) The monsoon's clouds and rain lower the temperature.

**Q3.** (2 marks) How do jet streams affect India's climate?
**Answer:** The westerly jet guides Western Disturbances into north-west India in winter; its northward shift in summer helps the sudden burst of the monsoon.

**Q4.** (3 marks) Explain why rainfall decreases from Kolkata to Delhi.
**Answer:** The Bay of Bengal branch enters from the east; blocked by the Himalayas it moves west up the Ganga plains; it sheds moisture as it goes, so less is left for places further west.

**Q5.** (3 marks) Station Y: temperature range 4 °C; maximum rain in July. Where is it, and which wind brings rain?
**Answer:** A coastal station on the west coast (small range); the Arabian Sea branch of the SW monsoon.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part II parts (2 marks each)

**Q1.** Name the seasons of India with their months.
**Answer:** Winter (Dec–Feb), summer (Mar–May).
Rainy/SW monsoon (Jun–Sep), retreating monsoon (Oct–Nov).

**Q2.** What is the source of winter rainfall in Punjab and Tamil Nadu?
**Answer:** Punjab — Western Disturbances (temperate cyclones).
Tamil Nadu — north-east (retreating) monsoon.

**Q3.** Justify: the Himalayas act as a climatic divide.
**Answer:** They block cold Siberian winds in winter.
They trap the monsoon and make it rain over India — north is cold and dry, south is warm and humid.

**Q4.** Differentiate between the SW monsoon and the retreating monsoon (two points).
**Answer:** SW monsoon is onshore (sea to land); retreating is offshore.
SW monsoon: June–September, rain over most of India; retreating: October–November, rain on the Coromandel coast.

**Q5.** Name two local winds of the summer season and where they blow.
**Answer:** Loo — northern plains.
Kalbaisakhi — West Bengal and Assam (also mango showers — Kerala, Karnataka).

**Q6.** Why does maximum rainfall occur after the summer season?
**Answer:** Summer heat creates low pressure over land.
It draws moist winds from the sea, which bring rain.

**Q7.** Mention two characteristics of the Indian monsoon.
**Answer:** Erratic and uncertain.
Unevenly distributed and mostly orographic (also seasonal; breaks).

**Q8.** What is meant by the burst of the monsoon? Where is it experienced?
**Answer:** The sudden onset of the monsoon with heavy rain, thunder and lightning.
Kerala, around 1 June.

**Q9.** Name two factors affecting the climate of India.
**Answer:** Latitude; altitude.
Distance from the sea; the Himalayas (also winds, jet streams, El Niño).

**Q10.** State two benefits of the Kalbaisakhi.
**Answer:** Helps tea, jute and rice in Assam and West Bengal.
Brings relief from the heat.

**Q11.** Name two states hit by tropical cyclones on the east coast.
**Answer:** Odisha.
Andhra Pradesh (also Tamil Nadu, West Bengal).

**Q12.** What is El Niño, and how does it affect India?
**Answer:** The periodic warming of the eastern Pacific off Peru.
It usually weakens the Indian monsoon, causing drought.

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Give reasons: (a) Kolkata gets more rain than Delhi. (b) Shimla gets snow but Ooty does not. (c) Winter monsoons bring less rain.
**Answer:** (a) The Bay of Bengal branch reaches Kolkata first and dries as it moves west.
(b) Shimla is higher and farther from the Equator than Ooty.
(c) Winter winds are offshore and carry little moisture.

**Q14.** Give reasons: (a) the interior Deccan gets low rainfall. (b) peninsular hills have no snow. (c) cricket in Chennai in winter is difficult.
**Answer:** (a) It lies in the rain shadow of the Western Ghats.
(b) The hills are not high enough.
(c) Chennai gets winter rain from the NE monsoon.

**Q15.** Give reasons: (a) Goa gets more rain than Puducherry. (b) Mawsynram is the rainiest place. (c) Mangaluru is cooler than Delhi in summer.
**Answer:** (a) Goa is windward of the Western Ghats; Puducherry is on the east coast.
(b) Funnel-shaped Khasi hills trap the Bay of Bengal branch.
(c) Mangaluru is coastal; Delhi is in the interior.

**Q16.** Give reasons: (a) Kanyakumari is equable. (b) Central Maharashtra gets less rain than coastal Maharashtra. (c) Jaipur has a higher range than Mumbai.
**Answer:** (a) It is near the sea and the Equator.
(b) Central Maharashtra is in the rain shadow; the coast is windward.
(c) Jaipur is continental; Mumbai is coastal.

**Q17.** Explain three factors that affect the climate of India.
**Answer:** Latitude — the south is hot all year; the north has cold winters.
Altitude — hill stations are cooler.
Distance from the sea — coastal places are equable, interior places extreme.

**Q18.** Describe the weather of the retreating monsoon season.
**Answer:** Clear skies and high temperature with humidity — October heat.
Cyclones over the Bay of Bengal.
Rain on the Coromandel coast from the NE monsoon.

**Q19.** Describe the two branches of the SW monsoon.
**Answer:** Arabian Sea branch — heavy rain on the west coast; rain shadow on the Deccan.
Bay of Bengal branch — heavy rain in the north-east; moves west up the Ganga plains.
Rain decreases westward; Tamil Nadu stays dry.

**Q20.** Describe the cold weather season.
**Answer:** Dec–Feb; temperatures fall northward; frost and snow in the north.
High pressure; dry offshore NE winds.
Western Disturbances in the north-west; NE monsoon rain in Tamil Nadu.

**Q21.** Give three differences between the summer monsoon and the retreating monsoon seasons.
**Answer:** Onshore SW winds vs NE winds / withdrawal.
Heavy rain and high humidity vs clear sky.
Rain over the whole country vs rain only on the Coromandel coast.

**Q22.** Explain how relief affects the climate of India with two examples.
**Answer:** Western Ghats — windward west coast very wet; leeward Deccan dry.
Khasi hills — Mawsynram very wet; Shillong drier.
Aravallis parallel to the winds — Thar is a desert.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Tropical monsoon climate; monsoon = seasonal reversing wind (*mausim*).
- Factors: latitude (Tropic of Cancer), altitude (1 °C/165 m), distance from sea, winds, Himalayas (climatic divide; Western Ghats windward/leeward; Aravallis parallel), jet streams, El Niño.
- Mechanism: differential heating; ITCZ shift; Coriolis turns SE trades into SW monsoon; Tibetan Plateau; jet streams.
- Seasons: winter Dec–Feb; summer Mar–May; rainy Jun–Sep; retreating Oct–Nov.
- Summer: 45 °C NW; low pressure; Loo; dust storms; Kalbaisakhi (Assam, WB — rice, tea, jute); mango showers (Kerala, Karnataka).
- Rainy: burst at Kerala ~1 June; Arabian Sea branch (west coast wet, Deccan rain shadow, Thar dry); Bay of Bengal branch (Mawsynram, NE India, Ganga plains east→west decrease); Coromandel dry.
- Retreating: clear skies; October heat; Bay of Bengal cyclones (Odisha, AP, TN, WB); NE monsoon rain in TN.
- Winter: cold north, warm south; high pressure; offshore NE winds; Western Disturbances (wheat; snow in Kashmir).
- Data: range = max − min; total = sum; small range → coastal; Oct–Dec rain → NE monsoon/Coromandel.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Tamil Nadu gets most rain from the SW monsoon | From the NE (retreating) monsoon in Oct–Dec. |
| Punjab's winter rain is the NE monsoon | It is the Western Disturbances. |
| Writing "range = 26.2" without the unit | Always write °C. |
| Mawsynram — Arabian Sea branch | Bay of Bengal branch. |
| Winter winds are onshore | They are offshore (land to sea). |
| Explaining only one of two places | Give the reason for both places. |
