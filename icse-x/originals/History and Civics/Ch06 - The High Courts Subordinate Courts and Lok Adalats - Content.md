## 1. Complete Lesson

### The High Courts

> - **Article 214:** there shall be a **High Court for each State**. Parliament may set up a **common High Court** for two or more States or Union Territories.
> - India has **25 High Courts**. The **Gauhati High Court** serves Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh; the **Meghalaya High Court** at Shillong was set up in **2013** (with the High Courts of Manipur and Tripura).
> - The High Court is the **highest court in a State**, below the Supreme Court.

**Composition**

- Each High Court has a **Chief Justice** and such **other judges** as the **President** decides from time to time — the number varies with the work (Allahabad has the most; small States have only a few).
- **Additional** and **acting** judges may be appointed for up to two years when work increases.

**Appointment**

- The judges are **appointed by the President**.
- The **Chief Justice** of a High Court is appointed after consulting the **Chief Justice of India** and the **Governor** of the State; other judges are appointed after also consulting the **Chief Justice of that High Court** (the collegium recommends names).
- The President can **transfer** a judge from one High Court to another, after consulting the Chief Justice of India.

**Qualifications** — a person must be a **citizen of India** and:
1. have held a **judicial office** in India for at least **10 years**; or
2. have been an **advocate of a High Court** (or of two or more High Courts in succession) for at least **10 years**.

> Since High Court judges retire at **62**, a person over 62 cannot be appointed. Unlike the Supreme Court, there is **no "distinguished jurist"** route.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2023, Q5(i))* Who appoints the judges of the High Court? Mention any two qualifications required for a person to be appointed as a judge of a High Court.
**Solution:** The **President**. Any two: must be a **citizen of India**; must **not be above 62** years; must have held a **judicial office** in India for at least **10 years**; must have been a **High Court advocate** for at least **10 years**.

**Term, removal and salary**

- A High Court judge holds office until the age of **62 years**; may **resign** by writing to the President.
- **Removal** is the same as for a Supreme Court judge — by the **President** after an **address** by **each House of Parliament** passed by a **special majority**, on the ground of **proved misbehaviour or incapacity**.
- Salaries are charged on the **Consolidated Fund of the State** and **cannot be reduced** during their term, except in a **Financial Emergency**.
- A retired permanent judge **cannot practise** in that High Court or in courts below it (but may practise in the Supreme Court and other High Courts).

### Jurisdiction of the High Court

#### 1. Original jurisdiction

Cases the High Court can hear **in the first instance** — directly, not as appeals:
- **Enforcement of Fundamental Rights** by issuing **writs** (Article 226). A High Court can issue writs not only for Fundamental Rights but also **"for any other purpose"** — that is, for ordinary legal rights — so its writ power is in this way **wider** than the Supreme Court's.
- **Election petitions** challenging the election of members of Parliament and of the State Legislature.
- Cases relating to **State revenue and its collection**.
- Some High Courts (such as Bombay, Calcutta, Madras and Delhi) hear **civil cases of high value**, and cases on **wills, marriage, divorce, company law, admiralty** and **contempt of court** in the first instance.
- Cases involving the **interpretation of the Constitution**, which it may **transfer** to itself from subordinate courts.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2025, Q5(i))* What is meant by the term Original Jurisdiction of the High Court? Mention any two cases which fall under this jurisdiction.
**Solution:** Original jurisdiction extends to cases that the High Court has authority to **hear and decide in the first instance** — cases that **come directly** to it. Any two: **wills**; **divorce and marriage**; **company law**; **admiralty**; **contempt of court**; **enforcement of Fundamental Rights (writs)**; **election petitions**; **State revenue and its collection**.

#### 2. Appellate jurisdiction

The High Court is the **chief court of appeal in the State**.
- **Civil cases:** it hears **appeals against the decisions of the District Judges** and other subordinate civil courts, especially in cases of high value or involving a question of law.
- **Criminal cases:** it hears **appeals against the decisions of the Sessions Courts** — for example, where a sentence of imprisonment of **more than seven years** has been given. A **death sentence** passed by a Sessions Court must be **confirmed by the High Court** before it can be carried out.
- It also hears appeals from many **tribunals**.

#### 3. Revisory (supervisory) jurisdiction

- The High Court can **call for the records** of any case decided by a **subordinate court** and **revise** (correct) its decision, if the lower court:
  - exercised a jurisdiction **not vested in it**, or failed to exercise one that was;
  - acted **illegally**, with an **error in procedure**, or against the **principles of natural justice**, causing a **miscarriage of justice**.
- It may **set aside** the lower court's verdict and give a new judgement.
- Under **Article 227** it has **superintendence** over all courts and tribunals in its territory (except military tribunals).

#### 4. Court of Record

Under **Article 215**, every High Court is a **Court of Record**:
- its **judgements and orders are preserved** as records for **evidence and future reference**;
- they serve as **precedents** that **all subordinate courts in the State** must follow in similar cases;
- it can **punish for contempt** of itself.

#### 5. Judicial review

Like the Supreme Court, a High Court can examine **State and Union laws** and **executive orders** and declare them **unconstitutional and void** if they violate the Constitution.

#### 6. Administrative functions

- **Controls** the subordinate courts — their **posting, promotion** and **discipline** (District Judges are appointed by the **Governor** in consultation with the High Court).
- Makes rules for the working of the lower courts and may **transfer** a case from one subordinate court to another, or to itself.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2025, Q5(iii))* Explain, with reference to the High Court, (a) Revisory Jurisdiction (b) Court of Record.
**Solution:** **(a)** If a **subordinate court** has exercised a jurisdiction **not vested in it**, or an **error or injustice** has happened, the High Court may **call for the record** of the case and **set aside** the subordinate court's verdict, announcing a new judgement. **(b)** Its **judgements and orders are preserved** as a record and can be **produced in any court as precedents**; subordinate courts of the State must follow them in similar cases; it can **punish for contempt** of court.

### Writs issued by the High Courts

The High Courts issue the same five writs as the Supreme Court (Chapter 5):

| Writ | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Habeas Corpus** | Produce a detained person before the court; release if the detention is illegal |
| **Mandamus** | Command a public official or body to perform its legal duty |
| **Prohibition** | Stop a lower court or tribunal from acting beyond its jurisdiction |
| **Certiorari** | Quash an order of a lower court or tribunal passed without jurisdiction |
| **Quo Warranto** | Ask a person by what authority he or she holds a public office |

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2026, Q1(v))* Mr Anand has been illegally detained by the police, and his family has not been able to meet him. The family approaches the High Court. Which writ will the court issue?
**Solution:** **Habeas Corpus.**

### The subordinate courts

Below each High Court is a **network of subordinate courts** in every district.

| Civil side | Criminal side |
|---|---|
| **Court of the District Judge** — the **highest civil court** of the district | **Sessions Court** — the **highest criminal court** of the district |
| Subordinate Judge / Civil Judge (Senior Division) | Chief Judicial Magistrate |
| Munsiff / Civil Judge (Junior Division) | Judicial Magistrates (First and Second Class) |
| Small Causes Courts | |

> **Revenue courts** (the Board of Revenue, the Commissioner, the Collector and the Tehsildar) decide disputes about **land revenue** and land records.

**Court of the District Judge and the Sessions Court compared**

| Point | Court of the District Judge | Sessions Court |
|---|---|---|
| Kind of cases | **Civil** cases — disputes over **property, land, money transactions, contracts, marriage, divorce, succession** | **Criminal** cases — **murder, robbery, dacoity, kidnapping**, serious crimes |
| Presided over by | The **District Judge** | The **Sessions Judge** |
| Status | Highest **civil** court in the district | Highest **criminal** court in the district |
| Appeals | Hears appeals from the lower civil courts; appeals from it go to the High Court | Hears appeals from Magistrates; appeals from it go to the High Court |
| Powers | Has **administrative** control over the subordinate civil courts of the district | Has **no administrative** functions; can pass any sentence, but a **death sentence** must be **confirmed by the High Court** |

> Very often the **same person** is both the District Judge and the Sessions Judge — called the District Judge when deciding civil cases and the Sessions Judge when deciding criminal cases.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2024, Q1(ii))* Civil Cases : Court of District Judge :: Criminal Cases : __________
**Solution:** **Sessions Court.**

### Lok Adalats — the People's Courts

> - **Lok Adalat** means **"People's Court"**. It settles disputes by **mutual agreement (compromise)**, with the help of a judge and other members, instead of a long trial.
> - The first Lok Adalat was held in **Gujarat in 1982**. The **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987** gave Lok Adalats **legal (statutory) status**. They are organised by the **legal services authorities** (the National Legal Services Authority, NALSA, and State and District authorities); **National Lok Adalats** are held in every district on fixed days.

**How a Lok Adalat works**

- It is presided over by a **serving or retired judicial officer**, with **one or two other members** — usually a lawyer and a **social worker**.
- It takes up **pending cases** (transferred from courts with the parties' consent) and disputes at the **pre-litigation stage** (before a case is filed).
- Typical cases: **motor-accident claims**, **family and matrimonial disputes**, **bank-loan recovery**, **electricity and water bills**, **land acquisition**, **labour disputes**, and **minor (compoundable) offences**. Serious crimes cannot be settled in a Lok Adalat.
- The procedure is **informal**: parties can speak for themselves **without lawyers**, an application can be made on **plain paper**, and **no court fee** is charged — a fee already paid in court is **refunded** if the case is settled.
- Its decision, called an **award**, is treated as a **decree of a civil court**; it is **final and binding**, and **no appeal** lies against it.

**Advantages of Lok Adalats**

1. **Speedy justice** — cases are settled in a day, saving years of litigation.
2. **Inexpensive** — **no court fee**, fees refunded, no need of lawyers; **free legal aid** to the poor.
3. **Spirit of compromise** — both sides agree to the settlement, so there is **no ill-feeling**; family and neighbourly relations are preserved.
4. **Reduces the burden** and **backlog** of the regular courts, so the higher courts can attend to serious cases.
5. **Final and binding** — no appeals, so the dispute ends.
6. **Pre-litigation settlement** — disputes are solved before they reach the courts.
7. **Justice at the doorstep** — Lok Adalats can be held **anywhere** — in villages, colleges, even mobile vans — and promote **social justice** for weaker sections.
8. **Informal and simple** — no complicated procedure or rules of evidence.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q1(vi))* A couple from Jodhpur separated for six years over petty family quarrels. Before the Lok Adalat Family Court, with counselling, they agreed to live together and withdrew the divorce petition. Which advantage of the Lok Adalat is highlighted?
**Solution:** It **works on the spirit of compromise**.

### Summary of the chapter

- **High Court:** one for each State (common courts possible); CJ + judges fixed by the President; appointed by the President after consulting the CJI, the Governor and the HC Chief Justice.
- **Qualifications:** citizen + 10 years judicial office or 10 years HC advocate; retirement at 62; removal like SC judges.
- **Jurisdiction:** original (writs — wider than the SC's, election petitions, State revenue, wills, marriage, divorce, company law, admiralty, contempt), appellate (civil from District Judges; criminal from Sessions Courts; confirms death sentences), revisory/supervisory, Court of Record (Art. 215), judicial review, administrative control.
- **Subordinate courts:** District Judge (civil, highest in district, administrative powers) v. Sessions Judge (criminal, highest in district, no administrative powers).
- **Lok Adalats:** People's Courts; Legal Services Authorities Act 1987; compromise; no court fee; award final and binding; speedy, cheap, reduces backlog.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The High Courts
Each State's highest court has its own Chief Justice and judges, chosen through consultation between the President, the CJI, the Governor and the High Court. Learn the differences from the Supreme Court: retirement at 62; no jurist route; salary from the State's Consolidated Fund.

### Section 2 — Jurisdiction
The High Court hears some cases first (original), most cases on appeal from the district, and supervises all lower courts (revisory). Its writ power is wider than the Supreme Court's because it covers legal rights too.

### Section 3 — The district courts
Civil and criminal justice in the district run on two ladders that meet at the High Court. The District Judge and the Sessions Judge are the top rungs; remember which is civil and which criminal.

### Section 4 — Lok Adalats
Lok Adalats make justice quick, cheap and friendly through compromise. Examiners give a situation and ask for the advantage it shows — match the clue (family reunited → compromise; decades-old case solved → speedy justice; no fee → inexpensive).

## 5. Important Facts, Formulas and Results

### 5.1 Must-know facts

- High Court for each State (Art. 214); **25** High Courts; Meghalaya High Court since **2013**.
- Judges appointed by the **President**; qualifications: citizen + **10 years** judicial office or HC advocate; retire at **62**.
- Writs under **Article 226** (wider: also "for any other purpose"); Court of Record under **Article 215**; superintendence under **Article 227**.
- **District Judge** — highest civil court; **Sessions Judge** — highest criminal court; death sentences confirmed by the High Court.
- Lok Adalats: People's Courts; first in **1982** (Gujarat); statutory under the **Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987**; award final, no appeal, no court fee.

### 5.2 Useful facts

- The oldest High Courts are those of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1862).
- The Gauhati High Court has benches at Kohima, Aizawl and Itanagar.
- National Lok Adalats settle lakhs of cases on a single day.

### 5.3 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- Many courts now hold e-Lok Adalats online.
- Family Courts and Consumer Commissions also help settle disputes outside the ordinary courts.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Justice Anna Chandy (1905–1996)
From Kerala, she became the **first woman judge of a High Court** in India (Kerala High Court, 1959), after being the first woman judge in a lower court.

### 6.2 Justice Leila Seth (1930–2017)
She was the **first woman Chief Justice of a High Court** in India (Himachal Pradesh, 1991) and the first woman judge of the Delhi High Court.

### 6.3 Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer (1915–2014)
A Supreme Court judge who championed **legal aid for the poor**; his report of 1973 and his work with Justice P. N. Bhagwati helped lay the foundation of free legal aid and the Lok Adalat movement.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Justice delayed is justice denied** — Lok Adalats and appeals procedures aim to make justice faster.
- **A ladder of courts** — the district courts, the High Court and the Supreme Court allow mistakes to be corrected at each level.
- **Justice for all** — legal aid and Lok Adalats bring courts within reach of the poor.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Your district court:** most people's first experience of the courts is at the district level.
- **National Lok Adalat days** are announced in newspapers; electricity and bank disputes are settled in large numbers.
- **The Meghalaya High Court** in Shillong hears appeals from all the district courts of Meghalaya.

### 8.1 Graded exercises — solutions

#### Exercise 6A — The High Court

**Q1.** Name the High Court that serves Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram and Arunachal Pradesh.
**Answer:** The Gauhati High Court.

**Q2.** At what age do High Court judges retire?
**Answer:** 62 years.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (3 marks) State three points of difference between the Supreme Court and a High Court.
**Answer:** Retirement — 65 v. 62; qualifications — SC also admits distinguished jurists and needs 5 years as HC judge; writ power — the HC can issue writs "for any other purpose" as well (also: jurisdiction over the whole country v. one State).

**Q2.** (4 marks) Explain the procedure followed in a Lok Adalat.
**Answer:** Presided over by a judicial officer with a lawyer and a social worker; takes up pending or pre-litigation cases with consent; parties can appear without lawyers, on plain paper, with no court fee; the award is a decree of a civil court — final, binding, no appeal.

**Q3.** (2 marks) Why is a death sentence passed by a Sessions Court sent to the High Court?
**Answer:** A death sentence must be confirmed by the High Court before it can be carried out, as a safeguard against error.

**Q4.** (3 marks) Name three kinds of cases suitable for Lok Adalats and one kind that is not.
**Answer:** Suitable — motor-accident claims, family disputes, bank-loan recovery (also electricity bills); not suitable — serious (non-compoundable) crimes such as murder.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part I questions (2 marks each)

**Q1.** Who appoints High Court judges, and in consultation with whom?
**Answer:** The President.
In consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor and the Chief Justice of the High Court.

**Q2.** State two qualifications of a High Court judge.
**Answer:** A citizen of India.
Ten years in judicial office or ten years as a High Court advocate (and not above 62).

**Q3.** What is the original jurisdiction of a High Court? Give one example.
**Answer:** The power to hear cases in the first instance.
Election petitions (or wills, divorce, State revenue, writs).

**Q4.** What is the revisory jurisdiction of a High Court?
**Answer:** Power to call for the record of a case decided by a subordinate court.
And correct the decision if the lower court erred or exceeded its jurisdiction.

**Q5.** Why is a High Court a Court of Record?
**Answer:** Its judgements are preserved and are binding precedents for subordinate courts.
It can punish for contempt of itself.

**Q6.** Name two writs issued by the High Court.
**Answer:** Habeas Corpus.
Mandamus (also: Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto).

**Q7.** State one difference between the District Judge and the Sessions Judge.
**Answer:** The District Judge decides civil cases; the Sessions Judge decides criminal cases.
The District Judge has administrative powers; the Sessions Judge has none.

**Q8.** What is a Lok Adalat?
**Answer:** A People's Court.
It settles disputes by compromise; its award is final and binding.

**Q9.** State two advantages of Lok Adalats.
**Answer:** Speedy justice.
Inexpensive — no court fee.

**Q10.** How do Lok Adalats help the higher courts?
**Answer:** They settle large numbers of cases by compromise.
So the burden and backlog of the higher courts is reduced.

**Q11.** Why is a death sentence passed by a Sessions Court referred to the High Court?
**Answer:** It must be confirmed by the High Court.
As a safeguard against error before a life is taken.

**Q12.** Why is the writ power of a High Court wider than that of the Supreme Court?
**Answer:** It can issue writs for Fundamental Rights.
And also for ordinary legal rights — "for any other purpose".

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Who appoints High Court judges? Mention two qualifications.
**Answer:** The President.
A citizen of India.
Ten years in judicial office or ten years as a High Court advocate.

**Q14.** Mention three cases under the original jurisdiction of a High Court.
**Answer:** Enforcement of Fundamental Rights (writs).
Election petitions.
Wills, divorce, marriage, company law (also: State revenue; contempt; admiralty).

**Q15.** Explain the appellate jurisdiction of a High Court.
**Answer:** Civil appeals from District Judges and lower civil courts.
Criminal appeals from Sessions Courts; confirmation of death sentences.
Appeals from tribunals.

**Q16.** Explain the High Court as a Court of Record.
**Answer:** Its judgements are preserved for evidence and reference.
They bind subordinate courts as precedents and cannot be questioned when produced.
It can punish for contempt.

**Q17.** Distinguish between the Court of the District Judge and the Sessions Court on three points.
**Answer:** Civil v. criminal cases.
Highest civil v. highest criminal court of the district.
District Judge has administrative control over civil courts; the Sessions Judge has none.

**Q18.** Mention three advantages of Lok Adalats.
**Answer:** Speedy justice.
Inexpensive — no court fee, no lawyers needed.
Spirit of compromise; reduces the burden of courts; award final.

**Q19.** Explain the working of a Lok Adalat.
**Answer:** Presided over by a judicial officer with a lawyer and a social worker.
Takes up pending or pre-litigation disputes with consent; informal procedure, plain-paper application.
Its award is final, binding and not appealable.

**Q20.** State three differences between the Supreme Court and a High Court.
**Answer:** SC judges retire at 65; HC judges at 62.
SC admits distinguished jurists; HC does not.
SC writs only for Fundamental Rights (Art. 32); HC writs also for other purposes (Art. 226).

**Q21.** Explain three functions of a High Court other than hearing cases.
**Answer:** Superintendence over subordinate courts (Art. 227).
Administrative control — posting and promotion of lower-court judges.
Judicial review of laws and orders.

**Q22.** Mention three kinds of cases settled by Lok Adalats.
**Answer:** Motor-accident claims.
Family and matrimonial disputes.
Bank-loan recovery, electricity and water bills (also: land acquisition, labour disputes).

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (4 marks each)

### Part II parts (4 marks each)

**Q1.** Explain the revisory jurisdiction of the High Court and the High Court as a Court of Record.
**Answer:** Revisory: calls for records of subordinate courts' cases.
Revisory: sets aside decisions made without jurisdiction or against natural justice and gives a new judgement.
Court of Record: judgements preserved and binding as precedents on subordinate courts.
Court of Record: punishes for contempt.

**Q2.** What are Lok Adalats? Mention three advantages.
**Answer:** People's Courts settling disputes by compromise.
Speedy justice.
Inexpensive — no court fee.
Reduce the burden of courts; awards final.

**Q3.** Explain four ways in which the Lok Adalat brings justice to the doorstep of the common man.
**Answer:** Held anywhere — villages, colleges, mobile vans.
No court fee; no lawyers needed; plain-paper applications.
Quick settlement by compromise.
Free legal aid to weaker sections; disputes settled before litigation.

**Q4.** Explain the original and appellate jurisdiction of a High Court.
**Answer:** Original: writs for Fundamental Rights and other purposes.
Original: election petitions; wills, divorce, company law; State revenue.
Appellate: civil appeals from District Judges.
Appellate: criminal appeals from Sessions Courts; confirmation of death sentences.

**Q5.** Describe the composition, appointment, qualifications and tenure of High Court judges.
**Answer:** Chief Justice and other judges as fixed by the President.
Appointed by the President after consulting the CJI, the Governor and the HC Chief Justice.
Citizen + 10 years judicial office or HC advocate.
Retire at 62; removable only by a special-majority address of both Houses.

**Q6.** Distinguish between the Court of the District Judge and the Sessions Court.
**Answer:** District Judge — civil cases (property, money, marriage).
Sessions Judge — criminal cases (murder, robbery).
District Judge — highest civil court; administrative control over lower civil courts.
Sessions Judge — highest criminal court; no administrative functions; death sentences need HC confirmation.

**Q7.** (a) How do Lok Adalats help the superior courts? (b) Name three writs issued by the High Court.
**Answer:** (a) They settle large numbers of pending and pre-litigation cases by compromise.
(a) This reduces the burden and backlog of the superior courts; no appeals reach them.
(b) Habeas Corpus and Mandamus.
(b) Certiorari (also Prohibition, Quo Warranto).

**Q8.** Explain the procedure and the nature of the award of a Lok Adalat.
**Answer:** Presided over by a judicial officer with other members.
Informal procedure; parties may appear without lawyers; no court fee.
The award is a decree of a civil court.
It is final and binding; no appeal lies.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Which court?)** (a) A land dispute between two brothers; (b) a robbery case; (c) a petition against illegal detention by State police. Name the court for each.
**Model answer:** (a) Court of the District Judge (civil); (b) Sessions Court (criminal); (c) the High Court (writ of Habeas Corpus) — or the Supreme Court.

**Q2. (The angry neighbours)** Two neighbours have quarrelled for years over a boundary wall and an electricity bill. Suggest a quick, inexpensive way to settle it.
**Model answer:** A Lok Adalat — settlement by compromise, no court fee, quick and final.

**Q3. (A wrong verdict)** A Munsiff decides a case that was outside his jurisdiction. What can the High Court do?
**Model answer:** Use its revisory jurisdiction — call for the record and set aside the decision.

**Q4. (Choose the judge)** Candidate A: 63, retired district judge with 20 years' service; Candidate B: 48, High Court advocate for 12 years; Candidate C: 40, law professor. Who can be appointed a High Court judge?
**Model answer:** Only B. A is over 62; C has neither judicial office nor 10 years' practice (the jurist route applies only to the Supreme Court).

**Q5. (A sentence of death)** A Sessions Court sentences a person to death. Can it be carried out at once?
**Model answer:** No — it must be confirmed by the High Court; the convict may also appeal.

**Q6. (Why compromise?)** Why might a family prefer a Lok Adalat to a regular court for a property quarrel?
**Model answer:** It settles the matter quickly and cheaply by agreement, so family relations are not damaged, and the award is final.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — A new High Court judge

> A judicial officer aged 55, with 15 years' service in the State judicial service, is appointed to the High Court. The newspapers report that the Chief Justice of India and the Governor were consulted.

**(i)** Who appointed the officer?
**Answer:** The President.

**(ii)** Which qualification did the officer meet?
**Answer:** At least ten years in judicial office.

**(iii)** Till what age can the officer serve?
**Answer:** 62 years.

**(iv)** From which fund is the salary paid?
**Answer:** The Consolidated Fund of the State.

### Case 2 — A day at the district courts

> In one court, a judge hears a dispute over a shop's rent. In another, a judge tries a case of dacoity and later sentences the accused to death. The same judge supervises the working of the Munsiffs' courts in the district.

**(i)** Which court hears the rent dispute?
**Answer:** The Court of the District Judge.

**(ii)** Which court tries the dacoity case?
**Answer:** The Sessions Court.

**(iii)** Who must confirm the death sentence?
**Answer:** The High Court.

**(iv)** Which judge supervises the Munsiffs' courts?
**Answer:** The District Judge.

### Case 3 — National Lok Adalat Day

> On a Saturday, Lok Adalat benches sit in every district. A bank and a farmer settle a loan dispute; a couple agree to live together again; an accident victim accepts compensation. No one pays a court fee.

**(i)** What kind of court is this?
**Answer:** A Lok Adalat — a People's Court.

**(ii)** Which advantage does the couple's case show?
**Answer:** The spirit of compromise.

**(iii)** Which advantage does "no court fee" show?
**Answer:** It is inexpensive.

**(iv)** Can the bank appeal against the settlement later?
**Answer:** No; the award is final and binding.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- High Court for each State (Art. 214); 25 HCs; Gauhati HC (Assam, Nagaland, Mizoram, Arunachal); Meghalaya HC (2013).
- CJ + judges fixed by the President; appointed by the President (CJI, Governor, HC CJ consulted); transfer by the President.
- Qualifications: citizen + 10 years judicial office / HC advocate; retire at 62; removal like SC judges; salary from the State's Consolidated Fund.
- Original: writs (Art. 226 — wider), election petitions, State revenue, wills, divorce, company law, admiralty, contempt.
- Appellate: civil (District Judges), criminal (Sessions Courts), death-sentence confirmation, tribunals.
- Revisory/supervisory (Art. 227); Court of Record (Art. 215); judicial review; administrative control.
- District Judge (civil, admin) v. Sessions Judge (criminal, no admin).
- Lok Adalat: People's Court; 1982 Gujarat; LSA Act 1987; judicial officer + members; compromise; no fee; award final, no appeal; speedy, cheap, reduces backlog, justice at the doorstep.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| High Court judges retire at 65 | At 62. |
| A distinguished jurist can be an HC judge | Only for the Supreme Court. |
| Sessions Court = civil cases | Sessions Court = criminal; District Judge = civil. |
| The Governor appoints HC judges | The President (the Governor appoints District Judges). |
| An appeal lies against a Lok Adalat award | No appeal; it is final. |
| Lok Adalats try all criminal cases | Only compoundable offences and civil disputes. |
| Court of Record for HC is Art. 129 | Art. 215 (Art. 129 is for the SC). |
| State revenue cases come under advisory/consultative jurisdiction | They come under original jurisdiction. |
