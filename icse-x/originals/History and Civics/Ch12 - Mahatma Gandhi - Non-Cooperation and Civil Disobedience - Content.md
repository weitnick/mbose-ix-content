## 1. Complete Lesson

### Gandhi's arrival on the national scene

- **Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi** (1869–1948) returned to India from **South Africa** in **January 1915**, where he had used **satyagraha** — non-violent resistance based on **truth (satya)** and **non-violence (ahimsa)** — against racial laws.
- His first struggles in India were local: **Champaran** (Bihar, 1917) for indigo cultivators, **Kheda** (Gujarat, 1918) for peasants unable to pay revenue after crop failure, and the **Ahmedabad mill strike** (1918) for workers' wages. These made him a leader of the masses.
- With Gandhi the national movement entered its **third phase — the mass phase** (1919–1947), when millions of ordinary people joined it.

**Principles of satyagraha**
1. Truth and **non-violence** in thought, word and deed.
2. Opposing **injustice**, not hating the opponent; winning over the opponent through **suffering** and moral force.
3. **Non-cooperation** with evil; **civil disobedience** of unjust laws, openly and with readiness to accept punishment.
4. Willingness for **dialogue** and **negotiation**.

### PART A — THE NON-COOPERATION MOVEMENT (1920–1922)

#### Causes of the Non-Cooperation Movement

**1. The Rowlatt Act (March 1919) — the "Black Act"**
- Passed on the recommendation of a committee headed by **Justice S. A. T. Rowlatt**, in spite of the opposition of every Indian member of the Imperial Legislative Council.
- Its **repressive provisions**:
  1. Any person could be **arrested without a warrant** and without giving any reason.
  2. Persons could be **detained without trial** (up to two years).
  3. Trials could be held **in camera** — the public and newspapermen were not allowed to attend.
  4. There was **no right of appeal** against the judgement.
  5. The police could **search any place** without a warrant.
  6. The **movement of individuals** could be restricted; security could be demanded.
  7. The right of **Habeas Corpus** was suspended.
- People summed it up as "**No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal**" (no argument, no lawyer, no appeal).
- Gandhi called a nationwide **hartal** on **6 April 1919** — the **Rowlatt Satyagraha**, his first all-India campaign.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2025, Q8(ii))* Mention any three repressive provisions of the Rowlatt Act.
**Solution:** Any three: the public and newspapermen were not allowed to attend the trial (**in-camera trial**); **no appeal** against the judgement; the police could **search any place**; **arrest anyone without giving any reason or warrant**; **restriction on the movement** of individuals; **suspension of the right of Habeas Corpus**; "**No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal**".

**2. The Jallianwala Bagh Tragedy (13 April 1919)**
- In Amritsar (Punjab), two popular leaders, **Dr Satyapal** and **Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew**, were arrested on 10 April 1919; there were protests and some violence.
- On **13 April 1919** (**Baisakhi** day), thousands of people — many of them villagers who had come for the festival — gathered peacefully in **Jallianwala Bagh**, an enclosed ground with a single narrow exit.
- **General Reginald Dyer** arrived with troops, **blocked the exit** and ordered his men to **fire without warning**. About **1,650 rounds** were fired until the ammunition ran out. Official figures gave **379 killed**; Indian estimates were over **1,000**, with many more wounded.
- **Martial law** followed in Punjab, with public floggings and the humiliating "**crawling order**".
- The **Hunter Committee** inquiry let Dyer off lightly; in Britain he was praised by some.
- Reaction: **Rabindranath Tagore** gave up his **knighthood**; Gandhi returned his **Kaiser-i-Hind** medal. Indians lost all faith in British justice.
- The **Jallianwala Bagh Memorial** — a tall, flame-shaped red-stone pillar — now stands at the site in **Amritsar**.

**3. The Khilafat Movement (1919–1922)**
- After the **First World War**, the **Ottoman Empire (Turkey)**, which had fought against Britain, was broken up by the **Treaty of Sèvres** (1920). The Sultan of Turkey was the **Khalifa (Caliph)**, the religious head of the Sunni Muslims; his power and the holy places were taken away.
- Indian Muslims felt betrayed, because the British had promised fair treatment for Turkey.
- The **Khilafat Movement** was launched in India by the **Ali brothers — Maulana Mohammad Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali** — with **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad**, **Hakim Ajmal Khan** and **Hasrat Mohani**. A **Khilafat Committee** was formed and **17 October 1919** was observed as Khilafat Day.
- **Gandhi supported** the Khilafat cause, seeing it as a golden opportunity to unite **Hindus and Muslims** against the British.

**4. Other causes**
- The **Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms** (1919) were disappointing — real power remained with the British.
- **Economic hardship** after the First World War — high prices, unemployment, heavy taxes.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2023, Q7(ii))* What were the three causes of the Non-Cooperation Movement? Name the incident which led to the suspension of this movement.
**Solution:** **The Rowlatt Act**; **the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre**; **the Khilafat Movement** (3). The **Chauri Chaura incident** (1).

#### The launch

- Gandhi launched the Non-Cooperation Movement on **1 August 1920** (the day Tilak died).
- The Congress approved it at a **special session in Calcutta** (September 1920, President **Lala Lajpat Rai**) and confirmed it at the **Nagpur session** (December 1920). The Congress changed its goal to the attainment of **Swaraj by peaceful and legitimate means**.

#### The programme of the Non-Cooperation Movement

**(a) Negative programme — boycott / non-cooperation**
1. **Surrender of titles** and honorary posts.
2. **Boycott of government schools and colleges**.
3. **Boycott of law courts** by lawyers and litigants.
4. **Boycott of foreign goods**, especially foreign cloth; bonfires of foreign cloth.
5. **Boycott of the elections** to the legislative councils (1920).
6. Boycott of official functions; refusal to serve in the army overseas.
7. In a later stage — **non-payment of taxes**.

**(b) Positive (constructive) programme**
1. **National schools and colleges** — Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith, Bihar Vidyapith.
2. **Panchayats** to settle disputes instead of law courts.
3. **Swadeshi** — spinning on the **charkha**, wearing **khadi**.
4. **Removal of untouchability**; promotion of **Hindu–Muslim unity**; **prohibition** of liquor.
5. Raising the **Tilak Swaraj Fund** (over one crore rupees) and enrolling members.

**The movement in action**
- Thousands of students left government schools; leading lawyers such as **Motilal Nehru**, **C. R. Das**, **Vallabhbhai Patel** and **Rajendra Prasad** gave up their practice.
- Imports of foreign cloth fell sharply; liquor shops were picketed.
- When the **Prince of Wales** visited India (November 1921), he was greeted by **hartals** and empty streets.
- The government arrested about **30,000** people, including almost all leaders except Gandhi.

#### Suspension — the Chauri Chaura incident (5 February 1922)

- At **Chauri Chaura**, a village in the **Gorakhpur district** of **Uttar Pradesh**, a police officer had beaten some volunteers picketing a liquor shop.
- A procession of about **3,000 peasants** marched to the **police station** to protest; the police **fired** on them.
- The angry crowd **set the police station on fire**; **22 policemen** were killed.
- Gandhi, who believed firmly in **ahimsa**, was deeply shocked. He felt the people were not yet ready for a non-violent struggle and **suspended the movement on 12 February 1922** (the Bardoli resolution).
- Gandhi was arrested in March 1922 and sentenced to **six years' imprisonment**.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2019, Q7(b))* Explain briefly the reason for the suspension of this particular movement by Gandhi in 1922.
**Solution:** The tragedy at **Chauri Chaura**, a village in the **Gorakhpur district** of Uttar Pradesh: a police officer had beaten some farmers picketing a liquor shop; a procession of about **3,000 peasants** marched to the police station to protest; the **police fired** at them; the peasants reacted and **set the police station on fire**, and **22 policemen were killed**. Gandhi, who believed in **ahimsa**, was greatly shocked and **withdrew the movement on 12 February 1922**.

#### Impact of the Non-Cooperation Movement

1. The national movement became a **mass movement** — it gave the Congress a **national base** among peasants, workers, students, women and traders.
2. It **instilled confidence, courage and patriotism**; the fear of British power vanished.
3. The Congress became a **revolutionary** (action-oriented) party, with a new organisation down to the villages.
4. It **undermined the power and prestige** of the British Government.
5. It **fostered Hindu–Muslim unity** (through the Khilafat link).
6. It **promoted social reforms** — removal of untouchability, khadi, national schools.
7. It **spread nationalism** to every part of the country and popularised the **cult of Swaraj**.
8. It **affected British trade** and showed the **power of passive resistance**.

> After the suspension, some Congress leaders (**C. R. Das** and **Motilal Nehru**) formed the **Swaraj Party** (1923) to enter the councils and oppose the government from within.

### PART B — THE CIVIL DISOBEDIENCE MOVEMENT (1930–1934)

#### Causes of the Civil Disobedience Movement

**1. Reaction to the Simon Commission (1927–28)**
- In **November 1927** the British appointed a **statutory commission** under **Sir John Simon** to review the working of the 1919 reforms.
- It had **seven British members** and **no Indian** — an insult to India.
- When it arrived in **February 1928**, it was greeted everywhere with **black flags**, hartals and the slogan "**Simon, Go Back**".
- At **Lahore** (October 1928), **Lala Lajpat Rai** was struck in a **lathi charge** while leading the protest and died a few weeks later.

**2. The Nehru Report (1928) and its rejection**
- Indian leaders answered the British challenge by drafting a constitution — the **Nehru Report** (Motilal Nehru), which asked for **dominion status**. The British ignored it. The Congress gave the government **one year** to accept it.

**3. The Declaration of Poorna Swaraj — the Lahore Session (December 1929)**
- The Congress met at **Lahore** under **Jawaharlal Nehru**. It declared **Poorna (Purna) Swaraj — complete independence** — to be its goal.
- On **31 December 1929** Nehru hoisted the **tricolour** on the banks of the **Ravi**.
- **26 January 1930** was celebrated as **Independence Day** all over India.
- The Congress Working Committee authorised Gandhi to launch **Civil Disobedience**.

**4. Other causes**
- The vague **Irwin Declaration** (October 1929) about dominion status, without any date.
- **Gandhi's eleven demands** to the Viceroy (January 1930) — including the **abolition of the salt tax**, reduction of land revenue and the release of political prisoners — were **rejected**.
- **Economic depression** (1929–30) hit peasants and workers.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2018, Q2(e))* State any two causes that led to the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1930.
**Solution:** Any two: the **Simon Commission**; the **Declaration of Poorna Swaraj** at the Lahore session (1929); the **Nehru Report**; the **Viceroy's declaration**; **Gandhi's eleven demands**; the **salt satyagraha**.

#### The Dandi March (12 March – 6 April 1930)

- **Why salt?** Salt was a basic necessity used by **every Indian**, rich or poor; the government had a **monopoly** on its manufacture and taxed it — the **salt tax** hurt the poorest most. Breaking the salt law was a simple act everyone could join.
- On **2 March 1930** Gandhi wrote to the Viceroy **Lord Irwin**, informing him of his decision to start Civil Disobedience.
- On **12 March 1930** Gandhi began his march from the **Sabarmati Ashram** (Ahmedabad) with **78 followers**, walking about **240 miles (385 km)** to **Dandi**, a village on the **Gujarat sea-coast**.
- He reached Dandi on **5 April**; on the morning of **6 April 1930** he **broke the salt law** by picking up a handful of salt left by the sea-waves — the **signal** for the whole country to **disobey** the government's laws.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2020, Q7(a))* Briefly explain the Dandi March of 1930.
**Solution:** Any three: on **2 March 1930** Gandhi wrote a letter to the **Viceroy** communicating his decision to start the Civil Disobedience Movement; on **12 March** he began his historic march from **Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi**, a village on the Gujarat sea-coast; **78 persons** followed him; he reached Dandi on **5 April**; on the morning of **6 April** he **violated the salt laws** by picking up some salt left by the sea-waves; it was Gandhi's **signal to disobey** the government's laws.

#### The programme of the Civil Disobedience Movement

1. **Defiance of the salt laws** — making salt everywhere along the coast.
2. **Boycott** of **liquor shops**, government **schools and colleges** and **government jobs**.
3. **Boycott of foreign cloth** and British goods of all kinds; picketing of shops.
4. **Non-payment of taxes** and **land revenue**.
5. **Violation of other laws** — for example, **forest laws**.

**How the movement spread**
- **Sarojini Naidu** led the satyagraha at the **Dharasana Salt Works**, where peaceful volunteers were brutally beaten.
- **C. Rajagopalachari** led a salt march to **Vedaranyam** (Tamil Nadu).
- In the **North-West Frontier Province**, **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** (the "**Frontier Gandhi**") led his **Khudai Khidmatgars** (Red Shirts); soldiers of the Garhwal Rifles refused to fire on unarmed protesters at Peshawar.
- In the north-east, the young **Rani Gaidinliu** of the Naga hills led a struggle against British rule.
- **Women** took part in large numbers — making salt, picketing shops and courting arrest.
- About **60,000** people were arrested, including Gandhi; the government was **paralysed** in many areas.

#### The First Round Table Conference (1930–31)
The British called a **Round Table Conference** in London (November 1930 – January 1931) to discuss constitutional reform; the **Congress boycotted it**, so it achieved nothing.

#### The Gandhi–Irwin Pact (5 March 1931)

- The British realised that no settlement was possible without the Congress. Gandhi was released in **January 1931**; authorised by the Congress President **Vallabhbhai Patel**, he negotiated with the Viceroy **Lord Irwin**. The pact (also called the **Delhi Pact**) was signed on **5 March 1931**.

| The British Government agreed to | The Congress agreed to |
|---|---|
| **Withdraw all ordinances** and **end prosecutions** | **Suspend** the Civil Disobedience Movement |
| **Release all political prisoners** except those guilty of violence | **Participate** in the **Second Round Table Conference** |
| **Permit peaceful picketing** of liquor and foreign-cloth shops | **Not press** for an investigation into **police atrocities** |
| **Restore the confiscated properties** of the satyagrahis | |
| **Permit people near the sea-coast** to collect / manufacture **salt** for their own use | |

> The pact was approved at the **Karachi session** (March 1931). It treated the Congress as an **equal** in negotiations — but many young nationalists were disappointed that it did not save **Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru**, who were hanged on 23 March 1931.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2023, Q7(iii))* Mahatma Gandhi and Lord Irwin signed a pact in 1931. Mention any two conditions that the Congress agreed to and two conditions that the British Government agreed to according to this pact.
**Solution:** **The Congress agreed to** (any two): **suspend** the Civil Disobedience Movement; **participate** in the Second Round Table Conference; **not press for investigation** into police atrocities. **The British Government agreed to** (any two): **withdraw all ordinances** and end prosecutions; **release all political prisoners** except those guilty of violence; **permit peaceful picketing** of liquor and foreign-cloth shops; **restore the confiscated properties** of the satyagrahis; **permit people near the coast to manufacture salt**.

#### The Second Round Table Conference (September – December 1931)

- Held in **London**; **Gandhi** attended as the **sole representative of the Congress**.
- The conference spent most of its time on the **communal question** — the **representation of minorities** (Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians, and the depressed classes) in the legislatures.
- Gandhi was disgusted to find that most delegates were concerned only about **seats for their own communities**; the question of **independence** or **responsible government** **receded into the background**.
- Gandhi returned **"empty-handed"** — the British would grant neither freedom nor even dominion status.

**Example 7.** *(ICSE 2019, Q8(b))* What was the significance of the Second Round Table Conference held in 1931?
**Solution:** Any three: **Gandhi** was chosen as the **sole representative of the Congress**; the conference devoted most of its time to the **communal question** and the **representation of minorities** — the Muslims, Sikhs, Christians and Anglo-Indians — in the legislatures at the Centre and in the provinces; Gandhi was disgusted to find most leaders concerned only about **seats for their communities**; the question of **independence or responsible government receded into the background**; Gandhi returned **empty-handed**, as he could not persuade the British to grant freedom or even dominion status.

#### Renewal of the Civil Disobedience Movement (1932–1934)

**Reasons for the renewal**
1. The **failure of the Second Round Table Conference** — Gandhi returned empty-handed.
2. **Repression** under the new Viceroy, **Lord Willingdon** — emergency **ordinances** were issued; leaders such as **Jawaharlal Nehru** and **Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan** were arrested even before Gandhi returned.
3. The government showed **no intention** of honouring the spirit of the Gandhi–Irwin Pact.

**Course**
- Gandhi **renewed** the movement in **January 1932** and was **arrested** on 4 January; the Congress was declared **illegal**; over a lakh of people were jailed.
- In **August 1932** the British PM Ramsay MacDonald announced the **Communal Award**, giving separate electorates to the depressed classes; Gandhi went on a fast, which ended with the **Poona Pact** (September 1932) with **Dr B. R. Ambedkar** — reserved seats within joint electorates.
- The movement gradually weakened; it was **withdrawn in 1934**.

#### Impact of the Civil Disobedience Movement

1. **Created patriotic feelings** and national pride.
2. **Widened the base** of the freedom struggle — peasants, workers, traders, tribal people and the people of the frontier joined.
3. Made people **understand the significance of non-violence**.
4. Brought an **improvement in the status of the depressed classes** (Gandhi's Harijan campaign after the Poona Pact).
5. **Women** became an active **part of the movement** in large numbers.
6. **New methods of propaganda** — **Prabhat Pheris** (morning processions), **Vanar Senas** (children's brigades), secret bulletins.
7. The **Congress took part in the legislative elections** (1937) under the **Government of India Act, 1935**, which was partly a result of the movement.
8. **Imports of British cloth** fell sharply; British trade suffered.

**Example 8.** *(ICSE 2026 and 2027 specimens, Q8(ii))* The movement which began with this March made a considerable impact on the social and political surroundings of the country. Analyse this statement by mentioning any three impacts.
**Solution:** Any three: **created patriotic feelings**; **widened the base** of the freedom struggle; made people **understand the significance of non-violence**; **improvement in the status of the depressed classes**; **women became part of the movement**; **new methods of propaganda** like **Prabhat Pheris** were used; the **Congress took part in legislative elections**.

### Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience compared

| Point | Non-Cooperation Movement | Civil Disobedience Movement |
|---|---|---|
| Years | 1920–1922 | 1930–1934 |
| Main causes | Rowlatt Act, Jallianwala Bagh, Khilafat | Simon Commission, Purna Swaraj (Lahore 1929) |
| Goal | Swaraj (within a year); redress of Punjab and Khilafat wrongs | **Purna Swaraj** — complete independence |
| Method | **Non-cooperation** — refusing to cooperate with the government (boycott) | **Civil disobedience** — deliberately **breaking unjust laws** (salt law, forest law, non-payment of taxes) |
| Starting event | Launch on 1 August 1920 | **Dandi March** (12 March 1930) |
| Ending | Suspended after **Chauri Chaura** (1922) | Suspended by **Gandhi–Irwin Pact** (1931); renewed 1932; withdrawn 1934 |
| Hindu–Muslim unity | Strong (Khilafat) | Weaker; Muslims took less part |

### Summary of the chapter

- **Gandhi:** satyagraha (truth + non-violence); Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad; mass phase.
- **NCM causes:** **Rowlatt Act** (1919, arrest without warrant, no appeal, in-camera trial — "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"); **Jallianwala Bagh** (13 April 1919, Dyer, Amritsar); **Khilafat** (Ali brothers; Caliph; Treaty of Sèvres); Montford reforms; economic hardship.
- **NCM programme:** surrender titles; boycott schools, courts, councils, foreign goods; national schools, panchayats, khadi, Tilak Swaraj Fund.
- **Suspension:** **Chauri Chaura**, Gorakhpur, 5 Feb 1922 — 22 policemen killed; suspended 12 Feb 1922.
- **NCM impact:** mass movement; confidence; Hindu–Muslim unity; social reform; nationalism everywhere; British prestige shaken.
- **CDM causes:** **Simon Commission** (all-white, 1928); Nehru Report; **Poorna Swaraj** (Lahore, Dec 1929, Nehru; 26 Jan 1930); eleven demands rejected.
- **Dandi March:** 12 March – 6 April 1930; Sabarmati → Dandi; 78 followers; 240 miles; salt law broken.
- **CDM programme:** salt laws; boycott of liquor, schools, jobs, foreign cloth; non-payment of taxes; forest laws; Ghaffar Khan, Sarojini Naidu, women.
- **Gandhi–Irwin Pact** (5 March 1931): British — withdraw ordinances, release prisoners, picketing, property, salt; Congress — suspend CDM, attend 2nd RTC, no inquiry into police.
- **2nd RTC** (1931): Gandhi sole Congress delegate; communal question; empty-handed.
- **Renewal** (1932): RTC failure; Willingdon's repression; withdrawn 1934.
- **CDM impact:** patriotism; wider base; non-violence; depressed classes; women; Prabhat Pheris; 1937 elections.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Three causes, one movement
The Rowlatt Act showed British injustice in law, Jallianwala Bagh showed it in blood, and the Khilafat question gave Hindus and Muslims a common grievance. Gandhi joined them in one movement.

### Section 2 — Non-cooperation vs civil disobedience
Non-cooperation means **refusing to help** the government (boycott). Civil disobedience goes further — **breaking unjust laws** openly and accepting punishment.

### Section 3 — The Dandi March
Know the dates (12 March – 6 April 1930), the places (Sabarmati → Dandi), the numbers (78, 240 miles) and why salt was chosen.

### Section 4 — The Pact and after
Learn the Gandhi–Irwin Pact as two lists — what the British agreed and what the Congress agreed. Then: Second RTC (failure) → renewal (1932) → end (1934).

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Mahatma Gandhi (1869–1948)
The leader of the mass phase of the national movement, who used satyagraha — truth and non-violence — in the Non-Cooperation, Civil Disobedience and Quit India Movements, and worked for Hindu–Muslim unity, khadi and the removal of untouchability.

### 6.2 The Ali brothers — Maulana Mohammad Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali
Leaders who launched the Khilafat Movement in India and joined Gandhi in the Non-Cooperation Movement.

### 6.3 General Reginald Dyer (1864–1927)
The British officer who ordered the firing at Jallianwala Bagh on 13 April 1919.

### 6.4 Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964)
President of the Lahore session (1929), which declared Purna Swaraj; later free India's first Prime Minister.

### 6.5 Lord Irwin (Viceroy 1926–1931)
The Viceroy at the time of the Simon Commission, the Dandi March and the Gandhi–Irwin Pact.

### 6.6 Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan (1890–1988)
The "Frontier Gandhi", who led the Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts) in the Civil Disobedience Movement in the North-West Frontier Province.

### 6.7 Sarojini Naidu (1879–1949)
Poet and leader, the "Nightingale of India", who led the Dharasana Salt Works satyagraha (1930).

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Non-violence as strength** — Gandhi turned suffering into moral force that a powerful empire could not easily answer.
- **Simple symbols move millions** — salt, the charkha and khadi let every Indian take part.
- **Means and ends** — Gandhi stopped a successful movement at Chauri Chaura because wrong means would spoil a right end.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Peaceful protest worldwide:** Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela were inspired by Gandhi's methods.
- **Rights today:** the Constitution protects personal liberty (Article 21) and the right to approach the courts — the very rights the Rowlatt Act denied.

#### Exercise 12A — Non-Cooperation

**Q1.** Why was the Rowlatt Act called the Black Act?
**Answer:** Because it allowed arrest without warrant and detention without trial, with no appeal — it took away basic rights.

**Answer:** General Reginald Dyer.

**Q3.** Who launched the Khilafat Movement in India?
**Answer:** The Ali brothers — Maulana Mohammad Ali and Maulana Shaukat Ali.

**Q5.** Why was the Simon Commission boycotted?
**Answer:** It had seven British members and no Indian.

**Q6.** Where and under whom was Purna Swaraj declared?
**Answer:** At the Lahore session (December 1929), under Jawaharlal Nehru.

**Q7.** From where to where did the Dandi March go?
**Answer:** From the Sabarmati Ashram, Ahmedabad, to Dandi on the Gujarat coast.

#### Exercise 12C — Pact and Conference

**Q8.** When was the Gandhi–Irwin Pact signed?
**Answer:** On 5 March 1931.

**Q9.** Who represented the Congress at the Second Round Table Conference?
**Answer:** Mahatma Gandhi, as its sole representative.

**Answer:** The failure of the Second Round Table Conference (also: Willingdon's repression).

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (4 marks) Describe the Jallianwala Bagh Tragedy and its effects.
**Answer:** On 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi), General Dyer blocked the exit of Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, and fired without warning on a peaceful crowd; hundreds were killed. Effects: Tagore gave up his knighthood; Indians lost faith in British justice; it became a cause of the Non-Cooperation Movement.

**Q2.** (3 marks) Give three items each of the negative and positive programmes of the Non-Cooperation Movement. *(Answer in two lists.)*
**Answer:** Negative — surrender of titles; boycott of schools and courts; boycott of foreign goods. Positive — national schools; panchayats; khadi and the charkha.

**Q3.** (3 marks) What was the significance of the Lahore session of 1929?
**Answer:** Under Jawaharlal Nehru it declared Purna Swaraj the goal; the tricolour was hoisted on the Ravi on 31 December 1929; 26 January 1930 was celebrated as Independence Day; Gandhi was authorised to launch Civil Disobedience.

**Q4.** (3 marks) Give three reasons for the renewal of the Civil Disobedience Movement in 1932.
**Answer:** The failure of the Second Round Table Conference; repression under Lord Willingdon — ordinances and arrests of Nehru and Ghaffar Khan; the government's disregard of the Gandhi–Irwin Pact.

**Q5.** (4 marks) Distinguish between the Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience Movements (four points).
**Answer:** Years (1920–22 vs 1930–34); goal (Swaraj vs Purna Swaraj); method (refusing cooperation vs breaking unjust laws); end (Chauri Chaura vs Gandhi–Irwin Pact, then 1934).

**Q6.** (2 marks) Why was the Simon Commission called an "all-white commission"?
**Answer:** All its seven members were British; there was no Indian member, though it was to decide India's constitutional future.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (A diary entry, 13 April 1919)** Write two sentences a survivor of Jallianwala Bagh might have written that night.
**Model answer:** "We had come only to celebrate Baisakhi and listen to the speeches; the soldiers fired without a word of warning and there was no way out. How can anyone still believe in British justice?"

**Q2. (Choosing a symbol)** Why was salt a better symbol for a national movement than, say, a demand for more council seats?
**Model answer:** Salt was used by every Indian, rich or poor, in every region; breaking the salt law was simple and open to all, whereas council seats mattered only to a few educated people.

**Q3. (Stop or continue?)** Do you agree with Gandhi's decision to suspend the NCM after Chauri Chaura? Give one reason for and one against.
**Model answer:** For — non-violence was the movement's foundation and violence would bring brutal repression. Against — one local incident stopped a nationwide movement at its height and disappointed millions.

**Q4. (A modern satyagraha)** Your town's council plans to tax drinking water. Suggest three satyagraha-style actions.
**Model answer:** A peaceful march; a signed petition and open letter; a refusal to pay the unjust tax, accepting the legal consequences.

**Q5. (Negotiation)** Why did the British negotiate with Gandhi in 1931?
**Model answer:** The movement had won worldwide publicity and paralysed administration; no constitutional plan could succeed without the Congress.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — April 1919, Punjab

> A law passed in March allowed the police to arrest people without warrant. In April, a peaceful crowd gathered on a festival day in an enclosed garden in a Punjab city; troops blocked the exit and fired until their ammunition ran out.

**(i)** Name the law.
**Answer:** The Rowlatt Act.

**(ii)** Name the garden and the city.
**Answer:** Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar.

**(iii)** Who ordered the firing?
**Answer:** General Reginald Dyer.

**(iv)** Which movement did these events lead to?
**Answer:** The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920).

### Case 2 — A long walk to the sea

> A leader set out from his ashram with 78 followers on 12 March 1930 and walked about 240 miles to a coastal village, where he picked up a handful of salt.

**(i)** Name the ashram.
**Answer:** Sabarmati Ashram.

**(ii)** Name the coastal village.
**Answer:** Dandi.

**(iii)** On which date was the salt law broken?
**Answer:** 6 April 1930.

**(iv)** Which movement began with this act?
**Answer:** The Civil Disobedience Movement.

### Case 3 — Delhi, March 1931

> After weeks of talks, a leader and a Viceroy signed an agreement. One side agreed to release prisoners and allow salt-making near the coast; the other agreed to suspend its movement and attend a conference in London.

**(i)** Name the agreement.
**Answer:** The Gandhi–Irwin Pact (Delhi Pact).

**(ii)** Name the Viceroy.
**Answer:** Lord Irwin.

**(iii)** Which conference did the Congress agree to attend?
**Answer:** The Second Round Table Conference.

**(iv)** What was the result of that conference for India?
**Answer:** Failure — Gandhi returned empty-handed.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Gandhi: back 1915; Champaran 1917, Kheda 1918, Ahmedabad 1918; satyagraha.
- NCM causes: Rowlatt Act (March 1919: arrest without warrant, in camera, no appeal, search, Habeas Corpus suspended — "No Dalil, No Vakil, No Appeal"); Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919, Dyer, Amritsar, ~1,650 rounds; Tagore's knighthood); Khilafat (Ali brothers; Caliph; Sèvres); Montford reforms; economic distress.
- NCM: launched 1 Aug 1920; Calcutta (Sept) and Nagpur (Dec) 1920. Negative — titles, schools, courts, councils, foreign goods, taxes. Positive — national schools, panchayats, khadi, charkha, untouchability, Tilak Swaraj Fund.
- Chauri Chaura: Gorakhpur, 5 Feb 1922; police station burnt; 22 policemen died; suspended 12 Feb 1922.
- NCM impact: mass movement; confidence; Hindu–Muslim unity; social reform; British prestige and trade hit; Swaraj Party 1923.
- CDM causes: Simon Commission (Nov 1927; arrived Feb 1928; seven British, no Indian; "Simon Go Back"; Lajpat Rai); Nehru Report; Lahore 1929 (Jawaharlal Nehru; Purna Swaraj; 26 Jan 1930); eleven demands.
- Dandi: 12 March – 6 April 1930; Sabarmati → Dandi; 78; 240 miles; salt law broken.
- CDM programme: salt laws; boycott of liquor, schools, jobs, foreign cloth; non-payment of taxes; forest laws; Ghaffar Khan (NWFP); Sarojini Naidu (Dharasana); women.
- Gandhi–Irwin Pact, 5 March 1931: British — ordinances, prisoners, picketing, property, salt; Congress — suspend CDM, attend 2nd RTC, no police inquiry.
- 2nd RTC (Sept–Dec 1931): Gandhi sole delegate; communal question; empty-handed.
- Renewal 1932: RTC failure; Willingdon's repression; Gandhi arrested; Communal Award; Poona Pact; withdrawn 1934.
- CDM impact: patriotism; wider base; non-violence; depressed classes; women; Prabhat Pheris; 1937 elections.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The NCM was suspended because of Jallianwala Bagh | Jallianwala Bagh was a cause; Chauri Chaura caused the suspension. |
| The Khilafat Movement was launched by Jinnah | By the Ali brothers. |
| Dandi March — 1920 | 1930. |
| The Gandhi–Irwin Pact ended the CDM for good | It suspended it; the CDM was renewed in 1932 and withdrawn in 1934. |
| The Congress attended the First RTC | It attended only the Second. |
| Simon Commission → after Dandi March | Simon (1928) came before Dandi (1930). |
