## 1. Complete Lesson

### The British expansion in India

After the battles of **Plassey (1757)** and **Buxar (1764)**, the **East India Company** became a ruling power in India. In the next hundred years it brought almost the whole subcontinent under its control by **four policies of expansion**:

| Policy | How it worked | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Outright wars** | Conquest in battle | Anglo-Mysore wars (Tipu Sultan, 1799), Anglo-Maratha wars, conquest of Sind (1843), Anglo-Sikh wars — Punjab annexed (1849) |
| **Subsidiary Alliance** | Indian rulers made dependent by treaty | Hyderabad (1798), Mysore, Awadh (1801), the Peshwa (1802) |
| **Doctrine of Lapse** | States of rulers without a natural heir taken over | Satara (1848), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854) |
| **Pretext of misgovernance** | States annexed on the excuse of bad government | **Awadh (1856)** |

#### The Subsidiary Alliance (Lord Wellesley, 1798)

**Lord Wellesley**, Governor-General from 1798 to 1805, introduced the **Subsidiary Alliance** to bring Indian states under British control without war. A ruler who accepted it agreed that:
1. the British would be the **supreme (paramount) power**; the ruler would accept their **protection**;
2. a **British army** would be **stationed in the state**, and the ruler would **pay for its maintenance** — in cash or by giving up **territory**;
3. a **British Resident** would live at the ruler's court;
4. the ruler would **not make war, alliances or treaties** with any other power, or **negotiate** with other states, without British consent;
5. the ruler would **not employ any European** (other than the British) without the Company's permission;
6. in return, the British promised to **protect** the state from external attack and internal revolt, and not to interfere in its internal affairs.

> **Why it was disastrous for Indian rulers:** they **lost their independence**; the cost of the British troops **ruined** their finances and they often had to give up land; the **Resident** came to control their **internal affairs**; and, protected by British arms, many rulers grew careless of their people. **Hyderabad** was the first state to accept it (1798).

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2027 specimen, Q6(iii))* The Subsidiary Alliance proved to be disastrous to the Indian kingdoms. Justify this statement by stating any four clauses of this alliance.
**Solution:** The Indian rulers who accepted it **accepted the British as the supreme power** and **lost their independence**; they agreed to **maintain British troops at their own cost**; they **accepted a British Resident** at their court; they **could not enter into any alliance** with other European powers.

#### The Doctrine of Lapse (Lord Dalhousie)

**Lord Dalhousie**, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856, applied the **Doctrine of Lapse**: if the ruler of a dependent state **died without a natural (biological) male heir**, the state would **lapse** (pass) to the British. An **adopted son** could inherit the ruler's **personal property**, but **not the kingdom**; rulers had to take British **permission to adopt**. In this way **Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambalpur (1849), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853) and Nagpur (1854)** were annexed.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2025, Q6(i))* What was the Doctrine of Lapse? Who introduced it?
**Solution:** It was a **policy of expansion** under which, if a king **did not have a natural heir**, the kingdom **lapsed to the British**; an **adopted son was not allowed to rule**, and rulers had to take British permission to adopt. It was introduced by **Lord Dalhousie**.

#### Annexation of Awadh (1856)

**Awadh** had been loyal to the British and was under the Subsidiary Alliance since 1801. In **1856** Dalhousie annexed it on the **pretext (excuse) of misgovernance** by Nawab **Wajid Ali Shah**. Since many sepoys of the Bengal Army came from Awadh, this annexation angered them deeply.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2026, Q1(xi))* Under which policy of expansion was the place marked on the map [Awadh] annexed by the British?
**Solution:** **The pretext of misgovernance.**

### Causes of the Revolt of 1857

#### 1. Political causes

1. **Policy of expansion** — outright wars, the **Subsidiary Alliance**, the **Doctrine of Lapse** and annexation on the pretext of **misgovernance** made every Indian ruler feel insecure.
2. **Doctrine of Lapse** — rulers such as **Rani Lakshmi Bai** of Jhansi were denied the right to adopt an heir to the throne.
3. **Annexation of Awadh** — the deposed Nawab's followers and the sepoys from Awadh were bitter.
4. **Disrespect to the Mughal emperor** — Dalhousie announced that Bahadur Shah's successors would have to leave the Red Fort; **Canning** announced that the title of emperor would end with him. Indians felt humiliated.
5. **Treatment of Nana Sahib** — the British stopped the **pension** of **Nana Sahib**, adopted son of the last Peshwa, Baji Rao II.
6. **Absentee sovereignty** — India was ruled from **England**, by people who had no interest in India's welfare; its wealth flowed out.
7. **Unpopular administration** — corruption in the police and the courts, and the **exclusion of Indians from high posts**.

**Example 4.** *(ICSE 2018, Q6(a))* Explain any three political causes of the Revolt of 1857.
**Solution:** Any three: the **policy of expansion** — outright wars, the Subsidiary Alliance; the **Doctrine of Lapse**; the **annexations** (Awadh); **discourtesy to the Mughal emperor**; the **treatment of Nana Sahib**; an **unpopular administration**; **absentee sovereignty**.

#### 2. Socio-religious causes

1. **Interference in social customs** — laws abolishing **sati** (1829), allowing **widow remarriage** (1856) and suppressing female infanticide alarmed orthodox people, who felt their customs were under attack.
2. **Activities of Christian missionaries** — missionaries openly criticised Indian religions and tried to **convert** people; converts were favoured.
3. **Religious Disabilities Act (1850)** — this **law of property** allowed a person who changed religion to **inherit his father's property**, which was seen as encouraging conversion.
4. **Taxing religious places** — lands belonging to temples and mosques were taxed.
5. **Fears about modern innovations** — railways, the telegraph and **Western education** were thought to be means of destroying caste and religion; travelling together in trains seemed to break caste rules.
6. **Racial discrimination** — the British treated Indians as inferior; Indians were insulted and kept out of clubs, railway compartments and high posts.

#### 3. Economic causes

1. **Exploitation of resources and drain of wealth** — India's wealth was drained to Britain.
2. **Decay of cottage industries and handicrafts** — cheap **machine-made British goods** entered India almost duty-free, while Indian goods paid heavy duties in Britain; **spinning and weaving** declined and **artisans lost their livelihood**. The artisans also lost the **patronage** of the Indian courts that had been annexed.
3. **Ruin of the peasantry** — high **land revenue** under new systems, harsh collection, and the loss of land to **moneylenders**; **indigo cultivators** were cruelly treated; repeated **famines**.
4. **Decline of the landed aristocracy** — the **Inam Commission** confiscated thousands of rent-free estates in Bombay.
5. **Unemployment** — soldiers and officials of annexed states lost their jobs.
6. India was turned into a **supplier of raw materials** and a **market** for British goods — an agricultural colony.

**Example 5.** *(ICSE 2026 and 2027 specimens, Q2(iv))* Imagine you were an artisan in the year 1856. Mention any two ways in which the economic policies of the British would have affected you.
**Solution:** Any two: **loss of livelihood** — spinning and weaving stopped; **competition from machine-made goods**; **heavy taxation**; **loss of patronage** of the Indian courts; **non-availability of raw materials**.

#### 4. Military causes

1. **Ill-treatment** — Indian sepoys were insulted and treated with contempt by British officers.
2. **Low pay and allowances** — sepoys were **poorly paid**, ill-fed and badly housed, while British soldiers were paid far more.
3. **Bleak prospects of promotion** — the highest post an Indian could reach was that of a **subedar**.
4. **General Service Enlistment Act (1856)** — introduced under Lord **Canning**, it laid down that new recruits must be ready to **serve overseas** if required. **Crossing the sea** was against the religious beliefs of many sepoys, especially high-caste Hindus, who feared losing caste.
5. **Withdrawal of allowances** — the **bhatta** (foreign-service allowance) for serving in Sind and Punjab was stopped; the **free postage** sepoys enjoyed was withdrawn (1854).
6. **Interference with customs** — sepoys were forbidden to wear **caste or sect marks**, beards or turbans.
7. **Larger proportion of Indians** — Indians far outnumbered British troops, and many units were badly distributed; the British defeats in the **First Afghan War** and the Crimean War destroyed the idea that they could not be beaten.
8. **Discontent in the Bengal Army** — many of its sepoys came from **Awadh** and were angered by its annexation.

**Example 6.** *(ICSE 2019, Q2(a))* What was the General Service Enlistment Act?
**Solution:** An Act **passed in 1856** under which Indian soldiers of the Company's army could be **sent overseas** on duty. Going overseas was **taboo** for many Indian soldiers, especially Brahmins, as it went against their **religious sentiments**.

#### 5. The immediate cause — the Enfield rifle

- In 1857 the British introduced the new **Enfield rifle**. Its **cartridges** were wrapped in **greased paper**, and the sepoy had to **bite off** the end before loading.
- A rumour spread that the grease contained the **fat of cows and pigs**. The **cow is sacred to Hindus**, and the **pig is taboo to Muslims** — so both believed the British were deliberately trying to **destroy their religion**.
- The sepoys **refused to use** the cartridges. On **29 March 1857**, **Mangal Pandey** of the 34th Native Infantry attacked his officers at **Barrackpore**; he was hanged. On **10 May 1857** the sepoys at **Meerut** rose in revolt.

**Example 7.** *(ICSE 2018, Q6(b))* Briefly explain the immediate cause of the Great Revolt.
**Solution:** The introduction of the **Enfield rifle**: its cartridges were **greased with animal fat** (of cows and pigs), and the sepoys had to **bite them off**, which went against the **religious sentiments** of both Hindus and Muslims.

### The course of the Revolt (for continuity)

- On **10 May 1857** the sepoys at **Meerut** killed their officers and marched to **Delhi**, where on 11 May they proclaimed the aged **Bahadur Shah Zafar** Emperor of Hindustan.
- The Revolt spread across north and central India. Its leaders included:

| Centre | Leaders |
|---|---|
| Delhi | Bahadur Shah Zafar, General Bakht Khan |
| Kanpur | **Nana Sahib**, **Tantia Tope** |
| Lucknow (Awadh) | **Begum Hazrat Mahal** |
| Jhansi | **Rani Lakshmi Bai** |
| Bihar (Jagdishpur) | **Kunwar Singh** |
| Faizabad | Maulvi Ahmadullah |

- The British recaptured **Delhi in September 1857**; Lucknow and Kanpur were retaken; **Rani Lakshmi Bai** died fighting near **Gwalior** in June 1858; **Tantia Tope** was captured and executed in 1859. By **1858–59** the Revolt was crushed.
- **Why it failed:** it was **limited** to parts of the north and centre; many rulers and the educated classes **did not join**; the rebels lacked **unity, planning and a common aim**; the British had **better arms**, **resources** and communications (the telegraph).

> **Its nature:** the British called it a **"Sepoy Mutiny"**; Indian nationalists, especially V. D. Savarkar (1909), called it the **First War of Independence** — the first large, combined uprising of soldiers, princes, peasants and artisans against British rule.

### Consequences of the Revolt

#### 1. Changes in the administration

- The **Government of India Act, 1858** **ended the rule of the East India Company**; the **British Crown** took over the government of India directly.
- The Company's **Board of Control** and **Court of Directors** were **abolished**.
- A member of the British Cabinet was made **Secretary of State for India**, responsible for all matters of Indian government, with a **Council of India** of **15 members** to advise him.
- Actual governance continued under the **Governor-General**, who was now also called the **Viceroy** (the Crown's representative); **Lord Canning** became the first Viceroy.
- Appointments to the **Indian Civil Service** were to be made by **open competition**.

**Example 8.** *(ICSE 2024, Q6(i))* Mention any three changes made in the administration with the end of the Company's rule.
**Solution:** Any three: the government of India was assumed **directly by the Crown**; the Company's **Board of Control and Court of Directors were abolished**; a **Secretary of State for India**, a member of the British Cabinet, was made responsible for Indian affairs, advised by an **Indian Council**; the **Governor-General** was given the title of **Viceroy**; appointments to the Civil Service were made by **open competition**.

#### 2. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1 November 1858)

Read out by Lord Canning at **Allahabad**, it announced the policies of the Crown:
1. **No further annexations** — the British would not extend their territories.
2. The **rights, dignity and honour** of the **native princes** would be respected, and their **treaties** honoured; they could **adopt heirs** (the Doctrine of Lapse was given up).
3. **Non-interference** in the **religious and social** matters of Indians.
4. **Equal treatment** of all subjects, Indian and European; **appointments** would be based on **education, ability and integrity**, regardless of race or creed.
5. A **general pardon** to all who had taken part in the Revolt, **except** those guilty of **murdering British subjects**.
6. Respect for the **ancient rights, usages and customs** of India.
7. The government would **promote industries** and **works of public utility** for the material and moral progress of the people.

#### 3. Relations with the princely states

- The policy of **annexation ended**; the **Doctrine of Lapse** was dropped and princes were allowed to **adopt** heirs.
- The princes, many of whom had stayed loyal in 1857, became **allies** of the British — "**breakwaters**" against future storms.
- But the **paramountcy** of the Crown was asserted: the princes ruled under British supervision, and the British could intervene in case of misgovernment.

#### 4. Changes in the army

To prevent another revolt, the army was **reorganised** (on the advice of the **Peel Commission**, 1859):
1. The **number of European troops** was **increased** (in the Bengal Army, one European to two Indians).
2. European troops were kept in **key geographical and military positions**.
3. **Artillery** and **sophisticated weapons** were kept only with European troops — almost all Indian **artillery units**, except a few mountain units, were **disbanded**.
4. **Discrimination** on the basis of **caste, region and religion** was practised in recruitment; soldiers from "**martial races**" (Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans) were preferred, and the Bengal Army was reduced.
5. **Caste and community** units were introduced in regiments, so that soldiers would not unite — **divide and rule**.
6. **Newspapers, journals** and nationalist publications were **kept away** from the soldiers.

#### 5. End of the Mughals and the Peshwas

- The **Mughal dynasty ended**: **Bahadur Shah Zafar** was tried and **deported to Rangoon** (Myanmar), where he died in 1862; his sons were shot.
- The line of the **Peshwas ended**: **Nana Sahib** fled to **Nepal**.

**Example 9.** *(ICSE 2026 specimen, Q6(ii))* Analyse the impact of the failure of the Revolt of 1857 on the Mughals and the Peshwas.
**Solution:** The **rule of the Mughals came to an end**, as **Bahadur Shah was deported**; the **rule of the Peshwas ended**, as **Nana Sahib fled** from the country.

### Summary of the chapter

- **Expansion:** outright wars; Subsidiary Alliance (Wellesley, 1798); Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie); misgovernance (Awadh, 1856).
- **Causes:** political (expansion, lapse, Awadh, Mughal emperor insulted, Nana Sahib, absentee rule); socio-religious (social reforms, missionaries, Religious Disabilities Act, fears of innovations, racial discrimination); economic (drain, ruin of handicrafts and peasants, unemployment); military (ill-treatment, low pay, no promotion, General Service Enlistment Act 1856, loss of allowances).
- **Immediate cause:** greased cartridges of the Enfield rifle; Mangal Pandey; Meerut, 10 May 1857.
- **Consequences:** Crown rule (Act of 1858 — Secretary of State + Council of 15; Viceroy); Queen Victoria's Proclamation; princes as allies; army reorganised (more Europeans, artillery with Europeans, divide and rule); end of Mughals and Peshwas.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Expansion
The Company expanded by war, by treaty (Subsidiary Alliance), by inheritance law (Doctrine of Lapse) and by accusation (misgovernance). Each policy left a group of angry rulers and their followers.

### Section 2 — Causes
The Revolt had many causes acting together. In answers, name the group and give specific points: "Military — the General Service Enlistment Act (1856) required service overseas, which was against the religion of high-caste sepoys."

### Section 3 — The immediate cause
The greased cartridges united Hindu and Muslim sepoys in anger. Explain why each community was offended.

### Section 4 — Consequences
The Revolt changed British policy: the Crown took over, the princes were reassured, Indians were promised equality, and the army was reorganised to divide its soldiers.

## 4. Vocabulary (Key Terms)

|---|---|

| Subsidiary Alliance | Wellesley's system under which Indian rulers kept British troops at their cost and accepted British supremacy. |

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Mangal Pandey (1827–1857)
A sepoy of the **34th Bengal Native Infantry**, he attacked his British officers at **Barrackpore** on 29 March 1857 over the greased cartridges and was hanged on 8 April. He is remembered as the first martyr of 1857.

### 6.2 Rani Lakshmi Bai of Jhansi (c. 1828–1858)
When Dalhousie annexed **Jhansi** under the Doctrine of Lapse, refusing to recognise her adopted son, she led its defence in 1857–58 and **died fighting** near **Gwalior** in June 1858 — a symbol of courage in Indian history.

### 6.3 Bahadur Shah Zafar (1775–1862)
The **last Mughal emperor**, a poet, was proclaimed Emperor of Hindustan by the rebel sepoys in May 1857. After the fall of Delhi he was tried and **exiled to Rangoon**, ending the Mughal dynasty.

### 6.4 Nana Sahib (1824–?) and Tantia Tope (1814–1859)
**Nana Sahib**, adopted son of the last Peshwa, led the revolt at **Kanpur** after the British stopped his pension; he fled to Nepal and disappeared. His general **Tantia Tope** fought a brilliant guerrilla campaign until he was captured and executed in 1859.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Many grievances, one spark** — political, economic, social and military discontent exploded over the cartridges.
- **Unity across communities** — Hindus and Muslims, princes and peasants fought together, showing the possibility of common action.
- **Change after crisis** — the British changed their methods (Crown rule, conciliation of princes, divide and rule in the army).

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Place names and memorials:** roads and schools named after Rani Lakshmi Bai and Tantia Tope keep the memory alive.
- **Local history:** find out whether your region saw any rising in 1857 — for example, in the north-east the Revolt reached Assam, where Maniram Dewan was executed in 1858.

### 8.3 More practice in the ICSE style

**Q1.** (3 marks) Why did the Revolt of 1857 fail? Give three reasons.
**Answer:** It was confined to parts of north and central India; many rulers and the educated classes did not support it; the rebels lacked unity, planning and a common goal, while the British had better arms, resources and communications.

**Q2.** (2 marks) Why is the Revolt called the "First War of Independence"?
**Answer:** It was the first large uprising in which soldiers, princes, peasants and artisans, Hindus and Muslims, fought together against British rule.

**Q3.** (4 marks) Explain the relations of the British with the princely states after 1858.
**Answer:** Annexations ended; the Doctrine of Lapse was given up and adoption allowed; treaties and the princes' dignity were respected; the princes became loyal allies, but under the paramountcy of the Crown, which could intervene in case of misrule.

**Q4.** (3 marks) Explain three social reforms of the British that alarmed orthodox Indians.
**Answer:** The abolition of sati (1829); the Widow Remarriage Act (1856); the Religious Disabilities Act (1850), which let converts inherit property.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Part I questions (2 marks each)

**Q1.** What was the Subsidiary Alliance? Who introduced it?
**Answer:** A system under which Indian rulers kept British troops at their own cost and accepted British supremacy.
Introduced by Lord Wellesley (1798).

**Q2.** What was the Doctrine of Lapse? Name one state annexed under it.
**Answer:** If a ruler died without a natural heir, the state lapsed to the British; adopted sons could not rule.
Jhansi (or Satara, Nagpur).

**Q3.** Imagine you are a ruler who signed the Subsidiary Alliance. State two conditions that affect you.
**Answer:** I must pay for British troops stationed in my state.
I cannot make treaties or wars without British consent (also: a Resident at my court).

**Q4.** Imagine you were an artisan in 1856. State two ways British policies affected you.
**Answer:** Loss of livelihood because of competition from machine-made goods.
Loss of patronage of the Indian courts (also heavy taxation).

**Q5.** What was the General Service Enlistment Act? Why did it anger sepoys?
**Answer:** An Act of 1856 requiring recruits to serve overseas if ordered.
Crossing the sea was against their religion and meant loss of caste.

**Q6.** State two socio-religious causes of the Revolt.
**Answer:** Interference in customs — abolition of sati, widow remarriage.
Activities of Christian missionaries (also the Religious Disabilities Act; racial discrimination).

**Q7.** What was the immediate cause of the Revolt?
**Answer:** The greased cartridges of the Enfield rifle, which had to be bitten off.
The grease was believed to contain cow and pig fat, offending Hindus and Muslims.

**Q8.** How did the failure of the Revolt affect the Mughals and the Peshwas?
**Answer:** Bahadur Shah was deported to Rangoon — the Mughal rule ended.
Nana Sahib fled to Nepal — the Peshwa line ended.

**Q9.** State two administrative changes made by the Act of 1858.
**Answer:** The Crown took over the government of India.
A Secretary of State with a Council of 15 was appointed (also: the Viceroy; Board of Control abolished).

**Q10.** State two promises of Queen Victoria's Proclamation.
**Answer:** No further annexations; respect for the princes.
Non-interference in religion; equal treatment in appointments.

**Q11.** State two changes in the army after the Revolt.
**Answer:** The number of European troops was increased.
Artillery was kept with European troops only.

**Q12.** Why did the British call it a "Sepoy Mutiny" while Indians call it a "War of Independence"?
**Answer:** The British said only soldiers rebelled.
Indians see it as a combined struggle of soldiers, princes, peasants and artisans against foreign rule.

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Mention three policies used by the British to expand their power.
**Answer:** Outright wars.
The Subsidiary Alliance.
The Doctrine of Lapse (also annexation on the pretext of misgovernance).

**Q14.** Mention three conditions of the Subsidiary Alliance.
**Answer:** The ruler accepted the British as the paramount power.
A British army was kept in the state at the ruler's cost.
A British Resident lived at the court; no treaties without British consent.

**Q15.** Explain three political causes of the Revolt.
**Answer:** The Doctrine of Lapse (Jhansi).
The annexation of Awadh.
Insults to the Mughal emperor and Nana Sahib (also absentee sovereignty).

**Q16.** Explain three economic causes of the Revolt.
**Answer:** Drain of wealth to Britain.
Decay of handicrafts due to British machine-made goods.
Ruin of peasants by heavy land revenue (also unemployment; Inam Commission).

**Q17.** Explain three military causes of the Revolt.
**Answer:** Low pay and bleak promotion prospects.
The General Service Enlistment Act (1856).
Withdrawal of bhatta and ban on caste marks.

**Q18.** Explain the immediate cause of the Revolt.
**Answer:** The Enfield rifle's cartridges were greased and had to be bitten off.
The grease was believed to contain cow and pig fat.
Hindu and Muslim sepoys refused; Mangal Pandey's attack and the Meerut rising followed.

**Q19.** Mention three administrative changes after the Revolt.
**Answer:** End of the Company's rule; the Crown took over.
Board of Control and Court of Directors abolished; Secretary of State with a Council of 15.
Governor-General called Viceroy; civil service by competition.

**Q20.** State three promises of Queen Victoria's Proclamation.
**Answer:** No further annexations; princes' rights respected.
Non-interference in religion and social customs.
Equal treatment and a general pardon (except murderers of British subjects).

**Q21.** Explain three changes in the army after 1858.
**Answer:** More European troops, at key positions.
Artillery and modern weapons only with Europeans.
Caste- and community-based regiments; newspapers kept from soldiers.

**Q22.** Explain the relations with the princely states after 1858.
**Answer:** Annexations stopped; adoption allowed.
Treaties honoured and princes treated as allies.
But the Crown's paramountcy was asserted.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Expansion: wars (Mysore, Marathas, Sind 1843, Punjab 1849); Subsidiary Alliance (Wellesley, 1798; Hyderabad first); Doctrine of Lapse (Dalhousie; Satara 1848, Jhansi 1853, Nagpur 1854); misgovernance (Awadh 1856).
- Political causes: expansion, lapse, Awadh, Mughal emperor insulted, Nana Sahib's pension, absentee sovereignty, unpopular administration.
- Socio-religious: sati/widow remarriage laws, missionaries, Religious Disabilities Act 1850, temple taxes, fear of innovations, racial discrimination.
- Economic: drain of wealth, ruin of handicrafts and patronage, peasants and indigo farmers, Inam Commission, unemployment.
- Military: ill-treatment, low pay, subedar ceiling, GSEA 1856, bhatta and free postage withdrawn, caste marks banned, Bengal Army discontent.
- Immediate: Enfield cartridges (cow and pig fat); Mangal Pandey (29 Mar); Meerut (10 May 1857).
- Consequences: Act of 1858 (Crown; Secretary of State + Council of 15; Viceroy; ICS competition); Proclamation (1 Nov 1858, Allahabad); princes as allies, adoption allowed; army — more Europeans, artillery with Europeans, divide and rule, newspapers barred; Mughals and Peshwas ended.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The Subsidiary Alliance was Dalhousie's | It was Wellesley's (1798). |
| Awadh was annexed by the Doctrine of Lapse | On the pretext of misgovernance. |
| The GSEA was passed by Dalhousie | Under Canning, in 1856. |
| The Revolt began at Barrackpore | Mangal Pandey's act was at Barrackpore; the Revolt began at Meerut. |
| Queen Victoria became Empress in 1858 | She took the title of Empress of India in 1877; in 1858 the Crown took over. |
| Mixing the groups of causes | Keep political, socio-religious, economic and military causes separate. |
