## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

- In the early years most educated Muslims, guided by **Sir Syed Ahmad Khan**, stayed away from the Indian National Congress.
- By 1906 a group of Muslim leaders decided that the Muslims needed a **separate political organisation** of their own. On **30 December 1906** the **All India Muslim League** was founded at **Dacca**.
- Ten years later, in **1916**, the League and the Congress came together in the **Lucknow Pact** — a high point of **Hindu–Muslim unity** in the national movement.

### Factors leading to the formation of the Muslim League

**1. The British policy of "divide and rule"**
- After 1857 the British first distrusted the Muslims, whom they blamed for the Revolt. Later, as the Congress grew strong, they began to **favour the Muslims** to **weaken the national movement**.
- They encouraged the idea that the interests of Hindus and Muslims were **different**. The **Partition of Bengal** (1905), which created a Muslim-majority province, is an example.

**2. Relative backwardness of the Muslims in education, trade and industry**
- The Muslim upper classes were **slow to accept English education**; as a result, Muslims lagged behind in **government jobs**, the professions, **trade and industry**.
- Communal-minded leaders argued that Muslims could improve their position only by **organising separately** and seeking British favour.

**3. The role of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan**
- He founded the **Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental (M.A.O.) College at Aligarh** in **1875** to spread modern education among Muslims — the **Aligarh Movement**.
- In his later years he urged Muslims **not to join the Congress**, calling it a body of the Hindu majority, and advised **loyalty to the British**.

**4. Wrong (mis-)interpretation of history**
- Some British and Indian writers presented medieval Indian history as a long conflict between **Hindu and Muslim rulers**, ignoring the cooperation between them. This created **mistrust** between the communities.

**5. Loss of sovereignty of the Mughal rulers**
- The Muslim upper classes remembered that **power had passed from the Mughals to the British**; after 1857 the Mughal dynasty ended. A section of them felt they had lost status and needed to protect their position.

**6. Rise of Assertive Nationalism**
- Some Assertive leaders used **Hindu religious symbols** — the Ganapati and Shivaji festivals, Kali worship — to rouse the people. Some Muslims felt left out of such a movement.

**7. Economic backwardness of the country**
- With few jobs and little industry, educated Hindus and Muslims **competed** for the limited government posts; communal leaders exploited this competition.

**8. The Hindi–Urdu controversy**
- In 1900 the government of the United Provinces gave **Hindi (in Devanagari script)** equal status with **Urdu** in courts and offices. Many Urdu-speaking Muslims saw this as a threat.

**9. The Partition of Bengal (1905)**
- Many Muslims of East Bengal, led by **Nawab Salimullah of Dacca**, **supported** the Partition because it gave them a Muslim-majority province; the Hindu-led agitation against it widened the gap.

**10. The Simla Deputation (1 October 1906)**
- A deputation of about **35 Muslim leaders**, led by the **Aga Khan**, met the Viceroy **Lord Minto** at **Simla**.
- They demanded **separate electorates** for Muslims and representation more than their numbers (**weightage**). Minto gave a **sympathetic reply** — encouraging them to form a party of their own.

**Example 1.** *(ICSE 2025, Q2(iii))* Mention any two factors that led to the formation of the Muslim League.
**Solution:** Any two: **loss of sovereignty** by the Mughal rulers; the British policy of **divide and rule**; **relative backwardness** of the Muslim community in education, trade and industry; the **role of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan**; **misinterpretation of history**; **rise of Assertive Nationalism**; **economic backwardness** of the country; the **Hindi–Urdu controversy**; the **Aligarh Movement**; the **deputation led by the Aga Khan to Lord Minto**; the **Partition of Bengal**; the establishment of the **M.A.O. College**.

### Formation of the Muslim League (1906)

- The **All India Muslim League** was founded on **30 December 1906** at **Dacca**, at a meeting held during the session of the **All India Muhammadan Educational Conference**.
- The proposal was moved by **Nawab Salimullah of Dacca**; the meeting was presided over by **Nawab Viqar-ul-Mulk**.
- The **Aga Khan** became its first (permanent) **President**.
- Its leaders at first were mostly **landlords, nawabs and members of the Muslim upper class**, loyal to the British.

### Objectives of the Muslim League

1. To **promote among the Muslims of India feelings of loyalty / support** for the British Government.
2. To **remove any misconceptions** that might arise about the **intentions of the Government** with regard to the Muslims.
3. To **protect and advance the political rights and interests of the Muslims** of India.
4. To **represent the needs and aspirations** of the Muslims to the Government in **mild and moderate** language.
5. To **prevent the rise of hostility** between the Muslims and other communities of India — without harming the above objectives.

**Example 2.** *(ICSE 2023, Q2(v))* State any two objectives of the Muslim League.
**Solution:** Any two: to **promote among Muslims of India support for the British Government**; to **remove any misconceptions** regarding the intention of the Government with regard to the Muslims; to **protect and advance the political rights and interests of the Muslims**; to **represent their needs and aspirations** to the Government in a mild and moderate language; to **prevent the rise of hostility** between the Muslims of India and other communities.

### The Morley–Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act, 1909)

- Named after **John Morley** (Secretary of State for India) and **Lord Minto** (Viceroy).
- The Legislative Councils were **enlarged**, and more members were **elected** (indirectly).
- **Separate electorates** were introduced for the **Muslims** — Muslim voters alone would elect Muslim representatives.
- Effect: the British policy of **divide and rule** was written into the constitution; the League gained recognition as the voice of Muslims. Later, Gandhi and the nationalists regarded separate electorates as a root of **communal politics**.

### The League changes its outlook (1911–1913)

Between 1911 and 1913 the League moved closer to the Congress:
1. **Annulment of the Partition of Bengal (1911)** — the British cancelled the partition the League had supported; Muslim leaders felt the British could not be relied upon.
2. **The British attitude towards Turkey** — Britain did not help **Turkey** (whose Sultan was the **Caliph**, the religious head of Sunni Muslims) in its wars with **Italy (1911)** and in the **Balkan Wars (1912–13)**. Indian Muslims were angered.
3. **The Kanpur Mosque incident (1913)** — part of a mosque was demolished for a road, and protesters were fired upon.
4. **Rise of young nationalist Muslims** — leaders such as **Maulana Mohammad Ali** and **Shaukat Ali**, **Maulana Abul Kalam Azad** (*Al-Hilal*), **Hakim Ajmal Khan** and **Mazharul Haque** wanted to work with the Congress; **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** joined the League in **1913** while remaining in the Congress.
5. In **1913** the League adopted the goal of **self-government suitable to India** — the same goal as the Congress.

### The Lucknow Pact (1916)

**What it was**
- An agreement between the **Indian National Congress** and the **All India Muslim League**, made at their annual sessions held together at **Lucknow** in **December 1916**.
- Its chief architects were **Bal Gangadhar Tilak** (Congress) and **Muhammad Ali Jinnah** (League), helped by Annie Besant. The Congress session was presided over by **Ambika Charan Mazumdar**; the League session by **Jinnah**.
- At the same Lucknow session the **Moderates and the Assertives were reunited** in the Congress.

**Main provisions**
1. Both parties would demand **self-government** for India (a dominion within the Empire) **jointly**.
2. The Congress **accepted separate electorates** for the Muslims.
3. Muslims were given a **fixed proportion of seats** — **one-third** of the elected Indian members in the central legislature — and **weightage** in provinces where they were a minority.
4. **No Bill** affecting a community would be passed if **three-fourths** of that community's members opposed it.
5. **Four-fifths** of the members of the legislative councils should be **elected**.
6. **Half the members** of the Executive Councils should be **Indians**.
7. The **judiciary** should be **separated** from the executive.

**Signatories:** the **Indian National Congress** and the **All India Muslim League**.

**Example 3.** *(ICSE 2023, Q1(xii))* The Lucknow Pact was signed between __________. (a) The British and the Congress (b) The Congress and the Muslim League (c) The Early and the Assertive Nationalists (d) The Muslim League and the British
**Solution:** **(b) The Congress and the Muslim League.**

**Impact / significance of the Lucknow Pact**

*Positive*
1. **Hindu–Muslim unity** — the two communities and their parties came together on one platform for the first time.
2. **Joint demands** — the Congress and the League put forward **common constitutional demands**, which increased pressure on the British.
3. **Unity within the Congress** — the Moderates and the Assertives reunited after the Surat Split.
4. **Influence on British policy** — it contributed to the **Montagu Declaration (August 1917)**, which promised the gradual development of **responsible government** in India.
5. It prepared the ground for the joint **Khilafat and Non-Cooperation Movements** (1920–22).

*Negative*
1. By accepting **separate electorates**, the Congress **officially recognised communal representation** — politics continued to be seen in terms of religious communities.
2. The unity was between **leaders**, not the masses; it did not last, and communal differences grew in the 1920s, leading eventually to the demand for **Pakistan** (Chapter 14).

### The Lucknow Pact provisions explained

| Provision | What it meant in practice |
|---|---|
| Joint demand for self-government | The Congress and the League spoke to the British with **one voice**. |
| Separate electorates accepted by the Congress | Muslim voters would elect Muslim members — the Congress gave up its earlier opposition. |
| One-third of the elected Indian seats at the centre for Muslims | Muslims were assured a **fixed share** in the Imperial Legislative Council. |
| Weightage in minority provinces | Where Muslims were few (e.g. Bombay, Madras), they got **more seats** than their numbers. |
| Three-fourths rule | A Bill affecting a community could be stopped if **three-fourths of its members** opposed it — a **minority safeguard**. |
| Four-fifths elected councils | Only one-fifth of members would be nominated — more **democratic** councils. |
| Half the Executive Council Indians | Indians would share in **governing**, not only in debating. |
| Separation of judiciary from executive | Fairer **justice**. |

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Factors
Think of "push" factors (British divide and rule, Partition, Simla Deputation) and "inside" factors (backwardness, Sir Syed, loss of Mughal power, fear of Hindu majority). Any two or three make a full answer.

### Section 2 — Objectives
The objectives were moderate and loyalist — loyalty to the British, protection of Muslim interests, moderate language, no hostility with others. Do not write "independence" or "Pakistan" as an objective of 1906.

### Section 3 — From rivalry to the Pact
Between 1911 and 1913 events abroad and at home changed the League's mood; the Lucknow Pact was the result.

### Section 4 — Impact
Give both sides: unity and joint pressure, but also acceptance of separate electorates.

## 6. Person Profiles

### 6.1 Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898)
An educationist and reformer who founded the M.A.O. College at Aligarh (1875) and led the Aligarh Movement for modern education among Muslims; in politics he advised Muslims to remain loyal to the British and to keep away from the Congress.

### 6.2 The Aga Khan III (1877–1957)
Leader of the Ismaili Muslims, who led the Simla Deputation (1906) and became the first President of the Muslim League.

### 6.3 Nawab Salimullah of Dacca (1871–1915)
A Bengali Muslim leader who supported the Partition of Bengal and proposed the formation of the League at Dacca in 1906.

### 6.4 Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876–1948)
A Bombay lawyer who was a member of both the Congress and the League, presided over the League session at Lucknow and was a chief architect of the Lucknow Pact; later he led the demand for Pakistan.

### 6.5 Lord Minto (Viceroy 1905–1910)
The Viceroy who received the Simla Deputation and, with John Morley, introduced the reforms of 1909 with separate electorates.

### 6.6 Maulana Abul Kalam Azad (1888–1958)
A scholar and journalist who founded the Urdu weekly *Al-Hilal* (1912) to spread nationalist ideas among Muslims; he opposed separate Muslim politics, later became President of the Congress and was free India's first Education Minister.

### 6.7 Maulana Mohammad Ali (1878–1931)
A journalist (*Comrade*, *Hamdard*) who, with his brother Shaukat Ali, voiced Muslim anger over Britain's treatment of Turkey, worked for Hindu–Muslim cooperation and later led the Khilafat Movement.

### 6.8 Annie Besant (1847–1933)
The Theosophist leader who founded a Home Rule League in 1916 and helped bring the Congress groups and the League together at Lucknow.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas
- **Divide and rule** — a ruling power can weaken a movement by encouraging divisions.
- **Unity is possible** — the Lucknow Pact showed that the two communities could agree on common goals.
- **Choices have long effects** — separate electorates, accepted for unity in 1916, deepened communal politics later.

### 7.2 Real-life connections
- **Joint electorates today:** the Constitution of India has a single (joint) electorate for all citizens; reserved seats for SCs and STs are elected by all voters.
- **Coalitions:** political parties still sign agreements on shared programmes — as the Congress and the League did in 1916.

**Answer:** In October 1906 about 35 Muslim leaders led by the Aga Khan met Lord Minto at Simla and demanded separate electorates and weightage; Minto's sympathetic reply encouraged them to form their own party, and the League was founded at Dacca in December 1906.

**Q2.** (4 marks) Mention four provisions of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** A joint demand for self-government; Congress acceptance of separate electorates for Muslims; one-third of the elected Indian seats in the central legislature for Muslims; no Bill affecting a community to pass if three-fourths of its members opposed it (also: four-fifths of council members elected; half the Executive Council Indians).

**Q3.** (3 marks) Why did the Muslim League move closer to the Congress between 1911 and 1913?
**Answer:** The annulment of the Partition of Bengal (1911) disappointed it; Britain's attitude towards Turkey in the Italian and Balkan wars angered Muslims; younger nationalist leaders such as Mohammad Ali and Jinnah wanted cooperation with the Congress.

**Q4.** (2 marks) Give one positive and one negative effect of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** Positive — Hindu–Muslim unity and joint demands; negative — acceptance of separate electorates legitimised communal representation.

**Q5.** (3 marks) Explain the role of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan as a factor in the formation of the League.
**Answer:** He spread modern education among Muslims through the Aligarh Movement and the M.A.O. College (1875); he advised Muslims to stay away from the Congress, calling it a Hindu body; and he urged loyalty to the British — encouraging separate Muslim politics.

**Q6.** (2 marks) Why did Minto's reply to the Simla Deputation matter?
**Answer:** His sympathetic reply assured Muslim leaders that their demand for separate electorates would be considered; it encouraged them to form a separate party, and the demand was granted in 1909.

**Q7.** (4 marks) Explain four ways in which the British encouraged separate Muslim politics between 1905 and 1909.
**Answer:** The Partition of Bengal created a Muslim-majority province; Lord Minto welcomed the Simla Deputation; the British welcomed the League as a counterweight to the Congress; the Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) granted separate electorates.

**Q8.** (3 marks) Name three young Muslim leaders who favoured cooperation with the Congress before 1916, with one contribution of each.
**Answer:** Muhammad Ali Jinnah — architect of the Lucknow Pact; Maulana Abul Kalam Azad — edited *Al-Hilal*, spreading nationalism; Maulana Mohammad Ali — led Muslim opinion on Turkey and later the Khilafat Movement.

**Q9.** (2 marks) Why is the Lucknow Pact sometimes criticised?
**Answer:** By accepting separate electorates, the Congress officially recognised communal representation; the unity achieved was among leaders and did not last.

**Q8.** Give the role of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan as a factor in the formation of the League.
**Answer:** He founded the M.A.O. College and led the Aligarh Movement.
He advised Muslims to keep away from the Congress and stay loyal to the British.

**Q9.** Why did the League change its attitude towards the British after 1911?
**Answer:** The Partition of Bengal was annulled.
Britain did not help Turkey, whose Sultan was the Caliph.

**Q10.** Name the two chief architects of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** Bal Gangadhar Tilak.
Muhammad Ali Jinnah.

**Q11.** Give two provisions of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** A joint demand for self-government.
Congress acceptance of separate electorates for Muslims.

**Q12.** Arrange in order: Lucknow Pact, Surat Split, formation of the Muslim League.
**Answer:** Muslim League (1906), Surat Split (1907).
Lucknow Pact (1916).

### Part II parts (3 marks each)

**Q13.** Explain three factors that led to the formation of the Muslim League.
**Answer:** British divide and rule — favouring Muslims to weaken the Congress.
Backwardness of Muslims in education and jobs.
Sir Syed Ahmad Khan's advice; the Simla Deputation; the Partition of Bengal.

**Q14.** State three objectives of the Muslim League.
**Answer:** Loyalty to the British Government.
Protection of Muslim political rights and interests.
Representation of Muslim needs in moderate language; no hostility with others.

**Q15.** Describe the formation of the Muslim League.
**Answer:** Simla Deputation to Lord Minto (October 1906).
Meeting at Dacca on 30 December 1906; proposal by Nawab Salimullah.
The Aga Khan became President.

**Q16.** State three provisions of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** Joint demand for self-government.
Separate electorates for Muslims; one-third of elected Indian seats at the centre.
Four-fifths of council members elected; half the Executive Council Indians.

**Q17.** Explain three effects of the Lucknow Pact.
**Answer:** Hindu–Muslim unity.
Joint pressure leading to the Montagu Declaration (1917).
Acceptance of separate electorates — communal representation legitimised.

**Q18.** Why did the League move closer to the Congress between 1911 and 1913?
**Answer:** Annulment of the Partition of Bengal.
British attitude towards Turkey and the Caliph.
Rise of young nationalist Muslim leaders (Mohammad Ali, Azad, Jinnah).

**Q19.** What were the Morley–Minto Reforms? Give two features.
**Answer:** The Indian Councils Act of 1909.
Enlarged councils with more elected members.
Separate electorates for Muslims.

**Q20.** Explain the British policy of divide and rule as a factor in the formation of the League.
**Answer:** The British wanted to weaken the Congress.
They encouraged the idea that Hindu and Muslim interests were different.
The Partition of Bengal and the welcome to the Simla Deputation show this.

**Q21.** Give three factors other than British policy that led to the formation of the League.
**Answer:** Relative backwardness of Muslims.
Loss of Mughal sovereignty; misinterpretation of history.
Rise of Assertive Nationalism; Hindi–Urdu controversy.

**Q22.** Explain the significance of the year 1916 in the national movement.
**Answer:** The Lucknow Pact between the Congress and the League.
The Moderates and the Assertives reunited.
The Home Rule Leagues were founded.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Two voters, 1910)** Ahmad and Ramesh live in the same town in 1910. Explain how separate electorates affect the way they vote.
**Model answer:** Ahmad votes only for Muslim candidates in a Muslim constituency; Ramesh votes in the general constituency. Their votes are kept apart, so candidates need not appeal to both communities.

**Q2. (A joint statement)** Write two sentences the Congress and the League might have issued together in December 1916.
**Model answer:** "The Indian National Congress and the All India Muslim League jointly demand self-government for India. We stand united, and we call upon the British Government to grant it without delay."

**Q3. (Factor or objective?)** Classify: (a) the Simla Deputation; (b) protection of Muslim rights; (c) divide and rule; (d) loyalty to the British.
**Model answer:** Factors — (a) and (c); objectives — (b) and (d).

**Q4. (A history lesson)** Why is it harmful to teach history only as a conflict between communities?
**Model answer:** It hides the long periods of cooperation, creates mistrust between neighbours and was used to justify divide and rule.

**Q5. (Unity today)** Suggest two ways a school can build unity among students of different communities.
**Model answer:** Celebrate one another's festivals together, and form mixed teams for projects and sport.

**Q6. (A news reporter at Lucknow)** You report on the Lucknow sessions of December 1916. Write two facts you would put in the first paragraph.
**Model answer:** The Congress and the Muslim League have agreed on a joint scheme of reforms demanding self-government; and the Moderates and the Assertive Nationalists have reunited in the Congress after nine years.

**Q7. (Then and now)** How is the electoral system of independent India different from separate electorates?
**Model answer:** India has a single (joint) electorate — all voters in a constituency vote together, whatever their religion — so candidates must seek the support of every community.

## 13. Assertion–Reason Questions

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) A is true, but R is false.
- (b) A is false, but R is true.
- (c) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (d) Both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A.

**Q1.**
**A:** The Simla Deputation demanded separate electorates.
**R:** It was led by the Aga Khan.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** Both true; R does not explain the demand.

**Q2.**
**A:** The Muslim League was anti-British in 1906.
**R:** One of its objectives was to promote loyalty to the British Government.
**Answer:** (b)
**Explanation:** It was loyalist at first.

**Q3.**
**A:** The Lucknow Pact was signed between the Congress and the League.
**R:** Both sought self-government for India.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** A common goal brought them together.

**Q4.**
**A:** The annulment of the Partition of Bengal disappointed the League.
**R:** The League had supported the creation of a Muslim-majority province.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** The British cancelled it in 1911.

**Q5.**
**A:** The Morley–Minto Reforms introduced separate electorates.
**R:** The British wanted to keep Hindus and Muslims apart.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** Divide and rule.

**Q6.**
**A:** The Lucknow Pact created lasting Hindu–Muslim unity.
**R:** The Congress accepted separate electorates.
**Answer:** (b)
**Explanation:** The unity did not last; R is true.

**Q7.**
**A:** Sir Syed Ahmad Khan founded the M.A.O. College.
**R:** The Muslim League was founded at Dacca.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** Both true but unrelated.

**Q8.**
**A:** The Muslim League was formed in 1906.
**R:** The Congress split at Surat in 1907.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** The split came later and did not cause the League's formation.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

### Case 1 — Simla, October 1906

> About thirty-five Muslim leaders met the Viceroy at Simla. They asked that Muslims should elect their own representatives and should have more seats than their numbers alone would give. The Viceroy's reply was sympathetic. Within three months a new party was formed.

**(i)** Who led the deputation?
**Answer:** The Aga Khan.

**(ii)** Name the Viceroy.
**Answer:** Lord Minto.

**(iii)** What is the demand for "their own representatives" called?
**Answer:** Separate electorates.

**(iv)** Name the party formed and the place.
**Answer:** The All India Muslim League, at Dacca.

### Case 2 — Lucknow, December 1916

> Two political bodies held their annual sessions in the same city and agreed on a common scheme of reforms. One body accepted the other's demand for a separate electorate. The same city also saw the reunion of two groups that had parted nine years earlier.

**(i)** Name the two bodies.
**Answer:** The Indian National Congress and the All India Muslim League.

**(ii)** Name the agreement.
**Answer:** The Lucknow Pact.

**(iii)** Which two groups reunited?
**Answer:** The Moderates and the Assertive Nationalists.

**(iv)** Name one leader of each body who shaped the agreement.
**Answer:** Tilak (Congress) and Jinnah (League).

### Case 3 — Why a new party?

**(i)** Name the policy in point 1.
**Answer:** Divide and rule.

**(ii)** What factor is point 2?
**Answer:** Relative backwardness of Muslims in education.

**(iii)** Name the reformer in point 3.
**Answer:** Sir Syed Ahmad Khan.

**(iv)** Name the event in point 4.
**Answer:** The Partition of Bengal.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- League: 30 Dec 1906, Dacca; Nawab Salimullah; Aga Khan first President; upper-class, loyalist.
- Factors: divide and rule; backwardness in education, trade, industry; Sir Syed (Aligarh, M.A.O. College 1875); misinterpretation of history; loss of Mughal sovereignty; Assertive Nationalism's Hindu symbols; economic backwardness; Hindi–Urdu controversy (1900); Partition of Bengal; Simla Deputation (Aga Khan → Minto, 1 Oct 1906).
- Objectives: loyalty to British; remove misconceptions; protect Muslim rights; moderate representation; no hostility with others.
- Morley–Minto 1909: separate electorates.
- 1911–13: annulment; Turkey/Caliph; Kanpur mosque; Ali brothers, Azad, Jinnah; self-government goal 1913.
- Lucknow Pact 1916: Congress + League; Tilak + Jinnah; joint self-government demand; separate electorates; one-third seats; three-fourths rule; four-fifths elected; half Executive Council Indians; Congress reunited.
- Impact: unity; joint pressure; Montagu Declaration 1917; Khilafat–NCM; but communal representation accepted; unity of leaders only.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The League was founded at Lucknow | At Dacca; the Pact was at Lucknow. |
| The League aimed at independence in 1906 | It was loyal to the British then. |
| Writing factors when objectives are asked | Factors = why it was formed; objectives = what it aimed to do. |
| The Lucknow Pact was between the Congress and the British | Between the Congress and the League. |
| Separate electorates came in 1919 | They came in 1909 (Morley–Minto). |
| The Surat Split caused the League's formation | The League (1906) came before the split (1907). |
