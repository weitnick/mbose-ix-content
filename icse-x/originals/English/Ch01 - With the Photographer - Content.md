## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*With the Photographer* is a short, funny story by the Canadian humorist **Stephen Leacock**. A man goes to a photographer's studio simply to have his **photograph taken**. He expects a picture that looks like him. Instead, the photographer treats his face as a **problem to be corrected**: he twists the man's head, finds fault with his mouth, ears and eyes, and finally produces a "photograph" in which almost every feature has been **retouched** or replaced. The narrator cannot even recognise himself — "**Is it me?**" — and storms out in a fit of tearful rage.

Under the laughter lies a serious point. The story mocks the **vanity** and false **perfection** of commercial photography, and defends a person's right to be seen **as they really are**. Every face, says the narrator, is "my face, the only one I have", and he has learnt to love it. A century later, in a world of beauty filters and photo-editing apps, the story is more relevant than ever.

### About the author

**Stephen Butler Leacock** (1869–1944) was born in England and moved with his family to **Canada** as a child. He became a professor of **economics and political science** at **McGill University** in Montreal, but he is best remembered as one of the most popular **humorists** writing in English in the early twentieth century. His books include *Literary Lapses* (1910), *Nonsense Novels* (1911) and *Sunshine Sketches of a Little Town* (1912). *With the Photographer* comes from his collection *Behind the Beyond* (1913), in a group of pieces about "familiar incidents" of everyday life. Canada's annual prize for humorous writing, the **Stephen Leacock Memorial Medal for Humour**, is named after him.

Leacock's typical method is to take an ordinary situation — opening a bank account, visiting a dentist, having a photograph taken — and tell it through a **timid, slightly ridiculous narrator** who exaggerates his own feelings, so that a small embarrassment becomes a comic disaster.

### The story, retold in our own words

#### 1. In the waiting room

The narrator walks into a photographer's studio and says he wants his photograph taken. The photographer looks at him "without enthusiasm". He is a drooping man in a grey suit, with "the dim eye of a natural scientist" — as if photography were a solemn scientific study and the customer merely a specimen. He tells the narrator, "Sit down. Wait."

The narrator **waits an hour**. To pass the time he **reads magazines** — and they are absurdly old: a ladies' magazine from 1912, a girls' magazine from 1902 and an infants' journal from 1888. *(2025 specimen Q1(xii).)* Sitting there, he begins to feel guilty, as if he had done something unforgivable in disturbing this great man's "scientific pursuits" with a face like his.

#### 2. Into the studio

After an hour the photographer opens the inner door and says, severely, "Come in." The narrator sits on a stool in a beam of sunlight that comes through a sheet of **factory cotton** hung under a **frosted skylight** (studios used daylight, softened through cloth).

The photographer rolls a big camera — "a machine" — into the middle of the room and **crawls into it from behind**. He looks once at the narrator and rushes out again, tearing at the cotton sheet and the window panes with a hooked stick, "apparently frantic for light and air". He crawls back in and pulls a **black cloth** over himself. He is so quiet under it that the narrator decides he must be **praying**, and keeps still.

#### 3. "The face is quite wrong"

When the photographer comes out, he looks grave and shakes his head. "**The face is quite wrong**," he says. The narrator answers meekly, "**I know; I have always known it.**" This is the first big laugh of the story: instead of being offended, the narrator humbly agrees that his face is a mistake.

The photographer sighs that the face would be better "**three-quarters full**" — a photographer's term for turning the face partly sideways, so that three-quarters of it is visible. The narrator misunderstands it to mean "fuller" or "rounder" and eagerly agrees, glad that the man has "a human side". He starts a little speech about how hard, narrow faces become "wide, large, almost boundless" when they are three-quarters full — but the photographer has stopped listening.

The photographer takes the narrator's head in his hands and twists it sideways. The narrator thinks he means to **kiss** him, and closes his eyes. But the photographer only twists the face as far as it will go and stands looking at it.

#### 4. A list of instructions

The photographer is still not satisfied. "**I don't like the head**," he says. Then comes a rapid stream of orders, each more ridiculous than the last:

- "Open the mouth a little" — then at once, "Close it."
- "**The ears are bad; droop them a little more.**"
- "Roll the eyes in under the lids."
- Put the hands on the knees, turn the face a little upward, **expand the lungs**, **hump the neck**, **contract the waist**, and **twist the hip up towards the elbow**.

And still the face is "a trifle too full". The orders are impossible for any human body to obey — that is the joke.

#### 5. The narrator protests — and the photograph is taken

At last the narrator swings round on the stool. "**Stop**," he says, with emotion but, he believes, with dignity. He makes a passionate little speech:

> "This face is my face. It is not yours, it is mine. I've lived with it for forty years and I know its faults. … But it's my face, the only one I have …"

His voice breaks, but he goes on: such as it is, he has **learnt to love it**. This is his mouth, not the photographer's; these are his ears, and if the photographer's machine is too narrow — he starts to rise from his seat.

**Snick!** The photographer has pulled a string. The photograph is taken — at the very moment of the outburst. The narrator imagines the machine still "**staggering from the shock**". The photographer, pursing his lips in a pleased smile, says he caught the features "**just in a moment of animation**". The narrator replies bitingly, "So! features, eh? You didn't think I could animate them, I suppose?" He asks to see the picture; the photographer says he must first **develop the negative** — the narrator should come back on **Saturday** to see a **proof**.

#### 6. Saturday: the proof

On Saturday the photographer beckons him in. He seems quieter and graver, and there is "a certain **pride** in his manner". He unfolds the **proof** (a trial print) of a large photograph and they both look at it in silence.

"**Is it me?**" the narrator asks. "**Yes,**" the photographer says quietly, "**it is you.**" *(2025 specimen Q4; 2027 specimen Q1(xii).)*

The narrator examines it feature by feature, and the photographer explains, with professional satisfaction, what he has done:

| Feature | What the photographer has done |
|---|---|
| **eyes** | "I've **retouched** them. They come out splendidly, don't they?" |
| **eyebrows** | "The eyebrows are **removed**." A new process, the "**Delphide**", puts in new ones. |
| **hair and forehead** | The Delphide has been used to carry the hair away from the brow; he dislikes "the hair low on the skull" and likes to make "a new brow line". |
| **mouth** | "It's adjusted a little … yours is too low. I found I couldn't use it." |
| **ears** | They are "a good likeness" — the only feature that looks like the narrator's. But the photographer can "fix that": a process called the "**Sulphide**" removes the ears **entirely**. |

#### 7. The outburst

This is too much. The narrator draws himself up, "animating my features to their full extent", and speaks with "a withering scorn":

He came for a photograph that would look like him — something that would show his face "**as Heaven gave it to me**, humble though the gift may have been". He wanted something his **friends might keep after his death**, to reconcile them to his loss. That, it seems, is "no longer done". So let the photographer go on with his "**brutal work**": dip the negative in "sulphide, bromide, oxide, **cowhide**" — anything he likes; remove the eyes, correct the mouth, adjust the face, restore the lips, reanimate the necktie, reconstruct the waistcoat; coat it with gloss, shade it, emboss it, **gild** it, till even the photographer admits it is finished. And then — "**keep it for yourself and your friends**. They may value it. To me it is but a **worthless bauble**."

The narrator "broke into tears and left". The story ends there: the comic hero, defeated, keeps his self-respect only by walking out.

### Conclusion: what the story teaches

- A photograph — and, by extension, any portrait — should show a person **as they really are**, not an "improved" version that no one recognises.
- Our faces, with all their faults, are part of our **identity**; we have a right to accept and love them.
- "Experts" can become so absorbed in their own idea of perfection that they forget the **human being** in front of them.
- Leacock makes these points through **exaggeration, irony and self-mockery**, so that we laugh first and think afterwards.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The waiting room

- **What happens:** the narrator asks for a photograph; the photographer looks at him without enthusiasm and makes him wait an hour; he reads very old magazines.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**with the dim eye of a natural scientist**" — the photographer is pictured as a dreamy scientist absorbed in research; the customer is a mere specimen. Comic exaggeration of his self-importance.
  - "**Sit down. Wait.**" — curt, rude orders; no welcome, no courtesy.
  - **The old magazines** (1912, 1902, 1888) — a joke about waiting rooms everywhere, full of out-of-date reading. *(2025 specimen Q1(xii): reading magazines.)*
  - "**I had done an unwarrantable thing in breaking in on the privacy of this man's scientific pursuits with a face like mine**" — the narrator, absurdly humble, feels guilty for having a face at all. *Unwarrantable* = unjustified.
- **Humour:** role reversal — the paying customer feels like an intruder; the shopkeeper acts like a great scientist.

### Section 2 — The studio and the camera

- **What happens:** the narrator sits in softened sunlight; the photographer crawls in and out of his camera, adjusts the light, and hides under a black cloth.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**a beam of sunlight filtered through a sheet of factory cotton hung against a frosted skylight**" — early studios used diffused daylight for portraits.
  - "**crawled into it from behind**" — exaggeration: a large box camera is described as if it were a cave or tent.
  - "**apparently frantic for light and air**" — as if one look at the narrator's face made the photographer gasp for breath.
  - "**I knew that he was praying and I kept still**" — mock-solemnity; the black focusing cloth is treated as a religious ritual.

### Section 3 — "The face is quite wrong"

- **What happens:** the photographer condemns the face; the narrator agrees; the "three-quarters full" misunderstanding; the head-twisting.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The face is quite wrong.**" — "I know; I have always known it." — **understatement** and self-mockery; the narrator's meek acceptance is the joke.
  - "**three-quarters full**" — a photographic pose (face turned three-quarters to the camera); the narrator thinks it means "plumper". A **comic misunderstanding**.
  - "**I thought he meant to kiss me, and I closed my eyes**" — absurd misreading of the photographer's handling of his head.

### Section 4 — The impossible instructions

- **What happens:** the photographer issues a rapid series of orders about mouth, ears, eyes, hands, lungs, neck, waist and hip.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Open the mouth a little. … Close it.**" — contradictory orders in quick succession.
  - "**The ears are bad; droop them a little more.**" — impossible: nobody can droop their ears.
  - "**hump the neck … contract the waist … twist the hip up towards the elbow**" — the list builds to physical absurdity (climax of exaggeration).
- **Humour:** the photographer treats the human body like clay to be moulded.

### Section 5 — "This face is my face" — Snick!

- **What happens:** the narrator protests; the photograph is taken at that moment; it must be developed.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**This face is my face. It is not yours, it is mine.**" — the heart of the story: the right to one's own identity.
  - "**I've lived with it for forty years and I know its faults**" — touching and funny; he admits its faults but claims it as his own.
  - "**such as it is, I've learned to love it**" — self-acceptance.
  - "**Snick!**" — onomatopoeia for the camera's shutter.
  - "**I could see the machine still staggering from the shock**" — personification and exaggeration; even the camera is shaken.
  - "**I caught the features just in a moment of animation**" — the photographer is pleased to have caught the face alive; the narrator is offended by "features", as if his face had none worth having.
  - "**proof**" — a trial print shown to the customer before the final copy.

### Section 6 — Saturday: the proof

- **What happens:** the photographer, proud of his work, shows the proof; the narrator cannot recognise himself; the photographer explains each change.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**there was a certain pride in his manner**" — he believes he has *improved* the face. *(2025 specimen Q4(i))*
  - "**Is it me?**" / "**Yes, it is you.**" — the narrator cannot recognise his own face; the photographer's calm reply is comic and sinister. *(2027 specimen Q1(xii))*
  - "**the Delphide**" and "**the Sulphide**" — invented, scientific-sounding names (echoing real photographic chemicals) for processes that add eyebrows or remove ears.
  - "**The ears, though, strike me as a good likeness**" — the ears are the **only original feature** left. *(2025 specimen Q4(iv))*
  - "**a process now — the Sulphide — for removing the ears entirely**" — the climax of absurdity.

### Section 7 — The outburst

- **What happens:** the narrator delivers a scornful speech and leaves in tears.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**as Heaven gave it to me, humble though the gift may have been**" — the natural face is a God-given gift, to be shown honestly.
  - "**something that my friends might keep after my death, to reconcile them to my loss**" — the true purpose of a portrait: to remember a real person.
  - "**sulphide, bromide, oxide, cowhide**" — an absurd list that slides from chemicals into "cowhide" (leather) — **bathos** and a rhyme joke.
  - "**reanimate the necktie and reconstruct the waistcoat**" — mock-scientific language applied to clothes.
  - "**coat it with an inch of gloss, shade it, emboss it, gild it**" — a list of decorations; the "photograph" has become an ornament.
  - "**To me it is but a worthless bauble.**" — a showy but valueless trinket; the final rejection.
  - "**I broke into tears and left.**" — comic anticlimax after his grand speech.
- **Exam focus:** 2025 specimen Q4(v) — why he is angry, and whether the outburst is justified.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- In Leacock's time, studio portraits were taken with large wooden cameras on stands, using glass-plate negatives. Long exposures meant sitters had to keep very still; head-rests were sometimes used.
- **Retouching** negatives with pencils and etching knives to smooth skin and remove blemishes was a common studio practice from the late nineteenth century.
- Today, **beauty filters** and editing apps can change a face in seconds — a modern version of the "Delphide" and "Sulphide". Many people now worry, as the narrator does, about pictures that do not show real faces.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Identity and self-acceptance:** a face, with all its faults, belongs to its owner and is part of who they are.
- **Truth versus artificial beauty:** a "perfect" picture that does not resemble the sitter is worthless.
- **The arrogance of experts:** a professional so absorbed in technique forgets the customer's wishes.
- **The purpose of portraits:** to keep a true memory of a person for friends and family.
- **Dignity of the ordinary person:** the timid narrator finally stands up for himself.

### 7.1 Leacock's techniques of humour

| Technique | Example |
|---|---|
| exaggeration (hyperbole) | waits an hour; photographer "crawled into" the camera; "staggering from the shock" |
| understatement / self-mockery | "I know; I have always known it" |
| comic misunderstanding | "three-quarters full" taken to mean plumper; thinks he will be kissed |
| impossible instructions | "droop them a little more"; "twist the hip up towards the elbow" |
| mock-scientific language | "natural scientist", "scientific pursuits", "superficies", "Delphide", "Sulphide" |
| bathos / anticlimax | "sulphide, bromide, oxide, cowhide"; grand speech ending in tears |
| personification | the camera "staggering from the shock" |
| onomatopoeia | "Snick!" |
| irony | "Is it me?" — "Yes, it is you", when it plainly is not |

### 7.2 Irony in the story

1. The customer pays for a service but is treated like an intruder.
2. The photograph is taken at the very moment the narrator protests — and the photographer calls it "a moment of animation".
3. The photographer insists the picture "is you", although every feature has been changed.
4. The ears — the only true likeness — are the next thing to be removed.
5. The narrator wanted a picture for his friends; he tells the photographer to keep it for *his* friends.

### 7.3 Significance of the title

"With the Photographer" names the whole experience — the narrator's time **in the company of** the photographer. It sounds like an ordinary, everyday visit, which makes the absurd events funnier. The title also puts the photographer at the centre: the story is about what happens when an ordinary person falls into the hands of a self-important "expert".

**2025 specimen Q4.**

**(i) Where was the narrator? Why had he gone there? Why do you think that there was a certain pride in the photographer's manner?** [3]
The narrator was in the **photographer's studio**. He had gone there **to have his photograph taken**. There was pride in the photographer's manner because he was **proud of his work** — he believed he had **improved the narrator's face** by retouching and altering it.

**(ii) What does the word 'proof' mean in this context? Why did the narrator ask, "Is it me?"** [3]
A **proof** is a **trial print** of a photograph, shown to the customer **before the final copy** is made. The narrator asked "Is it me?" because he **did not recognise himself** — the photographer had changed his features so much that the picture no longer looked like him.

**(iii) Which of the narrator's facial features had the photographer altered?** [3]
He had **retouched the eyes**, **removed the eyebrows** (to put in new ones by the "Delphide" process), **carried the hair back from the forehead** to make a new brow line, and **adjusted the mouth**, which he found "too low".

**(iv) What was the only part of the narrator's face that seemed original in the photograph? How did the photographer plan to 'fix' this?** [3]
The **ears** were the only original feature — "a good likeness". The photographer planned to fix this with a new process, the "**Sulphide**", which **removes the ears entirely**.

**(v) At the end of the story the narrator flies into a rage. What makes him angry? How would you justify the narrator's angry outburst?** [4]
**What makes him angry:** the photograph has **no original feature** left; he wanted a picture that **looked like him**, showing his face "as Heaven gave it", for his friends to keep after his death, but he gets an artificial picture he cannot recognise, and even his ears are to be removed. **Justification:** his anger is justified because he has been **humiliated** — kept waiting, ordered about, his face called "quite wrong" — and his **sense of self** has been ignored; the photographer has replaced his real identity with an image of his own making. A portrait that does not resemble the sitter is, as he says, a "worthless bauble".

**2026 Improvement Q1(vii).** **(b) An unnamed man**, of about forty, who tells the story in the first person. (The "natural scientist" is only how he describes the photographer's dim eye.)

**2026 Improvement Q4.**

**(i) The photographer's attitude at first; how the narrator describes him.** [3]
He is **indifferent and unwelcoming**: he looks at the narrator "**without enthusiasm**" and curtly tells him to sit and wait (for an hour). The narrator describes him as **a drooping man in a grey suit**, with "**the dim eye of a natural scientist**" — as if he studied faces like specimens.

**(ii) What fault does he find with the face? Two instructions.** [3]
He says "**the face is quite wrong**"; he does not like the head, and later finds the face "**a trifle too full**" (it would be better "three-quarters full"). **(iii) The funniest exaggerated description, and why.** [3]
*Personal response; for example:* the photographer **twisting the narrator's head sideways** while the narrator **closes his eyes, thinking he means to kiss him** — funny because the timid narrator so badly misreads a rough professional gesture. Or the instructions to **droop the ears** and **roll the eyes in under the lids**, which are physically impossible and show how absurdly the photographer treats a face as clay.

**(iv) What did the narrator want the photograph for? How does the story end?** [3]
He wanted a picture of **his face as Heaven gave it to him**, for **his friends to keep after his death** and remember him by. At the end, finding the proof **retouched beyond recognition**, he pours out his anger at the photographer — and then **breaks down in tears and leaves the studio**.

**(v) Compare the photographer's and the narrator's feelings on seeing the proof. Why do they feel so?** [4]
The photographer is **proud and pleased**: he thinks he has **improved the face** by retouching the eyes, removing the eyebrows (to put in new ones by the "Delphide"), raising the brow line and adjusting the mouth, and he plans to remove the ears by the "Sulphide". The narrator is **shocked, hurt and finally furious**; he does not recognise himself ("Is it me?"). They feel this way because the photographer cares only for **an ideal, artificial picture**, while the narrator wanted **a true likeness** of his own face, faults and all.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> When the photographer came out at last, he looked very grave and shook his head.
> "The face is quite wrong," he said.
> "I know," I answered quietly; "I have always known it."

**(i)** Where had the photographer been? What had he done before that? [3]
**Answer:** He had been **under the black cloth** of his camera ("the machine"), after crawling into it from behind. Before that he had looked once at the narrator, rushed out, and torn at the cotton sheet and window panes with a hooked stick, "frantic for light and air"; the narrator thought he was **praying** under the cloth.

**(ii)** What is humorous about the narrator's reply? [3]
**Answer:** Instead of being offended, he **meekly agrees** that his face is wrong and says he has always known it. This **self-mockery and understatement** is funny because no one expects a customer to accept such an insult so humbly.

**(iii)** What does the photographer suggest next, and how does the narrator misunderstand it? [3]
**Answer:** He suggests that the face would be better "**three-quarters full**" — turned partly to one side. The narrator thinks he means **fuller or rounder**, agrees enthusiastically, and starts a speech about narrow faces becoming "wide, large, almost boundless".

**(iv)** What does the photographer then do with the narrator's head? What does the narrator think? [3]
**Answer:** He takes the head in his hands and **twists it sideways** as far as it will go. The narrator thinks he means to **kiss** him and closes his eyes.

**(v)** What does this part of the story show about the photographer's attitude to his customer? Give two examples from the instructions that follow. [4]
**Answer:** He treats the narrator not as a person but as **material to be corrected**, ignoring his feelings and his words. Examples: "**The ears are bad; droop them a little more**"; "Roll the eyes in under the lids"; "**hump the neck … contract the waist … twist the hip up towards the elbow**" — impossible orders that show he wants a body that does not exist.

**Extract B.**
> "This face is my face. It is not yours, it is mine. I've lived with it for forty years and I know its faults."

**(i)** What provokes the narrator to say these words? [3]
**Answer:** The photographer's endless criticism and instructions — "I don't like the head", "The ears are bad", "droop them a little more", "hump the neck", and finally that the face is "a trifle too full".

**(ii)** What else does the narrator say in this speech? [3]
**Answer:** He knows his face is "out of drawing" and was not "made for" him, but it is the **only one he has**; such as it is, he has **learnt to love it**; this is his mouth and these are his ears, and if the machine is too narrow — at which point he starts to rise.

**(iii)** What happens at that moment? What does the photographer say? [3]
**Answer:** "**Snick!**" — the photographer pulls a string and **takes the photograph**; the narrator imagines the machine "staggering from the shock". The photographer says he caught the features "**just in a moment of animation**".

**(iv)** Why does the narrator reply "bitingly"? [3]
**Answer:** He is offended by the word "**features**" and by the suggestion that his face is ordinarily lifeless: "You didn't think I could animate them, I suppose?"

**(v)** What does this speech reveal about the narrator, and what is its importance in the story? [4]
**Answer:** It shows that, though timid and humble, he has **self-respect** and **accepts himself** with all his faults. It states the story's central **theme**: a person's face is their own identity and should not be "corrected" by someone else. It also prepares for the final outburst, when he rejects the falsified photograph.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** How does the photographer receive the narrator? What does the narrator do while waiting?
**Answer:** He looks at him without enthusiasm and curtly says, "Sit down. Wait."
The narrator waits an hour.
He reads very old magazines — from 1912, 1902 and even 1888.

**Q2.** Why does the narrator feel he has done "an unwarrantable thing"?
**Answer:** The photographer seems like a great scientist absorbed in his work.
The narrator feels he has intruded on his "scientific pursuits".
He even feels guilty for bringing "a face like mine" — comic self-mockery.

**Q3.** Describe what the photographer does with his camera and the light.
**Answer:** He rolls the camera into the room and crawls into it from behind.
After one look he rushes out, tearing at the cotton sheet and window panes, "frantic for light and air".
He then hides under a black cloth so quietly that the narrator thinks he is praying.

**Q4.** "The face is quite wrong." How does the narrator respond, and why is it funny?
**Answer:** He answers quietly, "I know; I have always known it."
Instead of being offended, he humbly agrees.
The humour lies in this unexpected self-mockery and understatement.

**Q5.** Explain the misunderstanding about "three-quarters full".
**Answer:** The photographer means a pose with the face turned three-quarters to the camera.
The narrator thinks it means plumper or fuller.
He eagerly agrees and starts a speech about faces becoming "boundless".

**Q6.** List any three of the photographer's instructions. Why are they funny?
**Answer:** "Droop the ears a little more"; "roll the eyes in under the lids"; "twist the hip up towards the elbow" (also: expand the lungs, hump the neck, contract the waist).
They are impossible for any human body to carry out.
They show the photographer treating the narrator like clay.

**Q7.** What does the narrator say in his first protest?
**Answer:** "This face is my face. It is not yours, it is mine."
He has lived with it for forty years and knows its faults.
It is the only face he has, and he has learnt to love it.

**Q8.** How and when is the photograph taken? What does the photographer say about it?
**Answer:** "Snick!" — he pulls a string while the narrator is protesting and rising.
The narrator imagines the machine staggering from the shock.
The photographer says he caught the features "just in a moment of animation".

**Q9.** What is a "proof"? Why did the narrator ask, "Is it me?" *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** A proof is a trial print shown to the customer before the final copy.
The narrator could not recognise himself.
The photographer had changed his features so much.

**Q10.** Which of the narrator's features had been altered? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** The eyes had been retouched.
The eyebrows removed (new ones by the Delphide), and the hair carried back to make a new brow line.
The mouth adjusted because it was "too low".

**Q11.** What was the only original feature in the photograph? How was it to be "fixed"? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** The ears — "a good likeness".
The photographer could fix that with the "Sulphide" process.
It would remove the ears entirely.

**Q12.** Why did the narrator want a photograph in the first place?
**Answer:** He wanted a picture that looked like him.
It would show his face "as Heaven gave it".
His friends could keep it after his death, to reconcile them to his loss.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** What makes the narrator angry at the end? Is his outburst justified? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** The photograph has no original feature left, and even the ears are to be removed.
He wanted a true likeness for his friends; he gets a stranger's face.
His outburst is justified: he has been humiliated and his sense of self ignored.
A portrait that does not resemble the sitter is, as he says, a "worthless bauble".

**Q14.** Describe the character of the photographer.
**Answer:** He is rude and unwelcoming — "Sit down. Wait."
He is self-important, like "a natural scientist".
He is obsessed with his own idea of perfection and ignores the customer's feelings.
He is proud of work that destroys the likeness.

**Q15.** Describe the character of the narrator.
**Answer:** He is timid and humble, waiting an hour and feeling guilty.
He is naive, misunderstanding "three-quarters full".
He is sensitive and emotional, but has self-respect: "This face is my face."
He is a comic hero whose grand speech ends in tears.

**Q16.** Explain any four techniques of humour Leacock uses.
**Answer:** Exaggeration: the photographer crawls into the camera; the machine staggers.
Understatement: "I know; I have always known it."
Misunderstanding: "three-quarters full"; expecting a kiss.
Absurd lists and invented words: "sulphide, bromide, oxide, cowhide"; the Delphide and Sulphide.

**Q17.** How is the story relevant in today's world?
**Answer:** Photo-editing apps and beauty filters change faces in seconds.
Many people post pictures that do not look like them.
This can harm self-esteem and create false ideals of beauty.
The narrator's cry, "This face is my face", supports accepting ourselves as we are.

**Q18.** Explain the irony in "Is it me?" — "Yes, it is you."
**Answer:** The photograph plainly does not look like the narrator.
Every feature has been altered.
Yet the photographer calmly insists it is him.
He believes the "improved" picture is truer than the real face — the reverse of the truth.

**Q19.** Explain the narrator's final speech beginning "Go on, then, with your brutal work."
**Answer:** He mockingly tells the photographer to do whatever he likes to the negative.
He lists absurd chemicals and changes — remove the eyes, reanimate the necktie, gild it.
He tells him to keep it for himself and his friends.
To the narrator it is "but a worthless bauble".

**Q20.** Discuss the significance of the title.
**Answer:** It names an ordinary, everyday visit — being "with" a photographer.
Its plainness makes the absurd events funnier.
It puts the photographer at the centre of the story.
It shows what happens when an ordinary person falls into the hands of an overbearing "expert".

He was a drooping man in a gray suit, with the dim eye of a natural scientist. (i) How does the photographer receive the narrator? What is suggested by "the dim eye of a natural scientist"? [3] (ii) How long does the narrator wait and what does he do? [3] (iii) How does the narrator feel while waiting? [3] (iv) What happens when he is called into the studio? [3] (v) What does this opening reveal about the two characters, and how does it set up the humour of the story? [4]

(ii) An hour; he reads very old magazines — a ladies' magazine of 1912, a girls' magazine of 1902 and an infants' journal of 1888 (2025 specimen Q1(xii)).
(iii) He feels he has done "an unwarrantable thing" in disturbing the photographer's "scientific pursuits" with a face like his — guilty and inferior.
(iv) The photographer says "Come in" severely; the narrator sits in softened sunlight; the photographer crawls into the camera, rushes out "frantic for light and air", then hides under a black cloth.
(v) The photographer is cold and self-important; the narrator is timid and humble; this role reversal — the paying customer apologetic, the shopkeeper superior — sets up the comic conflict and the exaggeration that runs through the story.

**Q2.** Read the extract and answer. "I think," said the photographer, pursing his lips in a pleased smile, "that I caught the features just in a moment of animation." (i) What had happened just before? [3] (ii) What had the narrator been saying? [3] (iii) Why does the narrator reply "bitingly"? What does he say? [3] (iv) Why can't the narrator see the picture at once? [3] (v) Comment on the humour of this episode. [4]
**Answer:** (i) The photographer had pulled a string — "Snick!" — and taken the photograph just as the narrator was rising from the seat in protest.
(ii) That his face was his own, not the photographer's; he had lived with it for forty years, knew its faults, but loved it; his mouth and ears were his own.
(iii) He resents the word "features" and the idea that his face is usually lifeless: "So! features, eh? You didn't think I could animate them, I suppose?"
(iv) The negative must first be developed; he is to come back on Saturday to see a proof.
(v) The photograph is taken at the most undignified moment, mid-protest; the photographer is pleased, calling it animation; the camera "staggering from the shock" is comic personification; the narrator's biting reply shows hurt pride — the contrast between his feelings and the photographer's satisfaction is the joke.

**Q3.** Read the extract and answer. "Listen! I came here for a photograph — a picture — something which (mad though it seems) would have looked like me." (i) What has the photographer just said about the ears? [3] (ii) What other changes had he made to the face? [3] (iii) What did the narrator want the photograph for? [3] (iv) What does the narrator tell the photographer to do with the photograph? [3] (v) What is the message of this ending, and how is it relevant today? [4]
**Answer:** (i) That they are a good likeness, but he can fix that in the print with the "Sulphide" process, which removes the ears entirely.
(ii) Eyes retouched; eyebrows removed (new ones by the Delphide); hair carried back to make a new brow line; mouth adjusted because it was too low.
(iii) To depict his face as Heaven gave it, humble though the gift was, for his friends to keep after his death, to reconcile them to his loss.
(iv) To do whatever he likes to it — dip it in "sulphide, bromide, oxide, cowhide", remove the eyes, gild it — and then keep it for himself and his friends; to the narrator it is "a worthless bauble".
(v) A person's real face is part of their identity and should not be replaced by artificial perfection; today, with beauty filters and edited selfies, the story warns against false images that make people ashamed of their natural looks.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Story at a glance
- Narrator asks for a photograph; photographer "without enthusiasm", "the dim eye of a natural scientist"; "Sit down. Wait."
- Waits an hour; reads old magazines (1912, 1902, 1888); feels he has done "an unwarrantable thing".
- Studio: softened sunlight; photographer crawls into the camera, rushes out "frantic for light and air", hides under the black cloth — "praying".
- "The face is quite wrong." — "I know; I have always known it."
- "Three-quarters full" misunderstood; head twisted — he thinks he will be kissed.
- Instructions: mouth open/close; "droop the ears"; roll the eyes; expand lungs; hump neck; contract waist; twist hip to elbow.
- Protest: "This face is my face … the only one I have … I've learned to love it."
- "Snick!" — "a moment of animation"; negative to be developed; proof on Saturday.
- Proof: "Is it me?" — "Yes, it is you." Eyes retouched; eyebrows removed (Delphide); hair and brow line changed; mouth adjusted; ears a good likeness — to be removed by the Sulphide.
- Outburst: "as Heaven gave it to me"; for friends after his death; "sulphide, bromide, oxide, cowhide"; "keep it for yourself"; "a worthless bauble"; tears; leaves.

**Section B (16 marks each)**

**9.** (i) Under the black cloth of his camera; the narrator thought he was praying (3).
(ii) "I know; I have always known it" — meek self-mockery instead of offence (3).
(iii) A pose with three-quarters of the face visible; the narrator thought it meant plumper (3).
(iv) Any three: open/close the mouth; droop the ears; roll the eyes under the lids; hands on knees; expand the lungs; hump the neck; contract the waist; twist the hip towards the elbow (3).
(v) He treats the narrator as material to be moulded, not a person; obsessed with an impossible ideal; deaf to feelings (4).

**10.** (i) The photographer's endless criticism — "I don't like the head", the ears, the face "a trifle too full" (3).
(ii) He has lived with it forty years, knows its faults, it is the only one he has, and he has learnt to love it (3).
(iii) "Snick!" — the photograph is taken as he rises (3).
(iv) "a moment of animation"; "So! features, eh? You didn't think I could animate them, I suppose?" (3).
(v) It states the theme of identity and self-acceptance against artificial "improvement"; prepares for the final rejection (4).

**11.** (i) A trial print before the final copy; "Is it me?" (3).
(ii) Eyes retouched; eyebrows removed, new ones by the Delphide; mouth adjusted as too low (3).
(iii) He can fix that with the Sulphide, which removes the ears entirely (3).
(iv) Keep it for himself and his friends; it is a worthless bauble to the narrator (3).
(v) Personal response, e.g., yes — humiliated, identity erased, the picture useless for its purpose (4).
