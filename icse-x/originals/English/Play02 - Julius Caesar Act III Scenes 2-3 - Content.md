## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

Act III, Scene 2 takes place in the **Forum**, the great public square of Rome, a few hours after the assassination. Two men speak to the same crowd about the same dead man. **Brutus** speaks first, in calm **prose**, appealing to the people's **reason** and to his own **honour**. The crowd cheers him and even wants to make him Caesar. **Mark Antony** speaks next, in passionate **blank verse**, appealing to the people's **feelings** and **self-interest**. By the end of his speech the same crowd is running through the streets with fire-brands to burn the houses of the conspirators.

Scene 3 is a short, terrible scene: the maddened mob meets an innocent poet who happens to be named **Cinna**, the same name as one of the conspirators, and tears him to pieces. The scenes show how easily a crowd can be swayed by words, and how quickly the "freedom" the conspirators promised turns into **mob violence** and **civil war**.

### Where we are: a recap of Act III, Scene 1

Caesar has been stabbed to death in the Capitol on the Ides of March. Brutus declared that "ambition's debt is paid", and the conspirators bathed their hands in Caesar's blood. **Antony** sent a servant to ask for safe-conduct, shook the bloody hands of the conspirators, and asked to speak at Caesar's funeral. **Cassius** warned Brutus, "You know not what you do", but Brutus agreed, on **conditions**: Antony must not blame the conspirators, must speak all the good he can of Caesar, must say he speaks by their permission, and must speak from the same pulpit **after** Brutus. Alone, Antony revealed his rage, calling the conspirators "butchers" and prophesying civil war. A servant reported that **Octavius** was within seven leagues of Rome.

### Scene 2, retold in our own words

#### 1. The citizens demand an explanation

Brutus and Cassius enter the Forum with a crowd of **citizens (plebeians)** who demand, "We will be satisfied; let us be satisfied." Brutus divides the audience: those who want to hear Cassius are to follow him to another street; those who stay will hear Brutus. "**Public reasons shall be rendered / Of Caesar's death.**" Brutus goes up into the **pulpit** (the public speaking platform).

#### 2. Brutus's speech

Brutus speaks in **prose**, in short, balanced sentences:

> "Romans, countrymen, and lovers! hear me for my cause, and be silent, that you may hear: believe me for mine honour, and have respect to mine honour, that you may believe: censure me in your wisdom, and awake your senses, that you may the better judge."

He asks to be believed because of his **honour** — his reputation for honesty. ("Lovers" here means dear friends; "censure" means judge.) If any friend of Caesar asks why Brutus rose against Caesar, his answer is:

> "**Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.**"

He asks a pointed question: would they rather Caesar were living and they all died **slaves**, or Caesar dead and they all lived **free men**? Then comes his most carefully balanced sentence:

> "As Caesar loved me, I weep for him; as he was fortunate, I rejoice at it; as he was valiant, I honour him: but, as he was ambitious, I slew him."

So there are "tears for his love; joy for his fortune; honour for his valour; and death for his ambition." Brutus then challenges the crowd with three **rhetorical questions**: "Who is here so **base** that would be a bondman? … Who is here so **rude** that would not be a Roman? … Who is here so **vile** that will not love his country? If any, speak; for him have I offended. **I pause for a reply.**" The crowd answers, "**None, Brutus, none.**"

Brutus says the reasons for Caesar's death are **recorded ("enrolled") in the Capitol**: Caesar's glory has not been made smaller ("extenuated") where he deserved it, nor his offences exaggerated ("enforced").

#### 3. Antony enters with the body; the crowd cheers Brutus

Antony and others enter with **Caesar's body**. Brutus points out that Antony, though he had no part in the killing, will share in its benefits — a place in the free **commonwealth** — "as which of you shall not?" He ends with a dramatic promise: "as I slew my best lover for the good of Rome, **I have the same dagger for myself, when it shall please my country to need my death**."

The crowd is completely won over: "**Live, Brutus! live, live!**" "Bring him with triumph home unto his house." "Give him a statue with his ancestors." "**Let him be Caesar.**" "Caesar's better parts / Shall be crown'd in Brutus." This is deeply **ironic**: Brutus killed Caesar to stop one man from ruling Rome, and the people now want to make Brutus exactly what Caesar was. They have not understood his cause at all.

Brutus asks the people to stay and hear Antony, "Tending to Caesar's glories; which Mark Antony, / By our permission, is allow'd to make", and **departs alone** — his second great mistake (the first was letting Antony speak at all). He leaves the crowd and the last word to Antony.

#### 4. Antony goes up — a hostile audience

Antony goes up into the pulpit ("For Brutus' sake, I am beholding to you"). The mood is against him: "'Twere best he speak no harm of Brutus here." "This Caesar was a tyrant." "We are blest that Rome is rid of him." Antony must win over a crowd that has just cheered Caesar's killers — and he must do it without breaking Brutus's conditions.

#### 5. "Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears"

> "Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears;
> I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him.
> The evil that men do lives after them;
> The good is oft interred with their bones;
> So let it be with Caesar. The noble Brutus
> Hath told you Caesar was ambitious:
> If it were so, it was a grievous fault,
> And grievously hath Caesar answer'd it.
> Here, under leave of Brutus and the rest —
> For Brutus is an honourable man;
> So are they all, all honourable men —
> Come I to speak in Caesar's funeral."

Antony begins **humbly and cautiously**. He claims he has come only to **bury** Caesar, **not to praise** him — though in fact the whole speech praises Caesar and turns the people against the conspirators. He says people remember a man's **evil deeds**, while his **good deeds are buried** with him. He seems to accept Brutus's charge — "If it were so" — but the little word "**if**" already raises a doubt.

He then gives **three pieces of evidence** that Caesar was **not ambitious**, and after each he repeats, with growing **irony**, "**Brutus says he was ambitious; / And Brutus is an honourable man**":

1. **Captives and ransoms:** "He hath brought many captives home to Rome / Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill: / Did this in Caesar seem ambitious?" — Caesar filled the **public treasury**, not his own purse.
2. **Pity for the poor:** "When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept: / **Ambition should be made of sterner stuff**" — an ambitious man would be harder-hearted.
3. **The crown refused:** "You all did see that on the Lupercal / I **thrice presented him a kingly crown, / Which he did thrice refuse**: was this ambition?"

He insists, "**I speak not to disprove what Brutus spoke, / But here I am to speak what I do know**" — and that is exactly what he is doing. "You all did love him once, not without cause: / What cause withholds you then, to mourn for him? / **O judgment! thou art fled to brutish beasts, / And men have lost their reason.**" (There may be a pun on **brutish / Brutus**.) Then he breaks off, overcome: "**My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, / And I must pause till it come back to me.**" The pause is real grief — and also a **calculated** moment that lets him watch the crowd's reaction.

#### 6. The crowd begins to change

The citizens murmur: "Methinks there is much reason in his sayings." "Caesar has had great wrong." "He would not take the crown; / Therefore 'tis certain he was not ambitious." "Poor soul! his eyes are red as fire with weeping." "There's not a nobler man in Rome than Antony." In a few minutes, the crowd that cheered Brutus is starting to doubt him.

#### 7. The will — mentioned, but not read

Antony continues: "But yesterday the word of Caesar might / Have stood against the world; now lies he there, / And none so poor to do him reverence." He pretends he does not want to stir them to "**mutiny and rage**", for that would "do Brutus wrong, and Cassius wrong, / Who, you all know, are honourable men" — he would rather wrong the dead, himself and them.

Then he produces a **parchment with Caesar's seal** — **Caesar's will**, found in his private room ("closet"). He says he does not mean to read it, but if the common people heard it, they would kiss Caesar's wounds, dip their handkerchiefs ("napkins") in his "sacred blood", and beg a hair of him to leave to their children as a rich legacy.

Naturally the crowd demands, "**The will! read the will!**" Antony **teases** them: "It is not meet you know how Caesar loved you. / You are not wood, you are not stones, but men; / And, being men, bearing the will of Caesar, / It will inflame you, it will make you mad: / '**Tis good you know not that you are his heirs**." By refusing, he has told them the most important thing — that they are Caesar's **heirs**. He fears he has "o'ershot" himself, and that he wrongs "the honourable men / Whose daggers have stabb'd Caesar". The crowd now shouts: "**They were traitors: honourable men!**" "They were villains, murderers." The word "honourable" has turned into an insult.

#### 8. The mantle and the wounds

"You will compel me, then, to read the will? / Then make a ring about the corpse of Caesar, / And let me show you him that made the will." Antony **comes down** from the pulpit to stand among the people — no longer a speaker above them but a mourner among them.

> "**If you have tears, prepare to shed them now.**
> You all do know this mantle: I remember
> The first time ever Caesar put it on;
> 'Twas on a summer's evening, in his tent,
> That day he overcame the Nervii."

Antony holds up Caesar's **mantle (cloak)**, now torn and bloody. He reminds them of Caesar's victory over the **Nervii**, a fierce tribe in Gaul. Then he points to the holes in the cloak, as if he knew which dagger made each one: "Look, in this place ran **Cassius'** dagger through: / See what a rent the **envious Casca** made: / Through this the **well-beloved Brutus** stabb'd." (He did not see the stabbing — he was drawn away by Trebonius — but the crowd does not stop to ask.)

He imagines Caesar's blood rushing out "as rushing out of doors, to be resolved / If Brutus so unkindly knock'd, or no" — **personifying** the blood. "For Brutus, as you know, was Caesar's **angel**" (his dearest favourite):

> "**This was the most unkindest cut of all;**
> For when the noble Caesar saw him stab,
> Ingratitude, more strong than traitors' arms,
> Quite vanquish'd him: then burst his mighty heart."

"Most unkindest" is a **double superlative**, stressing the cruelty; "unkind" also carries the old sense of "unnatural" — against one's own kind. Caesar was killed not so much by the daggers as by Brutus's **ingratitude**. He fell, muffling his face in his mantle, "Even at the base of **Pompey's statua**, / Which all the while ran blood". "**O, what a fall was there, my countrymen!** / Then I, and you, and all of us fell down, / Whilst bloody treason flourish'd over us." Caesar's fall is made the fall of every Roman.

#### 9. The body revealed — "Revenge!"

The people weep. Antony says, "Kind souls, what, weep you when you but behold / Our Caesar's vesture wounded? Look you here, / **Here is himself, marr'd, as you see, with traitors.**" He pulls the cloak away from the **body** — the most powerful "prop" of all. The crowd explodes: "O piteous spectacle!" "O noble Caesar!" "O traitors, villains!" "**Revenge! About! Seek! Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay! / Let not a traitor live!**"

Antony "**restrains**" them — which only inflames them more:

> "Good friends, sweet friends, let me not stir you up
> To such a sudden flood of mutiny.
> They that have done this deed are honourable:
> What private griefs they have, alas, I know not,
> That made them do it …"

He claims, "**I am no orator, as Brutus is; / But, as you know me all, a plain blunt man, / That love my friend**", with neither "wit, nor words, nor worth, / Action, nor utterance, nor the power of speech, / To stir men's blood". This is **irony** — he is the greatest orator in the play and is doing exactly that. If he were Brutus, he says, he would "**put a tongue / In every wound of Caesar** that should move / The stones of Rome to rise and mutiny." The crowd roars, "We'll mutiny!" "We'll burn the house of Brutus!"

#### 10. The will read

Antony calls them back: "Why, friends, you go to do you know not what … **You have forgot the will I told you of.**" Now he reads it:

- to every Roman citizen, **seventy-five drachmas** (silver coins);
- all Caesar's **walks, private arbours and new-planted orchards** on this side of the **Tiber**, left to the people and their heirs **for ever** as **public parks** — "common pleasures, / To walk abroad, and recreate yourselves."

"**Here was a Caesar! when comes such another?**" The citizens answer, "Never, never." They decide to **burn Caesar's body in the holy place** and with the burning brands **set fire to the traitors' houses**. They tear down benches, windows, anything that will burn, and rush off carrying the body.

#### 11. "Mischief, thou art afoot"

Alone, Antony coolly remarks:

> "**Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot,
> Take thou what course thou wilt!**"

He has deliberately unleashed the riot and does not care where it leads. This shows his **ruthless** side: the grieving friend is also a calculating politician.

#### 12. News of Octavius and of Brutus

A **servant of Octavius** reports that Octavius **has already come to Rome**; he and **Lepidus** are at Caesar's house. Antony will go to him at once: "He comes upon a wish. **Fortune is merry**, / And in this mood will give us any thing." The servant adds that **Brutus and Cassius "are rid like madmen through the gates of Rome"** — they have fled. Antony replies, "Belike they had some notice of the people, / How I had moved them." The conspirators who ruled Rome in the morning are fugitives by evening.

### Scene 3, retold: Cinna the poet

**Cinna the poet** — not the conspirator — enters a street. He had dreamt that he feasted with Caesar, and "things unlucky charge my fantasy" (his imagination is full of ill omens). He has no wish to go out, "Yet something leads me forth" — to Caesar's funeral.

A group of **citizens** surrounds him and fires questions: What is your name? Where are you going? Where do you live? Are you married or a bachelor? Answer "directly", "briefly", "wisely", "truly". Cinna answers with a little wit ("Wisely I say, I am a bachelor"), which a citizen takes as an insult to married men. He says he goes to the funeral "as a friend" to Caesar, and lives "by the Capitol".

When he gives his name — "**Truly, my name is Cinna**" — the mob cries, "**Tear him to pieces; he's a conspirator.**" He protests: "**I am Cinna the poet, I am Cinna the poet.**" "**Tear him for his bad verses**," one shouts. "I am not Cinna the conspirator." "**It is no matter, his name's Cinna**: pluck but his name out of his heart, and turn him going." They kill him and rush off with fire-brands to burn the houses of Brutus, Cassius, Decius, Casca and Ligarius.

The scene shows the **senseless cruelty of a mob**: it no longer cares about truth or justice. The death of Cinna is **tragic and unnecessary** — the first innocent victim of the "havoc" Antony prophesied. *(2027 specimen Q1(v).)*

### Brutus and Antony: two speeches compared

| Point | Brutus's speech | Antony's speech |
|---|---|---|
| form | **prose** — plain, balanced, logical | **blank verse** — rhythmic, poetic, passionate |
| appeal | to **reason** and to his own **honour** | to **emotion**, **pity** and **self-interest** |
| length | short | long, in stages, with pauses |
| method | balanced sentences (antithesis); rhetorical questions; "I pause for a reply" | irony ("honourable men"); repetition; rhetorical questions; pauses; props (will, mantle, body) |
| evidence | asks to be believed; gives no proof that Caesar was ambitious | gives **facts**: ransoms, tears for the poor, the crown refused thrice; then the will |
| attitude to the crowd | speaks **above** them, assumes they will understand his principles | speaks **among** them, comes down, makes them a ring, calls them "friends", "kind souls" |
| result | crowd cheers him but misunderstands: "Let him be Caesar" | crowd riots: "Revenge! … Burn! Fire! Kill!" |
| mistake / skill | leaves before Antony speaks | keeps every condition in words, yet destroys the conspirators |

### Conclusion: why these scenes matter

- They are the **turning point** in the fortunes of the conspirators: by the end of Scene 2, Brutus and Cassius have fled Rome.
- They show the **power of oratory** — words, not swords, decide who rules Rome.
- They show the **fickleness of the crowd**: the people cheer Brutus, then want to burn his house within minutes.
- They show **Antony** at his most brilliant and most ruthless ("Mischief, thou art afoot").
- They show the **cost of political violence**: the innocent Cinna is killed only for his name.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The citizens and Brutus's speech

- **What happens:** the crowd demands reasons; Cassius takes some away; Brutus speaks in prose.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Romans, countrymen, and lovers!**" — *lovers* = dear friends. Brutus places "Romans" first: he appeals to them as citizens of the Republic.
  - "**believe me for mine honour**" — Brutus asks to be trusted because of his reputation; he relies on **ethos** (his own character) rather than evidence.
  - "**Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.**" — **antithesis**: the love of country outweighs love of a friend.
  - "**As Caesar loved me, I weep for him … but, as he was ambitious, I slew him.**" — a carefully balanced series; the final clause is the turn.
  - "**Who is here so base that would be a bondman?**" — *base* = low; *bondman* = slave. Three rhetorical questions that no one can answer "yes" without shame.
  - "**I pause for a reply.**" — a confident challenge; the crowd answers, "None, Brutus, none."
- **Exam focus:** compare with Antony's method (2027 specimen Q2 asks about Antony's arguments).

### Section 2 — Brutus's triumph and departure

- **What happens:** Antony brings in the body; Brutus offers to die by the same dagger if Rome needs it; the crowd cheers and wants to crown him; Brutus leaves Antony to speak.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**I have the same dagger for myself, when it shall please my country to need my death.**" — a noble, dramatic gesture; also **foreshadowing**, since Brutus will die on his own sword at Philippi.
  - "**Let him be Caesar. / Caesar's better parts / Shall be crown'd in Brutus.**" — **dramatic irony**: the crowd wants a new Caesar, which is exactly what Brutus killed to prevent.
  - "**Good countrymen, let me depart alone, / And, for my sake, stay here with Antony**" — Brutus's fatal over-confidence: he leaves the field to Antony.

### Section 3 — The opening of Antony's speech

- **What happens:** Antony faces a hostile crowd and begins carefully.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears**" — "Friends" comes first: Antony appeals to warmth and friendship. *Lend me your ears* = listen to me (metonymy).
  - "**I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him.**" — he seems to obey Brutus and calm the crowd; in fact he will praise Caesar throughout. *(2027 specimen Q2(i))*
  - "**The evil that men do lives after them; / The good is oft interred with their bones**" — people remember a man's wrongs and forget his good deeds. *(2027 specimen Q2(v))*
  - "**If it were so, it was a grievous fault, / And grievously hath Caesar answer'd it**" — the "if" casts doubt; Caesar has paid heavily (with his life).
  - "**For Brutus is an honourable man; / So are they all, all honourable men**" — at first respectful; by repetition it becomes **sarcastic** (verbal irony).

### Section 4 — The three arguments

- **What happens:** Antony disproves the charge of ambition with evidence.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**He hath brought many captives home to Rome / Whose ransoms did the general coffers fill**" — the ransom money went into the public treasury.
  - "**When that the poor have cried, Caesar hath wept: / Ambition should be made of sterner stuff**" — a truly ambitious man would be harder.
  - "**I thrice presented him a kingly crown, / Which he did thrice refuse: was this ambition?**" — the crowd saw it with their own eyes at the feast of the Lupercal.
  - "**I speak not to disprove what Brutus spoke, / But here I am to speak what I do know**" — irony: he is disproving Brutus point by point.
  - "**O judgment! thou art fled to brutish beasts, / And men have lost their reason**" — apostrophe; good sense seems to have left human beings.
  - "**My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, / And I must pause till it come back to me**" — a moving metaphor; also a skilful pause.
- **Exam focus:** 2027 specimen Q2(iii) (any two arguments and their impact) and Q2(iv) (which argument had the greatest impact).

### Section 5 — The will as bait

- **What happens:** Antony mentions the will, refuses to read it, and makes the crowd beg.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**But yesterday the word of Caesar might / Have stood against the world; now lies he there, / And none so poor to do him reverence**" — contrast between yesterday's power and today's helplessness; even the poorest man will not honour him.
  - "**You are not wood, you are not stones, but men**" — he flatters their humanity and feelings.
  - "**'Tis good you know not that you are his heirs**" — **reverse psychology**: by "not telling", he tells them they are the heirs.
  - "**They were traitors: honourable men!**" (a citizen) — the irony has done its work; "honourable" is now a sneer.

### Section 6 — The mantle and the body

- **What happens:** Antony descends, shows the cloak, the wounds and finally the body.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**If you have tears, prepare to shed them now.**" — a direct emotional appeal.
  - "**That day he overcame the Nervii**" — reminds them of Caesar's service to Rome.
  - "**See what a rent the envious Casca made**" — *rent* = tear; "envious" = full of malice.
  - "**This was the most unkindest cut of all**" — double superlative; Brutus's blow was the cruellest because he was Caesar's beloved friend ("Caesar's angel").
  - "**Ingratitude, more strong than traitors' arms, / Quite vanquish'd him**" — personification: ingratitude, not daggers, killed Caesar.
  - "**O, what a fall was there, my countrymen!**" — Caesar's fall is the fall of every Roman.
  - "**Here is himself, marr'd, as you see, with traitors.**" — he reveals the body; the conspirators are now openly "traitors".

### Section 7 — "I am no orator"; the will read

- **What happens:** Antony pretends to calm the crowd, claims he is no speaker, then reads the will.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**I am no orator, as Brutus is; / But … a plain blunt man, / That love my friend**" — false modesty and irony.
  - "**put a tongue / In every wound of Caesar that should move / The stones of Rome to rise and mutiny**" — metaphor: each wound becomes a mouth crying for revenge; even stones would rebel.
  - "**seventy-five drachmas**" to each citizen; **walks, arbours and orchards** as public parks — an appeal to **self-interest**.
  - "**Here was a Caesar! when comes such another?**" — exclamation and rhetorical question; the crowd's answer: "Never, never."

### Section 8 — "Mischief, thou art afoot"; Scene 3

- **What happens:** the mob riots; Antony is satisfied; Octavius has arrived; Brutus and Cassius have fled; Cinna the poet is murdered.
- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt!**" — apostrophe to "Mischief"; Antony's cold indifference to the violence he has started.
  - "**Fortune is merry**" — Antony sees luck turning his way.
  - "**Brutus and Cassius / Are rid like madmen through the gates of Rome**" — *rid* = ridden; they have fled in panic.
  - "**I am Cinna the poet**" / "**Tear him for his bad verses**" / "**It is no matter, his name's Cinna**" — mob madness; dark irony and cruel humour.
- **Exam focus:** 2027 specimen Q1(v) (the death of Cinna the poet: tragic and unnecessary).

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- In the historical account by Plutarch, Antony's funeral speech, the display of Caesar's bloody robe and the reading of the will did stir the people to burn the body in the Forum and attack the conspirators' houses.
- A man named Helvius Cinna, a poet, really was killed by the mob after being mistaken for the conspirator Cornelius Cinna, according to Plutarch.
- "Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears" is among the most quoted lines in English; its techniques (repetition, irony, rhetorical questions) are still studied in courses on public speaking.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **The power of language (rhetoric):** words decide the fate of Rome; Antony's speech undoes the conspirators more completely than any army.
- **Reason versus emotion:** Brutus appeals to reason and principle; Antony appeals to feeling and self-interest — and emotion wins.
- **The fickleness of the crowd:** the people switch sides within minutes; they follow whoever speaks last and most movingly.
- **Appearance and reality:** Antony appears to obey Brutus's conditions and to praise the conspirators as "honourable", while really destroying them.
- **Violence breeds violence:** the killing of Caesar leads to riot, the murder of the innocent Cinna, and civil war.
- **Political manipulation:** Antony uses grief, gifts and props to move the people; he then uses their fury for his own ends.

### 7.1 Irony in the scenes

1. **Dramatic irony:** the crowd cries "Let him be Caesar" to Brutus, who killed Caesar to prevent one-man rule.
2. **Verbal irony:** "Brutus is an honourable man" — repeated until it means the opposite.
3. **Verbal irony:** "I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him" — his speech is all praise.
4. **Verbal irony:** "I am no orator, as Brutus is" — said by the greatest orator in the play.
5. **Situational irony:** Brutus permitted Antony to speak to help the conspirators' cause; the speech ruins them.
6. **Dark irony:** "Tear him for his bad verses" — a poet is killed for his poetry and his name.

### 7.2 The stages of Antony's speech

| Stage | What Antony does | Effect on the crowd |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Humble opening | "I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him" | hostile crowd agrees to listen |
| 2. Evidence | ransoms, tears for the poor, the crown refused thrice | "Methinks there is much reason in his sayings" |
| 3. The pause | "My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar" | pity for Antony; doubt about Brutus |
| 4. The will as bait | mentions it, refuses to read it, hints they are heirs | "The will! read the will!"; "They were traitors" |
| 5. Descends; the mantle | Nervii; Cassius's, Casca's, Brutus's cuts; "most unkindest cut" | the people weep |
| 6. The body | "Here is himself, marr'd … with traitors" | "Revenge! … Burn! Fire! Kill!" |
| 7. False restraint | "I am no orator"; "a plain blunt man" | "We'll mutiny" |
| 8. The will read | 75 drachmas; walks and orchards for ever | "Never, never"; the mob runs to burn the houses |

### 7.3 Foreshadowing

- "I have the same dagger for myself" — Brutus's death by his own sword in Act V.
- Brutus and Cassius "rid like madmen" from Rome — their flight to the east and the war that follows (Acts IV and V).
- Octavius and Lepidus with Antony — the **triumvirate** of Act IV.

**2027 specimen Q1(v).** **(a) tragic and unnecessary.** Cinna the poet was innocent; the mob killed him only because he shared a name with the conspirator Cinna.

**2027 specimen Q2.**

**(i) What does Antony say he is there for? What does he say he is not there for? What does this tell you about him?** [3]
Antony says he has come **to bury Caesar**. He says he has **not** come **to praise Caesar**. This shows that he is **manipulative / deceptive**: he appears to respect Brutus's conditions and calm the hostile crowd, but in fact his whole speech praises Caesar and turns the people against the conspirators.

**(ii) Mention any two of the conditions that Brutus had laid down before allowing Antony to speak to the citizens of Rome. Why do you think he laid down these conditions?** [3]
Conditions (any two): **Brutus would speak first**; Antony would say he was speaking **with the permission** of the conspirators; Antony would **not blame** the conspirators; Antony would **speak well of Caesar**; Antony would speak **from the same pulpit** as Brutus. Brutus laid them down because he was **cautious / anxious** that Antony might **influence the crowd** against the conspirators — Cassius had warned him against allowing Antony to speak.

**(iii) List any two arguments that Antony uses immediately after he speaks these lines to prove conclusively that Julius Caesar was not ambitious. What impact do his words have on the listening Romans?** [3]
Arguments (any two): Caesar **brought many captives to Rome whose ransoms filled the general coffers** (the public treasury, not his own); Caesar **wept when the poor cried** — "Ambition should be made of sterner stuff"; Caesar **refused the crown three times** at the feast of the Lupercal. **Impact:** the Romans are **convinced that Caesar was not ambitious** — "Methinks there is much reason in his sayings" — and are gradually **stirred to rage** against the conspirators.

**(iv) Which one argument of Antony's do you think had the greatest impact on his listeners? Give reasons to support your answer.** [3]
*(Personal response — choose one argument and give two matching reasons.)* **Model answer (the will):** Caesar's **will** had the greatest impact. (1) It appealed to the people's **self-interest**: each citizen received seventy-five drachmas, and all received Caesar's walks, arbours and orchards as public parks for ever. (2) It proved Caesar's **love for the common people**, so they cried, "Here was a Caesar! when comes such another?" and rushed off to burn the traitors' houses. *(Other acceptable choices: Caesar's tears for the poor; the crown refused thrice; the ransoms; Caesar's robe; Caesar's mutilated body — each with two matching reasons.)*

**(v) Explain the following lines: 'The evil that men do lives after them, / The good is oft interred with their bones;' How can these lines be applied to Brutus and Caesar?** [4]
**Explanation:** People **remember only the wrongs** a person has done; their **good deeds are forgotten** and buried with them when they die. **Application:** after his death, **Brutus** is remembered for having **betrayed his friend** — his disloyalty to Caesar; **Caesar** is remembered by his killers for his **ambition and greed for power**, while his generosity and services to Rome are forgotten — until Antony reminds the people of them.

**2026 Improvement Q1(iv).** **(b) Logical and well-reasoned.** Brutus speaks in plain prose and appeals to reason and honour ("Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more"); it is Antony's speech that is emotional.

**2025 Q1(iii).** **(b) Cinna the poet.** The mob kills him only because he has the same name as Cinna the conspirator ("Tear him for his bad verses").

**2025 Q2.**

**(i) What does Antony say he is there for? What does he say he is not there for? What do you think he is actually there for?** [3]
He says he has come **to bury Caesar** — to speak at his funeral. He says he has **not come to praise him**. In fact he has come **to turn the people against the conspirators**: to stir them to mutiny and expose Brutus and the others for what they are.

**(ii) What were the three conditions that Brutus had laid down before allowing Antony to speak to the citizens of Rome?** [3]
Any three: **Brutus would speak first**; Antony must say he speaks **with the permission of the conspirators**; he must **not blame the conspirators**; he **may speak all good of Caesar**; he must speak **from the same pulpit**, after Brutus has finished.

**(iii) List the three arguments that Antony uses immediately after he speaks these lines to prove conclusively that Julius Caesar was not ambitious.** [3]
1. Caesar **brought many captives home to Rome**, and their ransoms **filled the general coffers** (the public treasury), not his own.
2. **When the poor cried, Caesar wept**; ambition "should be made of sterner stuff".
3. On the Lupercal Antony **three times offered him a kingly crown, which he three times refused**.

**(iv) Antony repeatedly uses certain words in his speech to describe Brutus. What are they? Why does he do this?** [3]
"**Brutus is an honourable man**" (and "so are they all, all honourable men"). He has promised **not to speak ill of the conspirators**, so he cannot attack them openly. By repeating the words after each proof of Caesar's goodness, he turns them into **sarcasm and irony**: the people begin to see that Brutus is **not honourable at all**, and the words end up mocking him.

**(v) Which one argument of Antony's do you think had the greatest impact on his listeners? Give a reason. What were the citizens' feelings towards Antony before he began, and how do they change by the end?** [4]
*Personal response; for example:* **Caesar's will**, which leaves every Roman **seventy-five drachmas** and his private gardens. It moves the citizens most because it proves, in money they can touch, that Caesar **loved the people** of Rome. (Other good choices: the sight of Caesar's **mutilated body**, or his **blood-stained mantle** pierced by the daggers.)
Before the speech the citizens are **hostile and suspicious** ("'Twere best he speak no harm of Brutus here"); at the end they are **loyal to Antony and wild for revenge** on the conspirators.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> **Brutus:** If then that friend demand why Brutus rose against Caesar, this is my answer: — Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more. Had you rather Caesar were living and die all slaves, than that Caesar were dead, to live all free men?

**(i)** Where is Brutus speaking and to whom? Why has he come there? [3]
**Answer:** In the **Forum** in Rome, from the **pulpit**, to the **citizens (plebeians)**. They had demanded to be "satisfied" — given reasons for Caesar's death — and Brutus has come to give them **public reasons** for the assassination.

**(ii)** Explain "Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more". [3]
**Answer:** Brutus did not kill Caesar because he did not love him; he loved Caesar, but his **love for Rome and its freedom was greater**. When the two loves clashed, he chose Rome.

**(iii)** What choice does Brutus put before the citizens in the last sentence? What figure of speech is it? [3]
**Answer:** Would they rather have **Caesar alive and all of them slaves**, or **Caesar dead and all of them free men**? It is a **rhetorical question** built on **antithesis** (living/dead, slaves/free men).

**(iv)** What three rhetorical questions does Brutus ask later in the speech, and how does the crowd reply? [3]
**Answer:** "Who is here so **base** that would be a bondman?" "Who is here so **rude** that would not be a Roman?" "Who is here so **vile** that will not love his country?" He says, "I pause for a reply," and the crowd answers, "**None, Brutus, none.**"

**(v)** How does the crowd respond at the end of Brutus's speech? What does its response show about the crowd and about Brutus? [4]
**Answer:** They cry "Live, Brutus! live, live!", want to give him a statue with his ancestors, and shout "**Let him be Caesar**" and "Caesar's better parts / Shall be crown'd in Brutus." This shows that the crowd has **not understood** his cause at all — they want a new Caesar, the very thing Brutus killed to prevent; they are **fickle** and moved by personality, not principle. It also shows Brutus's **naivety**: he believes he has convinced them by reason and leaves them to Antony.

**Extract B.**
> **Antony:** If you have tears, prepare to shed them now.
> You all do know this mantle: I remember
> The first time ever Caesar put it on;
> 'Twas on a summer's evening, in his tent,
> That day he overcame the Nervii.

**(i)** What had Antony done just before these lines? Why? [3]
**Answer:** He had **come down from the pulpit** and asked the citizens to **make a ring around Caesar's corpse**. He had told them he would read the will, but first wanted to show them "him that made the will", so that he could stand **among** them and move them with the sight of the body.

**(ii)** What is a "mantle"? Why does Antony mention the Nervii? [3]
**Answer:** A **mantle** is a cloak. By recalling the **victory over the Nervii**, a fierce tribe of Gaul, he reminds the people of Caesar's **great services to Rome** and makes the cloak a symbol of his glory.

**(iii)** How does Antony describe the stabs made by Cassius, Casca and Brutus? [3]
**Answer:** "Look, in this place ran **Cassius'** dagger through: / See what a rent the **envious Casca** made: / Through this the **well-beloved Brutus** stabb'd." He points to particular holes in the cloak, as if he knew which dagger made each.

**(iv)** Why does Antony call Brutus's blow "the most unkindest cut of all"? [3]
**Answer:** Brutus was "**Caesar's angel**", his dearest friend; when Caesar saw him stab, **ingratitude** hurt him more than the weapons — "then burst his mighty heart". "Most unkindest" is a **double superlative** stressing the cruelty.

**(v)** What does Antony do next, and how does the crowd react? What makes this part of the speech so effective? [4]
**Answer:** He **uncovers Caesar's body**: "Here is himself, marr'd, as you see, with traitors." The crowd cries, "O piteous spectacle!" "**Revenge! About! Seek! Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay!**" It is effective because he moves from **words to visible evidence** — the torn cloak, then the wounds — appealing directly to pity and horror; he names the conspirators one by one and calls them **traitors**, so the people's grief turns into **fury**.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** Why have the citizens gathered in the Forum? How do Brutus and Cassius deal with them?
**Answer:** They demand to be "satisfied" — to know why Caesar was killed.
Brutus divides the crowd: some follow Cassius to another street.
The rest stay to hear Brutus, who promises "public reasons" for Caesar's death.

**Q2.** Explain "Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more."
**Answer:** Brutus loved Caesar.
But his love for Rome and its freedom was greater.
When the two clashed, he chose Rome over his friend.

**Q3.** What rhetorical questions does Brutus ask, and why does no one answer?
**Answer:** "Who is here so base that would be a bondman?"; "… so rude that would not be a Roman?"; "… so vile that will not love his country?"
Answering "yes" would mean admitting to being base, rude or vile.
So the crowd replies, "None, Brutus, none."

**Q4.** What promise does Brutus make about his own death? Why is it significant?
**Answer:** He keeps the same dagger for himself, to use when his country needs his death.
It shows his sincerity and patriotism.
It foreshadows his suicide by his own sword at Philippi.

**Q5.** How does the crowd respond to Brutus's speech? What is ironic about it?
**Answer:** They cheer "Live, Brutus!" and want a statue for him.
They cry "Let him be Caesar."
Brutus killed Caesar to stop one-man rule, yet they want to make Brutus a new Caesar.

**Q6.** Why does Antony begin by saying "I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him"? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** The crowd is hostile to Caesar after Brutus's speech.
Antony must also obey Brutus's conditions.
So he pretends not to praise Caesar, though his speech does exactly that — manipulative and deceptive.

**Q7.** State any two conditions Brutus laid down for Antony, and why he laid them down. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Brutus would speak first; Antony would say he spoke by their permission (or: not blame them; speak well of Caesar; same pulpit).
Brutus was cautious and anxious that Antony might sway the crowd.
Cassius had warned him against letting Antony speak.

**Q8.** Give two arguments Antony uses to prove Caesar was not ambitious, and their impact. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** The ransoms of Caesar's captives filled the public treasury.
Caesar wept when the poor cried, and refused the crown thrice at the Lupercal.
The crowd is convinced Caesar was not ambitious and begins to turn against the conspirators.

**Q9.** Explain "My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar, / And I must pause till it come back to me."
**Answer:** Antony is so full of grief that his heart seems buried with Caesar.
He must stop speaking until he recovers.
The pause is also a clever way to watch the crowd's reaction.

**Q10.** How does Antony use Caesar's will before reading it?
**Answer:** He shows it and says he will not read it.
He says the people would kiss Caesar's wounds and treasure his hair if they heard it.
He hints that they are Caesar's heirs, making them demand it.

**Q11.** What does Antony say about Casca's and Brutus's stabs?
**Answer:** "See what a rent the envious Casca made."
"Through this the well-beloved Brutus stabb'd."
Brutus's was "the most unkindest cut of all", as Brutus was Caesar's angel.

**Q12.** What news does Octavius's servant bring? How does Antony react?
**Answer:** Octavius has come to Rome and is at Caesar's house with Lepidus.
Brutus and Cassius have ridden "like madmen" through the gates of Rome.
Antony says "Fortune is merry" and goes at once to Octavius.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** Compare the speeches of Brutus and Antony.
**Answer:** Brutus speaks in prose; Antony in blank verse.
Brutus appeals to reason and his honour; Antony to emotion, pity and self-interest.
Brutus gives no evidence; Antony gives facts and uses props — will, mantle, body.
Brutus wins brief applause; Antony turns the crowd into a mob.

**Q14.** Explain "The evil that men do lives after them; / The good is oft interred with their bones." Apply it to Brutus and Caesar. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** People remember the wrongs a person did.
Their good deeds are forgotten and buried with them.
Brutus is remembered for betraying his friend.
Caesar is remembered for his ambition, his services forgotten.

**Q15.** Which of Antony's arguments do you think had the greatest impact? Give reasons. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** (Personal response.) The will had the greatest impact.
It appealed to self-interest: seventy-five drachmas and public parks.
It proved Caesar's love for the common people.
The crowd cried "Here was a Caesar!" and rushed to burn the traitors' houses.

**Q16.** How does Antony use irony in his speech? Give two examples.
**Answer:** He repeats "Brutus is an honourable man" until the word becomes a sneer.
The crowd shouts "They were traitors: honourable men!"
He says "I am no orator, as Brutus is", though he is the better speaker.
He says he came "not to praise" Caesar, yet praises him throughout.

**Q17.** Show how the crowd is fickle in Act III, Scene 2.
**Answer:** After Brutus's speech they cheer him and want to crown him.
They call Caesar a tyrant.
After Antony's speech they call Brutus a traitor.
They run to burn the houses of the men they had cheered.

**Q18.** What is the significance of the Cinna the poet episode (Scene 3)?
**Answer:** It shows the senseless violence of the mob.
Cinna is killed only for his name — "his name's Cinna".
It is tragic and unnecessary, as the 2027 specimen says.
It is the first result of the "Mischief" and "havoc" Antony unleashed.

**Q19.** Explain "Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt!" What does it reveal about Antony?
**Answer:** Antony addresses Mischief as a person.
He has started a riot and lets it take any course.
It reveals that he is ruthless and calculating.
He uses the people's grief for his own political ends.

**Q20.** Brutus made two mistakes concerning Antony's speech. What were they, and what were the results?

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (16 marks each)

### Extract-based questions (16 marks each)

**Q1.** Read the extract and answer. **Antony:** "But yesterday the word of Caesar might / Have stood against the world; now lies he there, / And none so poor to do him reverence. / O masters, if I were disposed to stir / Your hearts and minds to mutiny and rage, / I should do Brutus wrong, and Cassius wrong, / Who, you all know, are honourable men." (i) Explain "But yesterday … to do him reverence". [3] (ii) What does Antony claim he does not want to do? Why is this ironic? [3] (iii) What does Antony produce next, and what does he say it would make the people do? [3] (iv) How does the crowd react to his refusal to read it? [3] (v) What do these lines show about Antony's method of persuasion? [4]
**Answer:** (i) Only yesterday Caesar's word could have stood against the whole world; today he lies dead, and even the poorest person does not stop to honour him — a contrast between his past power and his present helplessness.
(ii) He claims he does not want to stir the people to mutiny and rage, as that would wrong the "honourable" Brutus and Cassius; it is ironic because stirring them to rage is exactly his aim, and "honourable" is said with sarcasm.
(iii) A parchment with Caesar's seal — his will; if the commons heard it, they would kiss Caesar's wounds, dip their handkerchiefs in his blood, and beg a hair of him to leave to their children as a rich legacy.
(iv) They demand, "The will! read the will!"; when he hints that they are Caesar's heirs and fears he wrongs the "honourable men", they shout "They were traitors: honourable men!" and "villains, murderers".
(v) He uses verbal irony and pretended restraint, contrast, flattery ("O masters"), and reverse psychology — refusing what he wants them to demand; he never openly breaks Brutus's conditions, yet steadily turns the crowd against the conspirators.

**Q2.** Read the extract and answer. **Antony:** "I come not, friends, to steal away your hearts: / I am no orator, as Brutus is; / But, as you know me all, a plain blunt man, / That love my friend; and that they know full well / That gave me public leave to speak of him." (i) What had the crowd just shouted, and why does Antony speak these lines? [3] (ii) Why is "I am no orator, as Brutus is" ironic? [3] (iii) What does Antony say he would do "were I Brutus, / And Brutus Antony"? [3] (iv) What does Antony remind the crowd of next, and what does it contain? [3] (v) How does the crowd react, and what does Antony say once they leave? [4]
**Answer:** (i) They had cried "Revenge! … Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay!" and were ready to riot; he pretends to calm them ("let me not stir you up / To such a sudden flood of mutiny") while actually stirring them further.
(ii) Antony is the greatest orator in the play and has just won over a hostile crowd; his claim of being a "plain blunt man" without wit, words or power of speech is false modesty that makes him seem more honest.
(iii) He would ruffle up their spirits and "put a tongue / In every wound of Caesar" that would move "the stones of Rome to rise and mutiny".
(iv) The will, which they had forgotten: seventy-five drachmas to every citizen, and Caesar's walks, private arbours and new-planted orchards on this side of the Tiber, left to them and their heirs for ever as public parks.
(v) They cry "Never, never", decide to burn Caesar's body in the holy place and fire the traitors' houses, and rush off tearing up benches and windows; Antony says, "Now let it work. Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt!"

**Q3.** Read the extract (Scene 3) and answer. Cinna the poet says he dreamt that he feasted with Caesar, that things unlucky trouble his imagination, and that he has no wish to go out of doors, yet something leads him forth. The citizens surround him and demand his name, where he is going, where he lives, and whether he is married. (i) Who is this Cinna? Why is his dream significant? [3] (ii) What answers does he give to the citizens' questions? [3] (iii) What happens when he gives his name? [3] (iv) Quote or paraphrase the citizen's reply to "I am not Cinna the conspirator". What does it show? [3] (v) Why does Shakespeare include this short scene? [4]
**Answer:** (i) Cinna is a poet and a friend of Caesar, not the conspirator Cinna; his dream of feasting with Caesar is an ill omen, suggesting he will soon join Caesar in death.
(ii) He is going to Caesar's funeral "as a friend"; he lives "by the Capitol"; he says, "Wisely I say, I am a bachelor", which a citizen takes as an insult to married men; and his name is Cinna.
(iii) The mob cries "Tear him to pieces; he's a conspirator"; he protests "I am Cinna the poet", and a citizen shouts, "Tear him for his bad verses."
(iv) "It is no matter, his name's Cinna: pluck but his name out of his heart, and turn him going" — the mob does not care about truth or justice; it is blind, irrational and cruel.
(v) It shows the immediate result of Antony's speech — the "mischief" he set afoot; it shows the mindless violence of a mob; the death is tragic and unnecessary (2027 specimen Q1(v)); and it foreshadows the civil war in which many more innocent people will suffer.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Media literacy)** Antony uses props — the will, the mantle and the body — to move his audience. Give one modern example of how speakers or advertisers use visual "props" to influence people.
**Model answer:** Political speakers often stand beside victims, flags or photographs; advertisers use images of happy families or celebrities. Visual evidence can stir emotion more strongly than reasoned argument.

**Q2. (In Brutus's place)** If you were Brutus, what would you have done differently in the Forum?
**Model answer:** I would have stayed to hear Antony's speech and spoken after him, given clear evidence of Caesar's ambition, and refused to let Antony display the body.

**Q3. (Spot the error)** "Antony's speech openly accused Brutus of murder from the first line." Correct this.
**Model answer:** Antony began cautiously, praising Brutus as "honourable" and claiming he came "not to praise" Caesar; he turned the crowd gradually through irony and evidence.

**Q4. (Crowd psychology)** Why do you think the crowd changed sides so quickly? Give two reasons.
**Model answer:** (1) The crowd was moved by emotion and self-interest, not principle; (2) Antony spoke last, used vivid evidence, and Brutus was not there to answer him.

**Q5. (Values)** What lesson does the death of Cinna the poet teach about mob violence today?
**Model answer:** A mob acting on rumour or a name can harm innocent people; we should check facts and never take the law into our own hands.

## 13. Assertion–Reason Questions

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) A is true, but R is false.
- (b) A is false, but R is true.
- (c) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (d) Both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A.

**Q1.**
**A:** The crowd cried "Let him be Caesar" after Brutus's speech.
**R:** The crowd had fully understood Brutus's republican ideals.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** They had misunderstood him; they wanted a new Caesar.

**Q2.**
**A:** Antony said he came "to bury Caesar, not to praise him".
**R:** Antony had to appear to keep Brutus's conditions before a hostile crowd.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** He began cautiously for this reason.

**Q3.**
**A:** Antony said Caesar was not ambitious.
**R:** Caesar had refused the crown three times at the Lupercal.
**Answer:** (c)
**Explanation:** This was one of his three arguments.

**Q4.**
**A:** Antony read the will at the very start of his speech.
**R:** Antony used the will to make the people eager and to win them over.
**Answer:** (b)

**Q5.**
**A:** Antony called Brutus's blow "the most unkindest cut of all".
**R:** Brutus was Caesar's dearest friend.
**Answer:** (c)

**Q6.**
**A:** Brutus and Cassius fled from Rome.
**R:** Octavius had come to Rome and was at Caesar's house.
**Answer:** (d)

**Q7.**

**Q8.**

## 15. Revision Notes

### Scenes at a glance
- Citizens: "We will be satisfied"; Cassius takes some away.
- Brutus (prose): "Romans, countrymen, and lovers!"; "believe me for mine honour"; "Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more"; slaves or free men; "as he was ambitious, I slew him"; base/rude/vile; "I pause for a reply" — "None, Brutus, none."
- Antony brings the body; Brutus: "the same dagger for myself"; crowd: "Let him be Caesar"; Brutus leaves alone.
- Antony (verse): "Friends, Romans, countrymen"; "bury Caesar, not to praise him"; "The evil that men do lives after them"; "Brutus is an honourable man".
- Arguments: ransoms → general coffers; wept with the poor ("sterner stuff"); crown refused thrice at the Lupercal.
- "My heart is in the coffin there with Caesar" — pause; crowd shifts.
- Will shown, not read; "you are his heirs"; "They were traitors: honourable men!"
- Descends; ring round the corpse; mantle — Nervii; Cassius, envious Casca, well-beloved Brutus; "most unkindest cut"; Pompey's statue ran blood.
- Body shown: "Revenge! … Burn! Fire! Kill! Slay!"; "I am no orator"; "a tongue / In every wound".
- Will read: 75 drachmas; walks, arbours, orchards; "Here was a Caesar!"; mob burns the body and houses.
- "Mischief, thou art afoot"; Octavius and Lepidus at Caesar's house; Brutus and Cassius "rid like madmen".
- Scene 3: Cinna the poet killed for his name.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Brutus spoke in verse | Brutus spoke in prose; Antony in blank verse. |
| Antony read the will at once | He read it only near the end, after the mantle and the body. |
| Antony saw the stabbing | He was drawn away by Trebonius; he only claims to know each cut. |
| Octavius was still seven leagues away | By the end of Scene 2 he has reached Rome, at Caesar's house with Lepidus. |
| Cinna the poet was a conspirator | He was an innocent poet with the same name. |
| Antony openly broke Brutus's conditions | He kept them in words — he never directly blamed them, and called them "honourable". |
