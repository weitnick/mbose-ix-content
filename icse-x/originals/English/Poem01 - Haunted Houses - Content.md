## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

What is a haunted house? In stories, it is a creepy old building with groaning doors, cold draughts and angry spirits. **Longfellow** gives the phrase a new and gentle meaning. **Every house** in which people have **lived and died** is haunted — not by frightening ghosts, but by **"harmless phantoms"**: the memories and lingering presence of all who lived there before us.

The poem moves from the **house** to the **world** and then to the **human soul**. In the first half, the poet describes the quiet spirits who share our homes, and reminds us that we never truly **own** a house or land — the dead still hold their claim. In the second half, he describes a **spirit-world** that surrounds our physical world like an atmosphere, explains our inner struggle between **earthly desires** and **noble aspirations** as the pull of that unseen world, and ends with a beautiful image: a **bridge of moonlight** across the sea, along which our thoughts travel between this world and the world of spirits.

### About the poet

**Henry Wadsworth Longfellow** (1807–1882) was the most popular American poet of the nineteenth century. Born in **Portland, Maine**, he taught modern languages at Bowdoin College and **Harvard University**. His best-known works include *A Psalm of Life*, *The Village Blacksmith*, *The Wreck of the Hesperus*, *Evangeline* (1847), *The Song of Hiawatha* (1855) and *Paul Revere's Ride*; he also translated Dante's *Divine Comedy* into English. He was the first American poet honoured with a bust in **Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey**. Longfellow knew grief well — he lost his first wife young and his second wife in a fire in 1861 — and many of his poems reflect on memory, loss and the life of the spirit. *Haunted Houses* was published in **1858**, in the group of poems called *Birds of Passage* in his collection *The Courtship of Miles Standish and Other Poems*.

### The poem

> All houses wherein men have lived and died
> Are haunted houses. Through the open doors
> The harmless phantoms on their errands glide,
> With feet that make no sound upon the floors.
>
> We meet them at the door-way, on the stair,
> Along the passages they come and go,
> Impalpable impressions on the air,
> A sense of something moving to and fro.
>
> There are more guests at table, than the hosts
> Invited; the illuminated hall
> Is thronged with quiet, inoffensive ghosts,
> As silent as the pictures on the wall.
>
> The stranger at my fireside cannot see
> The forms I see, nor hear the sounds I hear;
> He but perceives what is; while unto me
> All that has been is visible and clear.
>
> We have no title-deeds to house or lands;
> Owners and occupants of earlier dates
> From graves forgotten stretch their dusty hands,
> And hold in mortmain still their old estates.
>
> The spirit-world around this world of sense
> Floats like an atmosphere, and everywhere
> Wafts through these earthly mists and vapors dense
> A vital breath of more ethereal air.
>
> Our little lives are kept in equipoise
> By opposite attractions and desires;
> The struggle of the instinct that enjoys,
> And the more noble instinct that aspires.
>
> These perturbations, this perpetual jar
> Of earthly wants and aspirations high,
> Come from the influence of an unseen star,
> An undiscovered planet in our sky.
>
> And as the moon from some dark gate of cloud
> Throws o'er the sea a floating bridge of light,
> Across whose trembling planks our fancies crowd
> Into the realm of mystery and night,—
>
> So from the world of spirits there descends
> A bridge of light, connecting it with this,
> O'er whose unsteady floor, that sways and bends,
> Wander our thoughts above the dark abyss.

### The poem explained, stanza by stanza

#### Stanza 1 — Every lived-in house is haunted

Every house in which people have **lived and died** is a haunted house. Through its open doors the **"harmless phantoms"** (ghosts) **glide** about on their errands, their feet making **no sound** on the floors. *(2025 specimen Q1(ix): the phantoms' movement is described by the word "glide"; Q6(ii): what turns a house into a haunted house.)*

The phantoms are **harmless** — the poet removes all fear from the idea of ghosts at once. They are busy "on their errands", as if continuing the ordinary household lives they once lived.

#### Stanza 2 — Where we meet them

We meet these phantoms **at the doorway**, **on the stairs**, and **along the passages**, where they come and go. They are **"impalpable impressions on the air"** — they cannot be touched; we feel them only as **a sense of something moving to and fro**. *(2025 specimen Q6(iii).)*

The ghosts are not seen with the eyes; they are felt as a presence — like a memory that suddenly comes back as we pass a familiar place.

#### Stanza 3 — Uninvited guests

At a dinner there are **more guests at the table than the hosts invited**. The brightly lit **hall** is **crowded ("thronged")** with **quiet, inoffensive ghosts**, as **silent as the pictures on the wall**.

The family portraits on the walls are a clue: the people in those pictures once sat at this table. Their spirits still gather at family meals and celebrations, silently sharing the occasion.

#### Stanza 4 — The speaker and the stranger

A **stranger** sitting by the speaker's **fireside** cannot see the forms the speaker sees, or hear the sounds he hears. The stranger perceives only **"what is"** — the **present**. But to the speaker, **"all that has been"** — the **past** — is **visible and clear**. *(2025 specimen Q6 extract and Q6(i); 2027 specimen Q1(vii): the speaker sees both the present and the past.)*

The difference is one of **memory and connection**. The speaker knows the history of the house and its people; for him, the past is alive in every room. The stranger, with no such memories, sees only furniture and walls.

#### Stanza 5 — Nobody truly owns a house

We have **no "title-deeds"** (legal proof of ownership) that truly make a house or land ours. The **owners and occupants of earlier times** stretch their **"dusty hands"** from their **forgotten graves** and still **hold their old estates "in mortmain"**. *(2025 specimen Q6(iv).)*

**Mortmain** (literally "dead hand") is a legal term for property held for ever by an institution that never dies, so that it can never be sold or passed on. Longfellow uses it as a **metaphor**: the dead keep their grip on the places they lived in. Our ownership is **temporary**; we are only the latest occupants in a long line.

#### Stanza 6 — The spirit-world around us

The **spirit-world** surrounds **"this world of sense"** (the physical world we know through our senses) like an **atmosphere**. Everywhere, through the **thick mists and vapours** of earthly life, it sends **"a vital breath of more ethereal air"** — a life-giving breath of a finer, heavenly kind.

The poem now widens from one house to the whole world: the spiritual is not far away; it is all around us, mingled with ordinary life.

#### Stanza 7 — Our divided lives

Our **"little lives"** are kept in **"equipoise"** (balance) by **opposite attractions and desires**: the struggle between **"the instinct that enjoys"** (the wish for earthly pleasure and comfort) and **"the more noble instinct that aspires"** (the wish to rise higher — towards goodness, truth and the spiritual).

#### Stanza 8 — The unseen star

These **"perturbations"** (disturbances), this **"perpetual jar"** (constant conflict) between **earthly wants** and **high aspirations**, come from the influence of **"an unseen star"**, **"an undiscovered planet in our sky"**.

Just as astronomers can detect an undiscovered planet by its pull on the orbits of other planets, we can sense the **spirit-world** by its pull on our souls. (The planet Neptune had been discovered in 1846 in exactly this way, through its effect on Uranus — an idea Longfellow's readers would have known.)

#### Stanzas 9 and 10 — The bridge of light

The poem ends with one long **extended simile**. Just as the **moon**, coming out from **"some dark gate of cloud"**, throws a **"floating bridge of light"** across the sea — a shining path over which our **"fancies"** (imaginations) crowd into the **"realm of mystery and night"** —

so, from the **world of spirits**, a **bridge of light** comes down and **connects** that world with ours. Over its **"unsteady floor, that sways and bends"**, our **thoughts wander** above the **"dark abyss"** (the deep unknown of death and eternity).

The bridge is **unsteady** — our understanding of the spirit-world is uncertain — but it is made of **light**: hope, memory, imagination and faith connect us to those who have gone before.

### Conclusion: the poet's message

- Our homes are full of the **presence of the past** — the people who lived and died there.
- We are only **temporary owners**; the dead still have a claim on the places they loved.
- A **spiritual world** surrounds and influences our physical lives.
- Human life is a struggle between **earthly desires** and **noble aspirations**.
- Memory and imagination form a **bridge of light** between the living and the dead.
- The mood is not fear but **calm, comfort and wonder**.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Stanzas 1–2: the harmless phantoms

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**All houses wherein men have lived and died / Are haunted houses**" — a surprising, universal definition: not some houses, but **all** lived-in houses. *(2025 specimen Q6(ii): houses where people have lived and died and are revisited by the spirits of those who have passed on.)*
  - "**The harmless phantoms on their errands glide**" — *phantoms* = ghosts; *glide* = move smoothly and silently. *(2025 specimen Q1(ix))*
  - "**With feet that make no sound upon the floors**" — silence; no footsteps, no terror.
  - "**at the door-way, on the stair, / Along the passages**" — places of passing through. *(2025 specimen Q6(iii): doorways, stairs, passageways, halls.)*
  - "**Impalpable impressions on the air**" — *impalpable* = cannot be touched; the ghosts are felt, not seen. Alliteration of *i* and *p* sounds.
  - "**A sense of something moving to and fro**" — vague, gentle presence.

### Section 2 — Stanza 3: the crowded table

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**There are more guests at table, than the hosts / Invited**" — the spirits of past family members join the meal.
  - "**the illuminated hall / Is thronged with quiet, inoffensive ghosts**" — *thronged* = crowded; *inoffensive* = harmless. The bright hall contrasts with the dark, traditional haunted house.
  - "**As silent as the pictures on the wall**" — **simile**; the portraits of ancestors hint at who the ghosts are.

### Section 3 — Stanza 4: the speaker and the stranger

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The stranger at my fireside cannot see / The forms I see, nor hear the sounds I hear**" — the visitor lacks the speaker's memories.
  - "**He but perceives what is; while unto me / All that has been is visible and clear**" — *what is* = the present; *all that has been* = the past. *(2025 specimen Q6(i): he can see and hear what the stranger cannot; the stranger perceives "what is" (the present), the narrator all that "has been" (the past). 2027 specimen Q1(vii): the speaker sees both the present and the past.)*
  - The **fireside** suggests home, warmth and reflection.

### Section 4 — Stanza 5: no title-deeds

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**We have no title-deeds to house or lands**" — no real, permanent ownership.
  - "**Owners and occupants of earlier dates / From graves forgotten stretch their dusty hands**" — the dead reach out from forgotten graves; personification and a vivid image.
  - "**And hold in mortmain still their old estates**" — *mortmain* = "dead hand", permanent ownership. *(2025 specimen Q6(iv): the living cannot claim ownership of the houses and lands they occupy; previous owners and occupants from long ago, now dead, reach out from their graves to stake a claim.)*
- **Note:** this image is the closest the poem comes to a "spooky" picture, yet its point is philosophical: possession is temporary.

### Section 5 — Stanzas 6–8: the spirit-world and the human soul

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**The spirit-world around this world of sense / Floats like an atmosphere**" — **simile**; the spiritual surrounds the physical.
  - "**A vital breath of more ethereal air**" — *vital* = life-giving; *ethereal* = heavenly, delicate.
  - "**Our little lives are kept in equipoise / By opposite attractions and desires**" — *equipoise* = balance between opposing forces.
  - "**the instinct that enjoys, / And the more noble instinct that aspires**" — earthly pleasure versus spiritual aspiration.
  - "**These perturbations, this perpetual jar**" — *perturbations* = disturbances; *jar* = clash; alliteration of *p*.
  - "**an unseen star, / An undiscovered planet in our sky**" — **metaphor** for the spirit-world: invisible, but known by its influence.

### Section 6 — Stanzas 9–10: the bridge of light

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**as the moon from some dark gate of cloud / Throws o'er the sea a floating bridge of light**" — the shining path of moonlight on water.
  - "**Across whose trembling planks our fancies crowd / Into the realm of mystery and night**" — our imaginations travel along it into the unknown.
  - "**So from the world of spirits there descends / A bridge of light, connecting it with this**" — the second half of the **extended simile**: a spiritual bridge joins the two worlds.
  - "**O'er whose unsteady floor, that sways and bends, / Wander our thoughts above the dark abyss**" — the bridge is uncertain, but our thoughts can cross it; the *abyss* is the mystery of death.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- The idea of an "undiscovered planet" detected by its pull was very real: **Neptune** was found in 1846 after astronomers noticed irregularities in the orbit of Uranus.
- **Mortmain** laws in England were passed from the Middle Ages onwards to stop land passing permanently to churches and corporations, which "never died".
- Many cultures, including Indian ones, honour ancestors at home — through photographs, lamps, prayers and remembrance days — a feeling close to Longfellow's.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **The presence of the past:** the dead live on in the places they knew.
- **Memory:** the speaker's memories make the past "visible and clear".
- **Impermanence of ownership:** no one really owns a house; we are temporary occupants.
- **The spiritual and the material:** a spirit-world surrounds and influences our physical lives.
- **Human nature:** a lifelong struggle between pleasure and noble aspiration.
- **Connection between the living and the dead:** a bridge of light links the two worlds.

### 7.1 Tone and mood

| Aspect | Description |
|---|---|
| tone | calm, reflective, philosophical, gently mystical |
| mood | **comfort and security**; wonder rather than fear |
| reason (any one) | the ghosts are "harmless" and "inoffensive"; they are like silent family portraits; the bridge of light suggests hope and connection |

*(2025 specimen Q6(v): mood — comfort and security; accept any one plausible reason.)*

### 7.2 Significance of the title

"Haunted Houses" normally suggests horror. Longfellow uses the familiar phrase to **surprise** the reader: **all** our houses are haunted — by memories and gentle spirits. The title thus changes the meaning of "haunted" from **frightening** to **familiar and comforting**.

### 7.3 Comparing poems in the syllabus

| Point | *Haunted Houses* | *When Great Trees Fall* |
|---|---|---|
| subject | the presence of the dead in our homes | the effect of a great person's death |
| attitude to death | the dead remain near us, harmlessly | grief, then healing and hope |
| closing note | a bridge of light between worlds | "we can be … better. For they existed." |
| shared idea | the dead continue to influence the living | |

**Solutions — MCQs**

**2025 specimen Q1(ix).** **(b) glide.** "The harmless phantoms on their errands glide."

**2025 specimen Q6.**

**(i) What makes the poet-narrator different from the stranger at his fireside?** [3]
The narrator **can see and hear** what the stranger **cannot see or hear** — the forms and sounds of the past. The stranger perceives only **"what is"** (the **present**), while the narrator perceives **all that "has been"** (the **past**).

**(ii) What, according to the poet, turns a house into a 'haunted' house?** [3]
A house becomes haunted when **people have lived** in it and **died**, and it is **revisited by the spirits** of those who have passed on.

**(iii) Where is one likely to meet the 'phantoms' in a haunted house?** [3]
In the **doorways**, on the **stairs**, along the **passageways**, and in the **halls** (even at the dinner table).

**(iv) What are the poet-narrator's views on owning property?** [3]
The living **cannot claim ownership** of the houses and lands they occupy — we have "no title-deeds". The **previous owners and occupants** from long ago, **now dead**, **reach out from their graves** ("stretch their dusty hands") to **stake a claim**, holding their old estates "in mortmain".

**(v) How do the poet's views of ghosts differ from the traditional perception of ghosts? How would you describe the mood that the poem evokes? Give ONE reason for your answer.** [4]
**Traditional** ghosts are **frightening, vengeful and malevolent**. The **poet's** ghosts are **comfortable, secure and benign** — "harmless phantoms", "quiet, inoffensive ghosts". The **mood** is one of **comfort and security**. **Reason (any one):** the ghosts glide silently "on their errands", like members of the household, and sit at table "as silent as the pictures on the wall" — familiar, not frightening.

**2026 Q1(xii).** **(d) 'She sways like a flower in the wind of our song;'** Both are **similes** ("like an atmosphere", "like a flower"). (Option (a) is an oxymoron, (b) a metaphor, (c) hyperbole.)

**2026 Improvement Q1(xii).** **(b) 'Of the forest's ferny floor'.** Both use **alliteration** (*p* — perturbations, perpetual; *f* — forest's, ferny, floor). (Option (a) is personification, (d) a simile.)

**2026 Improvement Q6.**

**(i) How are the ghosts described? How does this differ from traditional ghosts?** [3]
They are **quiet, inoffensive (harmless)** ghosts, **as silent as the pictures on the wall**; elsewhere they **glide** harmlessly on their errands. Traditional ghosts are **frightening** — they wail, clank chains, haunt and harm people. Longfellow's ghosts are **gentle presences of the past**: the memories of those who once lived in the house.

**(ii) The image in 'There are more guests at table than the hosts invited'. Why can't strangers see these ghosts?** [3]
A brightly lit dining hall with a **dinner party**, where, besides the invited guests, the **unseen spirits of former dwellers** also sit at the table among the living. The stranger at the fireside **perceives only "what is"** — the present; he has **no memories or associations** with the house. To the poet, who knows its past, **all that has been is visible and clear**.

**(iii) What is the spirit world compared to? What connects the two worlds? How is the bridge described?** [3]
The spirit world **"floats like an atmosphere"** around our world of the senses. A **bridge of light** descends from the world of spirits and connects it with ours, just as **the moon, through a dark gate of cloud, throws a floating bridge of light across the sea**. The bridge has an **unsteady floor that sways and bends** ("trembling planks"), over which our **thoughts and fancies** wander above the dark abyss.

**(iv) Explain 'mortmain'. Who holds the estates in mortmain?** [3]
"Mortmain" (literally "**dead hand**") is the **permanent, inalienable ownership of property**, held as if by a dead hand that never lets go. The **owners and occupants of earlier dates** — the dead — stretch their dusty hands **from forgotten graves** and **still hold their old estates** in mortmain; we who live there now have **no real title-deeds**.

**(v) The two opposing instincts that must be balanced; what influences their conflict.** [4]
Our lives are kept in **equipoise (balance)** between **the instinct that enjoys** — the desire for earthly comforts and pleasures — and **the more noble instinct that aspires** to higher, spiritual things. This struggle, this "**perpetual jar**" of **earthly wants and high aspirations**, comes from the influence of **"that unseen star, / That undiscovered planet in our sky"** — the **unseen spirit world** around us.

**2025 Improvement Q1(vii).** **(c) 'Is thronged with quiet inoffensive ghosts'** — *inoffensive* means harmless.

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> All houses wherein men have lived and died
> Are haunted houses. Through the open doors
> The harmless phantoms on their errands glide,
> With feet that make no sound upon the floors.

**(i)** According to the poet, which houses are haunted? [3]
**Answer:** **All** houses in which people have **lived and died** — not just a few old, ruined ones; every home holds the presence of those who lived there before.

**(ii)** How are the phantoms described? What is unusual about this description? [3]
**Answer:** They are **"harmless"**; they **glide** on their **errands** through the open doors, with **feet that make no sound**. It is unusual because ghosts are traditionally **frightening**; here they are gentle and busy, like members of the household.

**(iii)** Where else does the speaker say we meet them? [3]
**Answer:** **At the doorway, on the stair, along the passages**, and in the **illuminated hall** at the table, where they are "more guests … than the hosts invited".

**(iv)** Explain "Impalpable impressions on the air, / A sense of something moving to and fro". [3]
**Answer:** The ghosts **cannot be touched or seen**; we only **feel** them faintly, as a vague sense of **something moving back and forth** — like a passing memory.

**(v)** What is the rhyme scheme of the stanza? Name one poetic device in it and comment on its effect. [4]
**Answer:** **ABAB** (died/glide; doors/floors). **Device (any one):** **personification** — the phantoms have "errands" and "feet", which makes them seem like ordinary household members and removes fear; or **alliteration** in "harmless … have", "feet … floors", which gives the lines a soft, flowing sound matching the gliding movement.

**Extract B.**
> And as the moon from some dark gate of cloud
> Throws o'er the sea a floating bridge of light,
> Across whose trembling planks our fancies crowd
> Into the realm of mystery and night,—

**(i)** Describe the picture in these lines. [3]
**Answer:** The **moon** comes out from behind a **dark cloud** and throws a **shining path of light** across the sea — a "floating bridge" whose "planks" seem to **tremble** with the moving waves.

**(ii)** What do "our fancies" do? [3]
**Answer:** Our **imaginations crowd across** this bridge of moonlight into the **"realm of mystery and night"** — into the unknown, the world of dreams and wonder.

**(iii)** These lines begin a comparison that is completed in the next stanza. What is compared? [3]
**Answer:** The **moonlight bridge** over the sea is compared to a **bridge of light** that descends from the **world of spirits**, **connecting it with this world**; over its unsteady floor **our thoughts wander** above the dark abyss. (An **extended simile**: "as … so".)

**(iv)** What is the "dark abyss"? Why is the bridge "unsteady"? [3]
**Answer:** The **dark abyss** is the deep **mystery of death** and what lies beyond. The bridge is **unsteady** because our knowledge of the spirit-world is **uncertain** — we cross it only by thought, imagination and faith.

**(v)** How does this ending complete the poem's ideas? [4]
**Answer:** The poem began with the gentle ghosts in our houses and widened to the spirit-world that surrounds and influences us; the ending shows the **connection** between the two worlds as a **bridge of light** — hopeful, beautiful and open to our thoughts. It replaces fear of death with **wonder and comfort**, confirming the calm, reassuring mood of the whole poem.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** What, according to the poet, turns a house into a 'haunted' house? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** People have lived in it.
People have died in it.
It is revisited by the spirits of those who have passed on.

**Q2.** Where is one likely to meet the phantoms? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** At the doorways.
On the stairs and along the passageways.
In the halls — even at the dinner table.

**Q3.** What makes the poet-narrator different from the stranger at his fireside? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** He can see and hear what the stranger cannot.
The stranger perceives only "what is" — the present.
The narrator perceives all that "has been" — the past.

**Q4.** What are the poet-narrator's views on owning property? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** The living cannot truly claim ownership — "no title-deeds".
Former owners and occupants, now dead, reach out from their graves.
They still stake a claim, holding their estates "in mortmain".

**Q5.** How are the phantoms described in the first two stanzas?
**Answer:** Harmless; they glide on their errands through open doors.
Their feet make no sound on the floors.
They are impalpable — felt only as something moving to and fro.

**Q6.** Explain "There are more guests at table, than the hosts / Invited".
**Answer:** At meals, the spirits of earlier occupants join the living.
They are uninvited, quiet and inoffensive.
They are "as silent as the pictures on the wall".

**Q7.** Explain "hold in mortmain still their old estates".
**Answer:** Mortmain means "dead hand" — permanent ownership.
The dead seem still to grip the houses and lands they owned.
Our ownership is only temporary.

**Q8.** What does the poet say about the spirit-world in stanza 6?
**Answer:** It surrounds the physical world like an atmosphere.
It is everywhere.
It sends a life-giving breath of finer, heavenly air through earthly mists.

**Q9.** What are the two opposite instincts in stanza 7?
**Answer:** The instinct that enjoys — earthly pleasure and comfort.
The more noble instinct that aspires — towards higher, spiritual things.
Together they keep our lives in balance ("equipoise").

**Q10.** Explain "an unseen star, / An undiscovered planet in our sky".
**Answer:** It is a metaphor for the spirit-world.
We cannot see it, but we feel its pull.
Its influence causes the struggle between earthly wants and high aspirations.

**Q11.** Describe the moonlight image in stanza 9.
**Answer:** The moon comes out from a dark gate of cloud.
It throws a floating bridge of light across the sea.
Our fancies crowd across its trembling planks into the realm of mystery and night.

**Q12.** What is the rhyme scheme and metre of the poem?
**Answer:** Ten quatrains, each rhyming ABAB.
Written in iambic pentameter.
The regular form gives a calm, steady, meditative rhythm.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** How do the poet's views of ghosts differ from the traditional view? What mood does the poem evoke? *(2025 specimen)*
**Answer:** Traditional ghosts are frightening, vengeful, malevolent.
The poet's ghosts are comfortable, secure, benign — "harmless phantoms".
The mood is comfort and security.
Reason: they glide on errands and sit at table silent as family portraits.

**Q14.** Explain the extended simile in the last two stanzas.
**Answer:** As the moon throws a bridge of light across the sea, along which our imaginations travel,
so a bridge of light descends from the spirit-world to ours.
Our thoughts wander over its unsteady floor above the dark abyss.
It suggests hope and connection between the living and the dead.

**Q15.** Trace the development of ideas in the poem.
**Answer:** Stanzas 1–3: gentle ghosts in every home.
Stanza 4: the speaker sees the past; the stranger only the present.
Stanza 5: we do not truly own our homes.
Stanzas 6–10: the spirit-world around us, its pull on our souls, and the bridge of light.

**Q16.** Discuss the significance of the title.
**Answer:** "Haunted houses" usually suggests horror.
Longfellow says all lived-in houses are haunted.
The ghosts are memories and gentle spirits.
The title changes "haunted" from frightening to comforting.

**Q17.** What role does memory play in the poem?
**Answer:** The speaker sees "all that has been" because he remembers.
The stranger, without memories, sees only the present.
Family portraits and the uninvited guests at table suggest remembered ancestors.
Memory forms part of the bridge between the living and the dead.

**Q18.** Identify two poetic devices in the poem with examples and comment on their effect.
**Answer:** Simile: "As silent as the pictures on the wall" — gentle, familiar ghosts.
Metaphor: "hold in mortmain" — the dead's lasting claim.
Alliteration: "These perturbations, this perpetual jar" — the restless conflict.
Personification: "dusty hands" stretched from graves — makes the claim vivid.

**Q19.** What does the poem say about human nature?
**Answer:** Our lives are balanced between opposite desires.
The instinct that enjoys pulls us to earthly pleasure.
The nobler instinct aspires to higher things.
This constant conflict comes from the unseen influence of the spirit-world.

**Q20.** Is the poem frightening? Give your view with reasons.
**Answer:** (Personal response.) Mostly not.
The ghosts are harmless, quiet and inoffensive.
The only eerie image — dusty hands from graves — makes a point about ownership.
The ending's bridge of light leaves a mood of hope and wonder.

(ii) People who owned and lived in the house or land in former times and are now dead, in graves now forgotten.

(iv) Personification — "stretch their dusty hands"; metaphor — "mortmain"; alliteration — "dusty … dates"; rhyme ABAB (lands/hands, dates/estates).
(v) Personal response, e.g., yes: we are only temporary caretakers; houses outlive us and hold the memories of many generations; this should make us humble and respectful of the past.

**Q3.** Read the extract and answer. **Answer:** (i) Held in balance — our lives are steadied by two opposing pulls.
(ii) The instinct that enjoys (earthly pleasure and comfort) and the nobler instinct that aspires (towards higher, spiritual goals).
(iii) The influence of "an unseen star, / An undiscovered planet in our sky" — the spirit-world, felt by its pull though never seen.
(iv) Like the air that surrounds the earth, the spirit-world surrounds our physical world everywhere, breathing a "vital breath of more ethereal air" through earthly mists.
(v) The poem moves from ghosts in particular houses to the spiritual world around all of us and its effect on every human soul — from the house to the universe, from the past to human nature.

**Q4.** Read the extract and answer. **Answer:** (i) The moon, from a dark gate of cloud, throws a floating bridge of light over the sea, across whose trembling planks our fancies crowd into the realm of mystery and night.
(ii) The world of spirits and this physical world — the dead and the living.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Poem at a glance
- **St 1:** all houses where people **lived and died** are haunted; **harmless phantoms** **glide** on their **errands**; soundless feet.
- **St 2:** met at the **doorway**, **stair**, **passages**; **impalpable impressions**; something moving **to and fro**.
- **St 3:** **more guests at table** than invited; **illuminated hall thronged** with **quiet, inoffensive ghosts**, **silent as the pictures on the wall**.
- **St 4:** the **stranger at my fireside** sees only **what is**; to me **all that has been** is visible and clear.
- **St 5:** **no title-deeds**; dead owners **stretch their dusty hands** from forgotten graves; **mortmain**.
- **St 6:** the **spirit-world** floats **like an atmosphere**; **vital breath of more ethereal air**.
- **St 7:** lives in **equipoise** — the instinct that **enjoys** vs the instinct that **aspires**.
- **St 8:** **perturbations**, **perpetual jar** — from **an unseen star, an undiscovered planet**.
- **St 9–10:** moon's **floating bridge of light** over the sea → a **bridge of light** from the spirit-world; **unsteady floor**; thoughts above the **dark abyss**.
- **Form:** 10 quatrains; **ABAB**; iambic pentameter. **Mood:** comfort and security.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| The phantoms "slide" | They **glide** (2025 specimen). |
| The stranger sees the spirits | Only the speaker sees the past (2027 specimen). |
| Only old, ruined houses are haunted | All houses where people lived and died. |
| The ghosts are vengeful | They are harmless, inoffensive, benign. |
| Mortmain means "main road" | It means "dead hand" — permanent ownership. |
| The bridge is steady | Its floor "sways and bends". |
