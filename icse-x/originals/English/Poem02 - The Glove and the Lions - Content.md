## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*The Glove and the Lions* is a short, lively **narrative poem** by the English poet **Leigh Hunt**. It retells an old story from the court of **King Francis I of France**. The King and his court are watching a fierce fight between **lions** in a pit. Among the spectators is a brave knight, **Count de Lorge**, sitting beside the lady he loves. To show off before the court and to **test** his love, she **drops her glove** among the lions. De Lorge leaps into the pit, picks up the glove, comes back — and **throws the glove in her face**. The King approves: such a test, he says, comes not from **love** but from **vanity**.

The poem is a **sharp attack on vanity** and on a false idea of love that values **show and glory** above a loved one's life. In just twenty-four lines it gives us a vivid spectacle, a scheming character, a dramatic action and a crisp moral.

### About the poet

**James Henry Leigh Hunt** (1784–1859) was an English poet, essayist, critic and editor. With his brother he ran the newspaper ***The Examiner***, and in 1813 he was **imprisoned for two years** for criticising the Prince Regent — he famously decorated his prison room with flowers and wallpaper and kept on writing. He was a friend and early supporter of **John Keats** and **Percy Bysshe Shelley**. His best-known poems include *Abou Ben Adhem* and *Jenny Kiss'd Me*. *The Glove and the Lions* was published in **1836** in *The New Monthly Magazine*. The same old story was also told by the German poet Friedrich Schiller (*Der Handschuh*, 1797) and later by Robert Browning (*The Glove*, 1845).

### The poem

> King Francis was a hearty king, and loved a royal sport,
> And one day as his lions fought, sat looking on the court;
> The nobles filled the benches, with the ladies in their pride,
> And 'mong them sat the Count de Lorge, with one for whom he sighed:
> And truly 'twas a gallant thing to see that crowning show,
> Valour and love, and a king above, and the royal beasts below.
>
> Ramped and roared the lions, with horrid laughing jaws;
> They bit, they glared, gave blows like beams, a wind went with their paws;
> With wallowing might and stifled roar they rolled on one another,
> Till all the pit with sand and mane was in a thunderous smother;
> The bloody foam above the bars came whisking through the air;
> Said Francis then, "Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there."
>
> De Lorge's love o'erheard the King, a beauteous lively dame
> With smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same;
> She thought, "The Count, my lover, is brave as brave can be;
> He surely would do wondrous things to show his love of me;
> King, ladies, lovers, all look on; the occasion is divine;
> I'll drop my glove, to prove his love; great glory will be mine."
>
> She dropped her glove, to prove his love, then looked at him and smiled;
> He bowed, and in a moment leaped among the lions wild:
> His leap was quick, return was quick, he has regained his place,
> Then threw the glove, but not with love, right in the lady's face.
> "By God!" said Francis, "rightly done!" and he rose from where he sat:
> "No love," quoth he, "but vanity, sets love a task like that."

### The poem explained, part by part

#### Part 1 (lines 1–6) — The royal spectacle

**King Francis** was a **"hearty"** (jovial, full-blooded) king who loved a **"royal sport"** — watching his **lions fight**. One day, as they fought, he sat watching with his **court**. The **nobles** filled the benches, with the **ladies** sitting proudly among them, and among them sat **Count de Lorge** with the lady he loved ("one for whom he sighed"). *(2027 specimen Q6(i): King Francis, nobles and ladies seated together, watching the lions fight in the pit; Count de Lorge sighing for his beloved.)*

It was truly a **"gallant"** (splendid, noble) sight — a **"crowning show"** of all that a court admired: **"Valour and love, and a king above, and the royal beasts below."** The line neatly arranges the scene from top to bottom: the King high on his seat, the knights' valour and the ladies' love around him, and the "royal beasts" (lions, kings of animals) in the pit below. *(2027 specimen Q6 extract.)*

#### Part 2 (lines 7–12) — The lion fight

The poet now plunges us into the fight with powerful **sound and visual images**:

- The lions **"ramped and roared"** (reared up and roared), with **"horrid laughing jaws"** — their open jaws look like a terrible grin.
- They **bit**, they **glared**, they struck **"blows like beams"** (heavy wooden beams); **"a wind went with their paws"** — their swipes were so powerful they stirred the air.
- With **"wallowing might and stifled roar"** they **rolled on one another**, till the whole pit was a **"thunderous smother"** of sand and mane — a choking cloud of dust and fur.
- **"The bloody foam"** from their mouths flew **"above the bars"**, **whisking through the air** towards the spectators.

*(2027 specimen Q6(ii): visual images — laughing jaws, glared, blows like beams, rolled on one another, bloody foam whisked through the air; sound images — roared, stifled roar, thunderous smother.)*

Seeing the bloody foam fly past, the King remarks to his nobles: **"Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there"** — we are lucky to be up here and not down in the pit. It is a humorous, relieved comment; it also shows how dangerous the pit is.

#### Part 3 (lines 13–18) — The lady's plan

De Lorge's beloved **overhears** the King's remark. She is a **"beauteous lively dame"** (a beautiful, high-spirited lady) **"with smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same"**. Her smile never changes and her eyes are always sharp: the description hints that her smile is **fixed and artificial**, and her eyes **shrewd and calculating**. *(2027 specimen Q6(v).)*

She thinks: the Count, her lover, is **as brave as brave can be**; surely he would do **wonderful things** to show his love for her. The King, the ladies, the lovers — everyone is watching; **"the occasion is divine"** (the perfect opportunity). So she decides: **"I'll drop my glove, to prove his love; great glory will be mine."**

Her motive is revealed in her own words: not concern for de Lorge, but **"great glory"** for herself. She wants to **test his love** and to win **attention and admiration** before the whole court — **vanity**. The King's remark ("better here than there") gives her the idea: what could prove love better than going "there"? *(2027 specimen Q6(iii).)*

#### Part 4 (lines 19–24) — De Lorge's answer and the King's verdict

She **drops her glove** into the lions' pit, then **looks at him and smiles**. De Lorge **bows** — politely, without a word — and **in a moment leaps among the wild lions**. His leap is quick, his return is quick, and he is **back in his place**. Then he **throws the glove — "but not with love" — right in the lady's face**.

King Francis rises from his seat and exclaims, **"rightly done!"** He gives his verdict: **"No love … but vanity, sets love a task like that."** A person who truly loved would never send the beloved into deadly danger merely for show; only **vanity** sets such a task. *(2027 specimen Q6(iv).)*

De Lorge's action shows that he has **seen through** the lady: he proves his **courage**, but rejects her **cruel vanity**. By throwing the glove in her face before the whole court, he **shames** her — publicly, as she had tried to win glory publicly.

### Conclusion: the poet's message

- **True love** cares for the beloved's safety; it does not demand dangerous proofs.
- **Vanity** — the desire for glory and admiration — can make people cruel and selfish.
- **Courage** and **dignity** go together: de Lorge does the brave deed but refuses to be used.
- Outward charm ("smiling lips and sharp bright eyes") can hide a calculating heart.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — Lines 1–6: the court at the lion fight

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**King Francis was a hearty king, and loved a royal sport**" — *hearty* = jovial, vigorous; *royal sport* = a lion fight, entertainment fit for a king.
  - "**The nobles filled the benches, with the ladies in their pride**" — the whole court, dressed in splendour.
  - "**the Count de Lorge, with one for whom he sighed**" — the lady he loved and longed for.
  - "**'twas a gallant thing to see that crowning show**" — *gallant* = splendid; *crowning show* = the finest spectacle.
  - "**Valour and love, and a king above, and the royal beasts below**" — **internal rhyme** (*love / above*); a neat ordering of the scene from top to bottom; lions are "royal beasts" as "kings of the jungle".
- **Exam focus:** 2027 specimen Q6(i).

### Section 2 — Lines 7–12: the fight

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**Ramped and roared the lions**" — **alliteration** and **inversion**; *ramped* = reared up on the hind legs. *(2025 specimen Q1(vii))*
  - "**with horrid laughing jaws**" — the open jaws look like a terrible grin; **personification**.
  - "**gave blows like beams**" — **simile**; the paws strike like heavy wooden beams.
  - "**a wind went with their paws**" — **hyperbole**; so powerful the air moves.
  - "**With wallowing might and stifled roar they rolled on one another**" — *wallowing* = rolling heavily; *stifled* = muffled.
  - "**Till all the pit with sand and mane was in a thunderous smother**" — a choking cloud of sand and fur; **sound image**.
  - "**The bloody foam above the bars came whisking through the air**" — vivid **visual image**; *whisking* suggests speed and sound.
  - "**Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there.**" — *Faith* = truly (a mild oath); the King's light-hearted relief.
- **Exam focus:** 2027 specimen Q6(ii).

### Section 3 — Lines 13–18: the lady's scheme

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**De Lorge's love o'erheard the King**" — she heard his remark about "there".
  - "**a beauteous lively dame**" — beautiful and spirited (2027 specimen Q6(iii): de Lorge's beloved).
  - "**With smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same**" — a smile that never changes (artificial, fake, expressionless) and eyes that are shrewd and cunning. *(2027 specimen Q6(v))*
  - "**The Count, my lover, is brave as brave can be**" — she counts on his courage.
  - "**King, ladies, lovers, all look on; the occasion is divine**" — she sees a perfect stage for display.
  - "**I'll drop my glove, to prove his love; great glory will be mine.**" — **internal rhyme** (*glove / love*); her real aim is **her own glory** — vanity.

### Section 4 — Lines 19–24: the glove, the leap and the verdict

- **Key lines explained:**
  - "**She dropped her glove, to prove his love, then looked at him and smiled**" — her action, and her confident smile.
  - "**He bowed, and in a moment leaped among the lions wild**" — instant, silent courage; the bow is polite and perhaps ironic.
  - "**His leap was quick, return was quick, he has regained his place**" — repetition and quick rhythm; the sudden shift to the present tense ("he has regained") makes the moment immediate.
  - "**Then threw the glove, but not with love, right in the lady's face.**" — the climax; internal rhyme (*glove / love*) now used ironically. *(2027 specimen Q6(iv))*
  - "**rightly done!**" — the King approves de Lorge's action.
  - "**No love … but vanity, sets love a task like that.**" — the moral: true love would not endanger the beloved for show.

### 5.4 Beyond the text (general knowledge, not needed for exam answers)

- **Francis I** reigned as King of France from 1515 to 1547; he was a great patron of art and invited Leonardo da Vinci to France.
- Royal courts in Europe kept **menageries** (collections of wild animals), and animal fights were sometimes staged as entertainment — a practice now rightly seen as cruel.
- In medieval and Renaissance **chivalry**, a lady's glove or favour was a token a knight might carry; dropping a glove could also be a challenge.

## 7. Themes / Messages

- **Vanity versus true love:** true love protects; vanity demands proofs for its own glory.
- **Courage and dignity:** de Lorge is brave but will not be humiliated or used.
- **Appearance and reality:** the lady's charm hides a calculating heart.
- **Public display:** the court is a stage; the lady wants glory, and receives public shame.
- **Justice:** the King's verdict restores the moral balance.

### 7.1 Tone and mood

| Part | Tone / mood |
|---|---|
| lines 1–6 | splendid, festive |
| lines 7–12 | violent, thrilling, terrifying |
| lines 13–18 | ironic — the lady's thoughts expose her vanity |
| lines 19–24 | dramatic, swift; ending with stern moral approval |

### 7.2 Significance of the title

The title names the two things that decide the story: the **glove** — the lady's token, the test, and finally the instrument of her shame — and the **lions** — the danger she was willing to put her lover in. The plain title, like a fable's, prepares us for a simple story with a sharp moral.

### 7.3 Alternative titles (for the 2027 specimen Q6(v))

| Title | Reason |
|---|---|
| *Vanity Rebuked* | the King condemns the lady's vanity |
| *The Test of Love* | the lady tests de Lorge's love and fails the test herself |
| *Not with Love* | the key phrase of the climax |
| *Courage and Vanity* | the contrast between de Lorge and the lady |

**Solutions — MCQs**

**2025 specimen Q1(vii).** **(c) 'Rich gifts of raiment or grain or gold?'** "Ramped and roared" is **alliteration** (repeated *r*); option (c) repeats the *g* sound (gifts … grain … gold). Option (a) is a simile, (b) personification, (d) hyperbole/metaphor.
**2027 specimen Q1(viii).** **(b) 2 and 3 — provocative and challenging.** She provokes de Lorge and challenges him to prove his love; she is not scornful of him — she is proud of his bravery.

**2027 specimen Q6.**

**(i) Describe the scene at the beginning of the poem.** [3]
**King Francis**, the **nobles** and the **ladies** of the court are **seated together** on the benches, **watching the lions fight in the pit**; among them sits **Count de Lorge**, **sighing for his beloved**, who sits beside him.

**(ii) "Leigh Hunt uses vivid sound and visual images to describe the contest between the royal beasts in the pit." Justify the statement with close reference to the text.** [3]
**Visual images:** the lions' **"horrid laughing jaws"**; they **"glared"**; they **"gave blows like beams"**; they **"rolled on one another"**; **"the bloody foam above the bars came whisking through the air"**. **Sound images:** the lions **"roared"**; their **"stifled roar"**; the pit **"in a thunderous smother"**.

**(iii) Who was the 'beauteous lively dame' mentioned in the poem? What did she do? Why did she do this?** [3]
She was **de Lorge's beloved** (his lady love). She **dropped her glove into the lions' pit**. She did this (any one): **to test de Lorge's love**; **to win attention and admiration for herself** / **to attain glory** — out of **vanity**; she had **overheard the King say, "Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there,"** and saw her opportunity.

**(iv) When King Francis exclaims, 'rightly done!', what action of Count de Lorge does he applaud? Why do you think the Count behaved the way he did? Give a reason for your response.** [3]

### 8.2 More practice in the ICSE extract style

**Extract A.**
> Ramped and roared the lions, with horrid laughing jaws;
> They bit, they glared, gave blows like beams, a wind went with their paws;

**(i)** Where are the lions, and who is watching? [3]
**Answer:** In a **pit** below the royal benches; **King Francis**, his **nobles** and the **ladies** of the court — including **Count de Lorge** and his beloved — are watching the "royal sport".

**(ii)** Explain "horrid laughing jaws" and "blows like beams". [3]
**Answer:** The lions' wide-open jaws look like a **terrible grin** — dreadful and mocking. Their paws strike **heavy blows like wooden beams** — showing their immense strength.

**(iii)** Name the figure of speech in "Ramped and roared the lions" and in "a wind went with their paws". [3]
**Answer:** **Alliteration** (and inversion) in "Ramped and roared"; **hyperbole** (and alliteration of *w*) in "a wind went with their paws" — their swipes are so strong they seem to stir a wind.

**(iv)** How does the fight end, and what does the King say? [3]
**Answer:** The lions roll on one another till the pit is a **"thunderous smother"** of sand and mane, and **bloody foam** whisks above the bars; the King says, "**Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there**" — glad to be safe above.

**(v)** Why does the poet describe the fight in such detail? [4]
**Answer:** To show how **deadly** the pit is, so that we understand the **enormity** of the lady's demand: she is willing to send her lover **"there"** — into that savage danger — for her own glory. The vivid images make de Lorge's leap more **heroic** and the lady's vanity more **shocking**, preparing for the King's moral.

**Extract B.**
> She dropped her glove, to prove his love, then looked at him and smiled;
> He bowed, and in a moment leaped among the lions wild:

**(i)** Who is "She"? What had she thought before dropping the glove? [3]
**Answer:** **De Lorge's beloved**. She thought that the Count was "**brave as brave can be**" and would do wondrous things to show his love; with the King, ladies and lovers all watching, the occasion was perfect — "**great glory will be mine**".

**(ii)** Why does she smile at de Lorge? [3]
**Answer:** She is **confident** that he will obey and win her glory; the smile is also part of her **artificial charm** — "smiling lips … which always seemed the same".

**(iii)** What does de Lorge's bow suggest? [3]
**Answer:** **Courtesy and calm courage** — he accepts the challenge without hesitation or complaint; perhaps also a **cold irony**, since he is already judging her.

**(iv)** What does de Lorge do after retrieving the glove? [3]
**Answer:** He returns quickly to his place and **throws the glove — "but not with love" — right in the lady's face**.

**(v)** What does the King say, and what is the moral of the poem? [4]
**Answer:** The King rises and exclaims, "**rightly done!**" and adds, "**No love … but vanity, sets love a task like that.**" The **moral**: true love never puts the beloved in needless danger for show; such demands come from **vanity**, which deserves to be exposed and shamed.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (3 and 4 marks)

### Extract parts (3 marks each)

**Q1.** Describe the scene at the beginning of the poem. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** King Francis, the nobles and the ladies sit together on the benches.
They are watching the lions fight in the pit.
Count de Lorge sits among them, sighing for his beloved beside him.

**Q2.** Give examples of visual and sound images in the lion fight. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Visual: horrid laughing jaws; they glared; blows like beams; rolled on one another; bloody foam whisking through the air.
Sound: roared; stifled roar; thunderous smother.
(Any three in all, covering both kinds.)

**Q3.** Who was the 'beauteous lively dame'? What did she do and why? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** De Lorge's beloved.
She dropped her glove into the lions' pit.
To test his love and win glory and admiration for herself — vanity.

**Q4.** What did the King say about the fight, and how did it affect the lady?
**Answer:** "Faith, gentlemen, we're better here than there."
The lady overheard it.
It gave her the idea of sending de Lorge "there" — into the pit — to prove his love.

**Q5.** What did the lady think before dropping her glove?
**Answer:** That the Count was as brave as brave can be.
That he would do wondrous things to show his love.
That with everyone watching, the occasion was perfect: "great glory will be mine".

**Q6.** What did de Lorge do when the glove fell?
**Answer:** He bowed.
In a moment he leapt among the wild lions.
His leap was quick, his return was quick, and he regained his place.

**Q7.** What action of de Lorge does the King applaud, and why did de Lorge act so? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** Throwing the glove in the lady's face.
He was disillusioned — he saw through her vanity.
He wanted to shame her publicly.

**Q8.** Explain "No love … but vanity, sets love a task like that."
**Answer:** True love would never send the beloved into deadly danger.
Only vanity — a wish for glory — demands such a test.
It is the moral of the poem.

**Q9.** Explain "Valour and love, and a king above, and the royal beasts below."
**Answer:** The scene is arranged from top to bottom.
The King sits above; the knights' valour and ladies' love fill the benches; the lions fight below.
Internal rhyme (love/above) gives a musical effect.

**Q10.** Name and explain two devices in "Ramped and roared the lions, with horrid laughing jaws".
**Answer:** Alliteration — "Ramped and roared".
Personification — "laughing jaws", as if the lions grinned.
Both make the fight vivid and terrifying.

**Q11.** What do "smiling lips" and "sharp bright eyes" suggest about the lady? *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** "Smiling lips … which always seemed the same" — an artificial, fixed, expressionless smile.
"Sharp bright eyes" — shrewd and cunning.
She is charming on the surface but calculating within.

**Q12.** What is the form of the poem?
**Answer:** 24 lines in rhyming couplets.
Long, galloping lines in ballad style.
Internal rhymes such as glove/love and love/above.

### Extract parts (4 marks each)

**Q13.** Suggest your own title for the poem and justify it. *(2027 specimen)*
**Answer:** (Personal response.) *Vanity Rebuked*.
The lady's vanity drives the story.
De Lorge publicly rejects her selfish test.
The King ends by condemning vanity.

**Q14.** Write a character sketch of Count de Lorge.
**Answer:** Brave — he leaps among the lions in a moment.
Quick and skilful — "His leap was quick, return was quick".
Perceptive — he sees through the lady's vanity.
Self-respecting — he throws the glove in her face, refusing to be used.

**Q15.** Write a character sketch of the lady.
**Answer:** Beautiful and lively on the surface.
Artificial and calculating — fixed smile, sharp eyes.
Vain — she wants "great glory".
Selfish — she risks her lover's life for show.

**Q16.** Why does the poet describe the lion fight so vividly?
**Answer:** To show how deadly the pit is.
To make de Lorge's leap more heroic.
To make the lady's demand more shocking.
To prepare for the King's moral judgement.

**Q17.** Was de Lorge right to throw the glove in the lady's face? Give your view.
**Answer:** (Personal response.) Yes:
she treated his life as a toy for her glory;
he proved his courage but rejected her vanity;
public shame matched her public display — and the King approved.

**Q18.** How is irony used in the poem?
**Answer:** The lady seeks glory but gets public shame.
"To prove his love" — the test proves her vanity.
"glove … love" rhymes again, but "not with love".
The King's lighthearted "better here than there" inspires a dangerous plan.

**Q19.** What is the role of King Francis in the poem?
**Answer:** He presides over the spectacle.
His remark gives the lady her idea.
He judges the outcome: "rightly done!"
He states the moral: "No love … but vanity."

**Q20.** What message does the poem give about love?
**Answer:** True love cares for the beloved's safety.
It does not need public displays or dangerous proofs.
Vanity disguised as love is selfish.
Courage should be honoured, not exploited.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Poem at a glance
- **Lines 1–6:** **King Francis** — "hearty", loves a "royal sport"; nobles and ladies on benches; **Count de Lorge** with "one for whom he sighed"; "**Valour and love, and a king above, and the royal beasts below**".
- **Lines 7–12:** "**Ramped and roared**"; "**horrid laughing jaws**"; "**blows like beams**"; "**a wind went with their paws**"; "**stifled roar**"; "**thunderous smother**"; "**bloody foam … whisking**"; King: "**better here than there**".
- **Lines 13–18:** the lady **overhears**; "**beauteous lively dame**"; "**smiling lips and sharp bright eyes, which always seemed the same**"; "brave as brave can be"; "**I'll drop my glove, to prove his love; great glory will be mine**".
- **Lines 19–24:** she drops the glove, smiles; he **bows**, **leaps**; "His leap was quick, return was quick"; "**threw the glove, but not with love, right in the lady's face**"; King: "**rightly done!**"; "**No love … but vanity, sets love a task like that**".
- **Form:** 24 lines, rhyming couplets, internal rhyme, galloping rhythm.

### Common traps

| Trap | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| "Ramped and roared" is a simile | It is alliteration (2025 specimen). |
| The lady's action was scornful | Provocative and challenging (2027 specimen). |
| The glove fell by accident | She dropped it deliberately. |
| The King scolded de Lorge | He approved: "rightly done!" |
| De Lorge returned the glove lovingly | He threw it in her face — "not with love". |
| The lady's smile was warm | It was fixed and artificial. |
