## 1. Complete Lesson

### What is a resource?

#### Foundation first

You already know the word **environment** from earlier classes. It is everything around us — the land, water, air, plants and animals (the **natural** environment) and the roads, houses and fields that people have made (the **human-made** environment).

A **need** is something we must have to live and to live well — food, water, shelter, clothes, energy. This chapter asks one question: *when does a thing in our environment become useful enough to be called a resource?*

| A thing in nature | When is it a resource? |
|---|---|
| Water flowing in a river | When people use it to drink, to irrigate fields or to turn turbines that make electricity |
| Coal deep under the ground | Only after people learnt to mine it and burn it for heat and power |
| A forest on a hillside | When people use it for timber, fuel, fruit, medicine or to protect the soil |

**Small example.** The water stored in **Umiam Lake** near Shillong is used to make electricity. The same water would be "just water" if no one had built the dam and the power station. Human effort turned it into a resource.

Everything available in our environment which can be used to satisfy our needs, provided it is **technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable**, can be termed a **resource**.

- A material found in nature becomes a resource only when **human beings identify its usefulness** and have the means to use it. Coal lying deep under the ground was not a resource until people learnt to mine it and burn it for energy.
- The process of transformation of things available in our environment involves an **interdependent relationship between nature, technology and institutions**. Human beings interact with nature through technology and create institutions to accelerate their economic development.
- So **resources are a function of human activities**. Human beings themselves are **essential components of resources**: they transform material available in our environment into resources and use them.

> **Are resources free gifts of nature?** Many people think so, but this is not entirely true. Resources are not free gifts of nature; they **become resources through human effort**, knowledge and technology.

**The three conditions, one by one.** A thing must pass all three tests to be a resource.

| Condition | What it means | Example of a thing that fails it |
|---|---|---|
| **Technologically accessible** | We have the tools and know-how to reach it and use it | Coal lying deep underground, before people knew how to mine it |
| **Economically feasible** | Using it costs less than the value we get from it | A mineral found in such tiny amounts that digging it out costs more than it is worth |
| **Culturally acceptable** | Society accepts its use; it does not go against people's values and customs | Trees in a sacred grove, which the community has protected for generations and will not cut for timber |

**Why are resources called "a function of human activities"?** In Mathematics, a function means that one thing depends on another. Here, whether a thing is a resource *depends on* what people do. If people find a use for it, have the technology to use it and set up institutions (such as markets, banks, laws and government departments) to manage it, it becomes a resource. If they do not, it stays a mere natural object. So the same thing can be a resource in one age and not in another.

**Why are nature, technology and institutions called interdependent?** Each needs the other two. **Nature** provides the material — say, river water. **Technology** — a dam and turbines — turns it into electricity. **Institutions** — a power department, laws and money from the government — build the dam and send the power to homes. Remove any one of the three, and the river water does not become a resource.

**Why are human beings the most important resource?** Every other resource becomes useful only through people. It is people who identify a use, invent the technology and build the institutions. A healthy, educated and skilled population can turn even a small resource base into wealth.

> **In the exam, write:** "A resource must be **technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable**." Examiners look for all three phrases. Writing only "anything useful to us" earns at most half the marks.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (2 marks — SSLC 2026).** Explain why human resources are considered the most important resource among all types of resources.

*Model answer.*
1. Things in nature become resources only when **human beings identify their use** and apply their **knowledge, skills and technology** to them.
2. People **transform materials into resources**, build institutions and use resources to produce wealth. Without skilled and healthy people, other resources have **no value**.

**Why this scores:** 1 mark for saying that humans *convert* things into resources (knowledge, skill, technology); 1 mark for saying that other resources are of no use without people.

**Example 2 (3 marks).** What is a resource? State the three conditions a thing must fulfil to become a resource.

*Model answer.* A **resource** is everything available in our environment which can be used to satisfy our needs. It must be:
1. **Technologically accessible** — we have the technology to obtain and use it;
2. **Economically feasible** — it is worth using in terms of cost;
3. **Culturally acceptable** — its use is accepted by society.

**Why this scores:** 1 mark for each condition. The definition line is needed to frame the answer; give each condition a short meaning, not just the phrase.

**Example 3 (5 marks).** "Resources are a function of human activities." Explain this statement. Are resources free gifts of nature?

*Model answer.*
1. A material found in nature becomes a resource only when **human beings identify its usefulness** and have the means to use it. Coal under the ground was not a resource until people learnt to mine it and burn it.
2. The transformation of things into resources involves an **interdependent relationship between nature, technology and institutions**.
3. Human beings **interact with nature through technology** and create **institutions** to speed up economic development.
4. Human beings are themselves **essential components of resources**: they transform the material in the environment into resources and use them.
5. So resources are **not free gifts of nature**. They become resources only through **human effort, knowledge and technology**.

**Why this scores:** five clear points, one mark each. Point 2 (nature–technology–institutions) is the key phrase from the textbook. Point 5 answers the second part of the question; without it you lose a mark.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is air a resource? We do not "use" it with any technology.**
Yes. We breathe it, and it is used to burn fuel and turn windmills. It is accessible, it costs nothing to breathe and no culture objects to it, so it passes all three tests.

**Q. Is everything in nature a resource?**
No. A thing in nature is only a *possible* resource. It becomes a resource when people find a use for it and can use it at a reasonable cost in a way society accepts.

**Q. Can a resource stop being a resource?**
Yes. If a cheaper or better substitute is found, or if a society decides that a use is no longer acceptable, a thing may lose its value as a resource. Resources change with human needs and technology.

**Q. Why do some people say resources are free gifts of nature?**
Because the material itself — water, soil, minerals — is made by nature. But it is human effort that makes it useful. The textbook says the idea of "free gifts" is not entirely true, and this wrong idea led people to misuse resources.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Writing "a resource is anything found in nature". Many resources need **human effort** to become useful, and not everything in nature is a resource.
- Leaving out one of the three conditions, or writing "culturally feasible" or "economically acceptable". The exact pairs are **technologically accessible, economically feasible, culturally acceptable**.
- Writing that human beings are *not* resources. They are **essential components of resources**.

#### Check yourself

1. Name the three conditions a thing must satisfy to be called a resource.
2. Which three things share an interdependent relationship in turning nature into resources?
3. Why are resources not "free gifts of nature"?

**Answers:** 1. Technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable. 2. Nature, technology and institutions. 3. Because things in nature become resources only through human effort, knowledge and technology.

### Classification of resources

#### Foundation first

**What is classification?** To classify is to sort things into groups by one feature at a time. You can sort the students of your class by height, by village or by birthday month. Each way of sorting is a **basis**. The same student falls into a different group under each basis.

Resources are sorted in the same way, by **four bases**: origin, exhaustibility, ownership and status of development. So one resource gets **four labels**, one under each basis.

**Distances at sea.** The ownership classification uses the **nautical mile**, the unit of distance used at sea. One nautical mile is about **1.85 km**.

| Distance from the coast | Name | Who controls the resources |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 12 nautical miles (22.2 km) | **Territorial water** | The nation, fully |
| Up to 200 nautical miles | **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)** | The nation |
| Beyond 200 nautical miles | **Open ocean** | International institutions |

Resources can be classified in four ways.

![Fig. 1.1 Classification of resources](figures/ss_ch05_fig01_classification.png)

#### (a) On the basis of origin — biotic and abiotic

1. **Biotic resources** are obtained from the **biosphere and have life** — human beings, flora and fauna, fisheries, livestock, etc.
2. **Abiotic resources** are all those things which are composed of **non-living things** — rocks, metals, soil, water, air, etc.

#### (b) On the basis of exhaustibility — renewable and non-renewable

1. **Renewable (replenishable) resources** can be **renewed or reproduced** by physical, chemical or mechanical processes — solar and wind energy, water, forests and wildlife.
   - They may be **continuous or flow** resources (wind, water, solar energy, tidal energy) or **biological** (natural vegetation, wildlife).
2. **Non-renewable resources** occur over a **very long geological time**. Minerals and fossil fuels are examples; they take **millions of years** to form.
   - Some, like **metals**, are **recyclable**.
   - Others, like **fossil fuels**, **cannot be recycled** and get exhausted with their use.

#### (c) On the basis of ownership — individual, community, national and international

1. **Individual resources** are owned privately by individuals. Many farmers own land allotted by the government against the payment of revenue; in villages there are people with land ownership but also many landless. Urban people own plots, houses and other property. **Plantations, pasture lands, ponds, water in wells** are examples of individually owned resources.
2. **Community-owned resources** are accessible to **all the members of the community**. Village commons — **grazing grounds, burial grounds, village ponds** — and in urban areas **public parks, picnic spots and playgrounds** are open to all the people living there.
3. **National resources:** technically, **all the resources belong to the nation**. The country has legal powers to acquire even private property for public good — you might have seen roads, canals and railways being constructed on fields owned by individuals. Urban development authorities get empowered by the government to acquire land. **All the minerals, water resources, forests, wildlife, land within the political boundaries and the oceanic area up to 12 nautical miles (22.2 km) from the coast**, termed **territorial water**, and the resources therein belong to the nation.
4. **International resources** are regulated by **international institutions**. The oceanic resources **beyond 200 nautical miles** of the **Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ)** belong to the open ocean, and **no individual country can utilise these without the concurrence of international institutions**.

> India has got the right to mine **manganese nodules** from the bed of the Indian Ocean, from the area beyond the EEZ.

#### (d) On the basis of status of development — potential, developed, stock and reserves

1. **Potential resources** are found in a region but have **not been utilised**. For example, the western parts of India, particularly **Rajasthan and Gujarat**, have enormous potential for the development of **wind and solar energy**, but so far these have not been developed properly.
2. **Developed resources** are surveyed and their **quality and quantity have been determined** for utilisation. The development of resources depends on **technology and the level of their feasibility**.
3. **Stock:** materials in the environment which have the potential to satisfy human needs, but **human beings do not have the appropriate technology** to access them. For example, **water** is a compound of two gases, hydrogen and oxygen; **hydrogen** can be used as a rich source of energy, but we do not have the advanced technical know-how to use it for this purpose. So it is a stock.
4. **Reserves** are the **subset of the stock** which can be put into use with the help of **existing technical know-how**, but their use has **not been started**. These can be used to meet future requirements. **River water** can be used for generating hydroelectric power, but presently it is being utilised only to a limited extent. **Water in dams, forests**, etc. are reserves which can be used in the future.

> **Remember the difference:** *stock* — the technology does **not** exist yet; *reserves* — the technology exists, but the resource is kept for future use.

**One resource, four labels.** Every resource can be placed under all four bases at once.

| Resource | Origin | Exhaustibility | Ownership | Status of development |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Coal being mined in Jharkhand | abiotic | non-renewable (cannot be recycled) | national | developed |
| Fish in a village pond | biotic | renewable (biological) | community | developed |
| A farmer's own plantation | biotic | renewable (biological) | individual | developed |
| Water stored in a dam for future use | abiotic | renewable | national | reserve |
| Hydrogen in water, as a source of energy | abiotic | — | — | stock |

**Why are metals recyclable but fossil fuels not?** When we use iron or copper, the metal does not disappear. An old iron gate or a copper wire can be melted and made into something new. When we burn coal or petrol, it changes into gases, ash and heat. The energy is used up and cannot be collected back. So both are non-renewable, but only metals can be recycled.

**Why must flow resources be used when and where they occur?** Wind, sunlight and flowing water cannot be stored as they are. The wind that blows today over a hill is gone tomorrow. We can only catch it with a windmill or a solar panel at that moment and in that place.

**Why does the open ocean need international institutions?** The ocean beyond 200 nautical miles belongs to no single country. If every country took what it liked, the fish and minerals there could be finished quickly. So international institutions make the rules, and a country needs their **concurrence** (agreement) to use these resources. India mines manganese nodules there with that permission.

**Why is a privately owned field still a national resource?** Technically all resources belong to the nation. The government has the legal power to acquire private land for public good — to build a road, a canal or a railway line. The owner is paid, but cannot refuse the public use.

**Status of development, side by side:**

| Type | Key idea | Is it being used now? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potential | Found in a region, not developed properly | Not yet | Wind and solar energy in Rajasthan and Gujarat |
| Developed (actual) | Surveyed; quality and quantity known | Yes | Coal in working mines |
| Stock | Can satisfy needs, but **no technology** to use it | No | Hydrogen in water as energy |
| Reserves | Part of the stock; **technology exists** | Not yet — kept for the future | Water in dams, forests |

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is water renewable or non-renewable?**
Renewable. The water cycle keeps renewing it. But usable fresh water is limited, so it must still be used carefully.

**Q. Are forests renewable? People cut them down and they disappear.**
Forests are **biological** renewable resources: trees can grow again. But they renew slowly. If we cut them faster than they regrow, a renewable resource can be lost in practice.

**Q. What is the difference between "potential" and "reserves"? Both are not in use.**
A potential resource is found in a region but has **not been developed properly** — like Rajasthan's wind and solar energy. Reserves are part of the stock that we **can** use with today's technology but have **deliberately kept for the future** — like water in dams.

**Q. Is a "developed" resource the same as an "actual" resource?**
Yes. Some books and old papers say "actual resources" — resources that have been surveyed, whose quantity and quality are known, and which are being used now.

**Q. A national highway is used by everyone. Is it a community resource?**
No. It is built and owned by the nation, so it is a **national** resource. Community resources belong to the people of one village or town, like a village pond or a playground.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Swapping **stock** and **reserves**. Stock: no technology. Reserves: technology exists, use not started.
- Writing territorial water as 200 nautical miles. Territorial water is **12 nautical miles (22.2 km)**; **200** nautical miles is the limit of the EEZ.
- Calling all non-renewable resources non-recyclable. **Metals are recyclable**; fossil fuels are not.
- Giving "fish" as abiotic or "water" as biotic. Biotic resources have life; abiotic ones do not.

#### Check yourself

1. On the basis of origin, under which type would you place livestock?
2. Up to how many nautical miles from the coast does territorial water extend?
3. Hydrogen in water could be a rich source of energy, but we lack the technology to use it. What type of resource is it?

**Answers:** 1. Biotic. 2. 12 nautical miles (22.2 km). 3. Stock.

### Development of resources

#### Foundation first

This part names four **global ecological crises**. You met these words in Science and Geography in earlier classes. In one line each:

| Crisis | What it is | Main human cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Global warming** | A slow rise in the average temperature of the earth | Burning coal, petrol and wood adds heat-trapping gases to the air; cutting forests means fewer trees absorb them |
| **Ozone layer depletion** | Thinning of the ozone layer high in the atmosphere, which blocks harmful ultraviolet rays of the sun | Certain man-made chemicals used in refrigerators, air-conditioners and sprays |
| **Environmental pollution** | Harmful substances in air, water and soil | Smoke from factories and vehicles, waste dumped in rivers, overuse of chemicals |
| **Land degradation** | Land loses its fertility and becomes less useful | Deforestation, overgrazing, mining, over-irrigation |

All four come from the same root: using resources faster and more carelessly than nature can repair.

Resources are vital for human survival as well as for maintaining the quality of life. It was believed that resources are **free gifts of nature**. As a result, human beings used them **indiscriminately**, and this has led to the following major problems:

1. **Depletion of resources** for satisfying the **greed of a few individuals**.
2. **Accumulation of resources in a few hands**, which, in turn, divided society into two segments — the **haves and have-nots**, or the rich and the poor.
3. **Indiscriminate exploitation** of resources has led to **global ecological crises** such as **global warming, ozone layer depletion, environmental pollution and land degradation**.

An **equitable distribution of resources** has become essential for a sustained quality of life and global peace. If the present trend of resource depletion by a few individuals and countries continues, the future of our planet is in danger. Therefore, **resource planning** is essential for sustainable existence of all forms of life.

#### Sustainable development

> **Sustainable economic development** means "development should take place **without damaging the environment**, and development in the present should **not compromise with the needs of the future generations**."

Sustainable development balances three things at once: **economic growth, social justice and environmental protection**. It meets human development goals while sustaining the natural resources and ecosystem services on which the economy and society depend.

#### The Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit, 1992

- In **June 1992**, **more than 100 heads of states** met in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil**, for the first **International Earth Summit**. The Summit was convened to address the urgent problems of **environmental protection and socio-economic development** at the global level.
- The assembled leaders signed the **Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity**.
- The Rio Convention endorsed the **global Forest Principles** and adopted **Agenda 21** for achieving sustainable development in the 21st century.

> **Agenda 21** is the declaration signed by world leaders in 1992 at the **United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED)**, held in Rio de Janeiro. It aims at **achieving global sustainable development**. It is an agenda to **combat environmental damage, poverty and disease** through **global co-operation** on common interests, mutual needs and shared responsibilities. One major objective of Agenda 21 is that **every local government should draw its own local Agenda 21**.

**Why did the belief in "free gifts of nature" cause harm?** When people think a thing is free and endless, they do not count the cost of using it. They cut forests, dig minerals and pump water without asking how much is left or what happens to the land. The belief made people careless, and carelessness led to depletion.

**Why does depletion "for the greed of a few" matter to everyone?** Resources are shared by the whole of humanity, including people not yet born. When a few individuals or countries take far more than they need, less is left for the rest. The poor lose access first, and the damage — polluted rivers, warmer climate — falls on everybody.

**Why is equitable distribution linked to "global peace"?** When resources pile up in a few hands, society splits into the **haves and have-nots**. Countries and groups that are left out feel cheated, and quarrels over land, water and minerals follow. Fair sharing removes a major cause of conflict.

**The three goals of sustainable development.** Think of a three-legged stool. If one leg is missing, it falls.

| Goal | What it means | If it is ignored |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic growth** | More production, jobs and income | People stay poor |
| **Social justice** | Everyone gets a fair share of resources and benefits | Rich–poor divide widens |
| **Environmental protection** | Soil, water, air, forests and wildlife are kept healthy | Resources run out; future generations suffer |

**The Rio Earth Summit at a glance:**

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| When? | June 1992 |
| Where? | Rio de Janeiro, Brazil |
| Who? | More than 100 heads of states |
| Official name | United Nations Conference on Environment and Development (UNCED) |
| Why? | To address the urgent problems of environmental protection and socio-economic development at the global level |
| What was signed? | The Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity |
| What else? | Endorsed the global Forest Principles; adopted **Agenda 21** |

**Why "Agenda 21"?** An agenda is a list of things to be done. The "21" stands for the **21st century**: Agenda 21 is the world's to-do list for sustainable development in this century.

**Why should every local government draw its own local Agenda 21?** Problems differ from place to place. A town short of water and a district losing its forests need different plans. Local governments know their own area best, so the global goal is turned into local action — "think globally, act locally".

*Model answer.*

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Does sustainable development mean we should stop using resources?**
No. It means using them **wisely** — taking what we need today while leaving enough, in good condition, for those who come after us.

**Q. Was the Rio Summit the same as Agenda 21?**
No. The **Rio Earth Summit (UNCED)** was the meeting, held in June 1992. **Agenda 21** was one of the documents it adopted.

**Q. Who first gave the idea of sustainable development?**
The **Brundtland Commission Report (1987)** introduced the concept. Rio in 1992 turned it into a world programme. You will meet the Brundtland Report again under conservation.

**Q. How can a global ecological crisis start from local actions?**
Each forest cut or each car driven adds a little. Millions of such actions across the world add up to a warmer earth and a thinner ozone layer.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Writing **Agenda 11 or Agenda 41**. It is **Agenda 21** — for the 21st century.
- Dating the Rio Summit 1987 or 1972. It was **June 1992**; 1987 is the Brundtland Report.
- Placing the Earth Summit in Sao Paulo or Buenos Aires. It was in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil**.
- Defining sustainable development without mentioning **future generations**.

3. What did the summit adopt for achieving sustainable development in the 21st century?

**Answers:** 1. Depletion of resources, accumulation in a few hands (haves and have-nots), and global ecological crises. 2. Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, in June 1992. 3. Agenda 21.

| Rajasthan | North-west | The Thar Desert; very dry and sunny |
| Arunachal Pradesh | Far north-east, beside Assam | Himalayan hills with many fast rivers |
| Ladakh | Far north | A cold desert, high and isolated |

**What is planning?** Planning means deciding in advance *what* to do, *how* to do it and *with what*. A family plans its monthly spending; a country plans how to use its land, water, minerals and money. After Independence, India planned its development through **Five Year Plans**, beginning with the **First Five Year Plan** (from 1951).

Planning is the widely accepted strategy for the judicious use of resources. It has importance in a country like **India**, which has **enormous diversity in the availability of resources**.

- There are regions which are **rich in certain types of resources but deficient in others**. There are some regions which can be considered **self-sufficient** in terms of the availability of resources, and some regions which have an **acute shortage** of some vital resources.
  - **Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh and Chhattisgarh** are rich in **minerals and coal** deposits.
  - **Arunachal Pradesh** has an abundance of **water resources** but lacks **infrastructural development**.
  - **Rajasthan** is very well endowed with **solar and wind energy** but lacks **water resources**.
  - The cold desert of **Ladakh** is relatively isolated from the rest of the country. It has a very **rich cultural heritage** but is deficient in **water, infrastructure and some vital minerals**.
- This calls for **balanced resource planning** at the national, state, regional and local levels.

#### Resource planning in India

Resource planning is a complex process which involves three stages:

1. **Identification and inventory** of resources across the regions of the country. This involves **surveying, mapping and qualitative and quantitative estimation and measurement** of the resources.
2. **Evolving a planning structure** endowed with **appropriate technology, skill and institutional set-up** for implementing resource development plans.
3. **Matching the resource development plans with the overall national development plans.**

![Fig. 1.2 The three stages of resource planning](figures/ss_ch05_fig02_planning.png)

India has made concerted efforts for achieving the goals of resource planning **right from the First Five Year Plan**, launched after Independence.

**Resources alone are not enough.** The availability of resources is a necessary condition for the development of any region, but mere availability of resources **in the absence of corresponding changes in technology and institutions** may hinder development.

- There are many regions in our country that are **rich in resources** but are included among **economically backward** regions. On the contrary, there are some regions which have a **poor resource base** but are **economically developed**.
- The history of **colonisation** reveals that rich resources in colonies were the main attractions for the foreign invaders. It was primarily the **higher level of technological development** of the colonising countries that helped them to exploit the resources of other regions and establish their supremacy over the colonies.
- Therefore, resources can contribute to development only when they are accompanied by **appropriate technological development and institutional changes**. India has experienced all this in different phases of colonisation. In India, development, in general, and resource development in particular, does not only involve the availability of resources, but also the **technology, quality of human resources and the historical experiences** of the people.

> **How does a potential resource become an actual resource?** By (i) identifying it and preparing an inventory — surveying, mapping and measuring it; and (ii) building the planning structure — the technology, skills and institutions needed to use it.

**Why does India's diversity make planning essential?** If every region had a bit of everything, each could look after itself. But India's resources are spread unevenly: one region has minerals but not water, another has water but no roads. Without a plan, rich regions grow richer and others are left behind. Planning moves resources, money and technology to where they are needed, so that development is **balanced**.

**The three stages as a chain.** Each stage needs the one before it.

| Stage | Question it answers | Example (a river with power potential) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identification and inventory | *What do we have, where, and how much?* | Survey the river, map it, measure how much water flows through the year |
| 2. Planning structure | *Who will develop it, with what technology and skills?* | Engineers, a power department, funds, machinery for a dam and power station |
| 3. Matching with national plans | *Does it fit the country's overall goals?* | The power project is included in the national plan for electricity and industry |

**Why can a resource-rich region stay backward?** Resources are a *necessary* condition for development, but not a *sufficient* one. Minerals lying in the ground do not build schools or factories by themselves. A region also needs **technology** to use them, **institutions** (roads, banks, training, good administration) and **skilled people**. Where these are missing, the resources may even be carried away to be used elsewhere, and the local people gain little.

**Why could the colonisers exploit the colonies?** The colonies had rich resources, but the colonising countries had **higher technology** — ships, machines and weapons. With that technology they took raw materials from colonies like India, turned them into goods in their own factories and grew rich. The lesson: whoever has the technology controls the benefits of the resource.

### Conservation of resources

Resources are vital for any developmental activity. But **irrational consumption and over-utilisation** of resources may lead to socio-economic and environmental problems. To overcome these problems, **resource conservation at various levels is important**.

- **Mahatma Gandhi** was very apt in voicing his concern about resource conservation: **"There is enough for everybody's need and not for anybody's greed."** He placed the **greedy and selfish individuals** and the **exploitative nature of modern technology** as the root cause of resource depletion at the global level. He was **against mass production** and wanted to replace it with **production by the masses**.
- At the international level, the **Club of Rome** advocated resource conservation for the first time in a more systematic way in **1968**.
- Subsequently, in **1974**, the Gandhian philosophy was once again presented by **Schumacher** in his book ***Small is Beautiful***.
- The seminal contribution with respect to resource conservation at the global level was made by the **Brundtland Commission Report, 1987**. This report introduced the concept of **sustainable development** and advocated it as a means for resource conservation; it was subsequently published in a book entitled ***Our Common Future***.
- Another significant contribution was made at the **Earth Summit at Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, in 1992**.

![Fig. 1.3 Steps towards resource conservation](figures/ss_ch05_fig03_conservation.png)

**Ways to conserve resources** (in our everyday life):

1. **Reduce, reuse and recycle** — use less, use things again, and recycle metals, paper and plastic.
2. Use **renewable energy** (solar, wind) in place of fossil fuels wherever possible.
3. **Save water and electricity** at home and at school.
4. **Plant trees** and protect forests; practise afforestation wherever trees are cut.
5. Prefer **public transport**, cycling or walking to private vehicles.

**Milestones of resource conservation — a timeline:**

| Year | Event | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| — | Mahatma Gandhi: "There is enough for everybody's need and not for anybody's greed." | Greed and exploitative technology are the root cause of depletion |
| 1968 | Club of Rome | First systematic call for resource conservation at the international level |
| 1974 | Schumacher, *Small is Beautiful* | Presented the Gandhian philosophy again |
| 1987 | Brundtland Commission Report, *Our Common Future* | Introduced **sustainable development** as a means of conservation |
| 1992 | Earth Summit, Rio de Janeiro | World leaders adopted Agenda 21 |

**What did Gandhi mean by "need" and "greed"?** The earth produces enough to feed, clothe and house every person. The trouble begins when some people want far more than they need — more land, more goods, more profit. Their greed uses up what should have been shared.

**Why was Gandhi against mass production?** Mass production uses big machines in large factories. It consumes huge amounts of raw material and energy, and puts profit in a few hands. **Production by the masses** means many people producing in their homes and villages with simple tools, as with the spinning wheel. It gives work to more people and uses resources more gently.

**Why is conservation needed even for renewable resources?** Renewable does not mean endless. Forests, fish and fresh water renew only at a certain speed. If we use them faster than that speed, they too run out.

**Why do small daily actions matter?** One person switching off a fan saves little. But when millions of homes, schools and offices do the same, the saving is large. Conservation works best when it becomes everyone's habit.

1. A resource is anything in the environment that is technologically accessible, economically feasible and culturally acceptable; resources are a function of human activities.

3. Indiscriminate use has caused depletion, unequal accumulation and global ecological crises; sustainable development is the answer (Rio Summit 1992, Agenda 21).
4. Resource planning in India has three stages and has been followed since the First Five Year Plan; resources need technology and institutions to bring development.

**Answer:** (i) New **technology** made it possible to extract and use resources that were earlier inaccessible (deep coal, petroleum, minerals). (ii) **Industrialisation** needed huge amounts of raw materials and energy. (iii) Economic growth raised **incomes and demand** for goods, vehicles and electricity. (iv) **Mass production** and consumerism increased the use of resources. (v) Colonial and modern economies with better technology **exploited resources** of other regions on a large scale. As a result, resources are being depleted faster than they can be replaced.

**Q5.** Why is resource planning essential in India?
**Answer:** (i) India has **enormous diversity** in the availability of resources — some regions are rich in one resource but poor in others (Rajasthan has solar and wind energy but lacks water; Arunachal Pradesh has water but lacks infrastructure). (ii) Resources are **limited**, and indiscriminate use leads to depletion. (iii) Planning ensures **judicious and equitable use** and balanced regional development. (iv) It helps conserve resources for **future generations**.

**Q6.** Explain the three stages of resource planning in India.
**Answer:** (i) **Identification and inventory** of resources across regions — surveying, mapping, and qualitative and quantitative estimation and measurement. (ii) **Evolving a planning structure** with appropriate technology, skill and institutional set-up for implementing resource development plans. (iii) **Matching** the resource development plans with the **overall national development plans**. India has followed this since the First Five Year Plan.

**Q7.** Explain the problems caused by the indiscriminate use of resources.
**Answer:** (i) **Depletion** of resources to satisfy the greed of a few individuals. (ii) **Accumulation** of resources in a few hands, dividing society into the rich and the poor. (iii) **Global ecological crises** — global warming, ozone layer depletion, environmental pollution and land degradation. So equitable distribution and resource planning are essential.

Resource planning in India involves three stages:
1. **Identification and inventory** of resources across the regions of the country. This involves surveying, mapping, and the qualitative and quantitative estimation and measurement of resources.
2. **Evolving a planning structure** with the appropriate technology, skill and institutional set-up for carrying out resource development plans.
3. **Matching the resource development plans with the overall national development plans.**

**SSLC 2021, Q2(e).** **(iv)**. Reserves are a subset of stock that can be used with existing technology but are kept for the future, e.g. water in dams.

**SSLC 2021, Q3(e).** 1. **Renewable resources** can be renewed or reproduced by physical, chemical or mechanical processes. Examples: solar and wind energy, water, forests.
2. **Non-renewable resources** take millions of years to form and can be exhausted. Examples: coal, petroleum, minerals.

**SSLC 2020 (old), Q14(a).** **Actual** resources: they have been surveyed, and their quantity and quality determined, for use in present times.

**SSLC 2020 (old), Q14(b).** **Agenda 21**, signed at the Earth Summit in Rio de Janeiro in 1992.

**SSLC 2020 (old), Q16(a).** 1. **Renewable resources** can be **renewed or reproduced** by physical, chemical or mechanical processes, such as **solar and wind energy, water, forests and wildlife**.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (5 marks)

### Long answers (5 marks each)

**Q1.** Classify resources on the basis of ownership, with examples.
**Answer:** Individual resources are privately owned — plots, houses, plantations, wells.
Community-owned resources are open to all members — grazing grounds, village ponds, public parks.
National resources belong to the nation — minerals, forests, water, land within boundaries.
They include territorial water up to 12 nautical miles (22.2 km).
International resources lie beyond 200 nautical miles of the EEZ and are regulated by international institutions.

**Q2.** Classify resources on the basis of status of development.
**Answer:** Potential resources exist in a region but are not used — wind and solar energy in Rajasthan.
Developed resources are surveyed, and their quality and quantity are known.
Stock can satisfy needs, but the technology to use it is lacking — hydrogen from water.
Reserves are a subset of stock usable with existing technology but kept for future use.
Examples of reserves are water in dams and forests.

**Q3.** What problems have arisen from the indiscriminate use of resources? How can they be solved?
**Answer:** Resources are depleted to satisfy the greed of a few individuals.
They accumulate in a few hands, dividing society into the rich and the poor.
Over-exploitation causes global warming, ozone depletion, pollution and land degradation.
Equitable distribution and resource planning are needed.
Sustainable development and conservation protect resources for future generations.

**Q4.** Describe the Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit and Agenda 21.
**Answer:** In June 1992, over 100 heads of state met at Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.
They addressed environmental protection and socio-economic development globally.
They signed the Declaration on Global Climatic Change and Biological Diversity.
They endorsed the Forest Principles and adopted Agenda 21.
Agenda 21 seeks sustainable development through global co-operation; each local government should draw its own local Agenda 21.

**Q5.** Explain resource planning in India.
**Answer:** India has great diversity in resources — some regions rich in one resource, poor in others.
Stage 1: identification and inventory — surveying, mapping and measuring resources.
Stage 2: a planning structure with appropriate technology, skills and institutions.
Stage 3: matching resource plans with national development plans.
India has followed resource planning since the First Five Year Plan.

**Q6.** "Resources alone cannot bring development." Explain.
**Answer:** Mere availability of resources without technology and institutions may hinder development.
Many resource-rich regions of India are economically backward.
Some regions with a poor resource base are economically developed.
Colonisers exploited the resources of colonies through superior technology.
Development needs resources, technology, quality human resources and institutional change.

**Q7.** Why is conservation of resources necessary? Describe the efforts made for it.
**Answer:** Irrational consumption and over-use cause socio-economic and environmental problems.
Gandhi said there is enough for everybody's need, not for anybody's greed.
The Club of Rome (1968) advocated systematic conservation.
Schumacher's *Small is Beautiful* (1974) repeated Gandhian ideas.
The Brundtland Report (1987) and the Rio Summit (1992) promoted sustainable development.

**Q8.** Classify resources on the basis of origin and exhaustibility, with examples.
**Answer:** Biotic resources come from the biosphere and have life — flora, fauna, livestock.
Abiotic resources are non-living — rocks, metals, soil.
Renewable resources can be renewed — solar and wind energy, forests.
They may be flow resources (wind, water) or biological (vegetation, wildlife).
Non-renewable resources take millions of years to form; metals are recyclable, fossil fuels are not.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Solar on the roof)** A school in Shillong installs rooftop solar panels. Which category of resource has sunlight moved into?

**Model answer:** Resources alone do not bring development; without suitable technology, institutions, skilled people and fair distribution, the benefits go to a few.

**Q5. (Spot the error)** A student writes: "Hydrogen in water is a reserve, and water in dams is stock." Correct the statement.
**Model answer:** Hydrogen is stock (no technology yet); water in dams is a reserve (technology exists but use is kept for the future).

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Resource = technologically accessible + economically feasible + culturally acceptable; a function of human activities (nature + technology + institutions).
- Origin: biotic, abiotic. Exhaustibility: renewable (flow, biological), non-renewable (recyclable metals, non-recyclable fossil fuels).
- Ownership: individual, community, national (incl. territorial water to 12 nm / 22.2 km), international (beyond 200 nm EEZ).
- Status: potential (Rajasthan wind/solar), developed, stock (hydrogen), reserves (water in dams, forests).
- Indiscriminate use → depletion, inequality, global ecological crises; sustainable development; Rio 1992; Agenda 21.
- Resource planning: inventory → planning structure → match with national plans; since the First Five Year Plan.
- Conservation: Gandhi; Club of Rome 1968; Schumacher 1974; Brundtland 1987 (*Our Common Future*); Rio 1992.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Resources are free gifts of nature | They become resources through human effort. |
| Stock and reserves are the same | Stock lacks technology; reserves have it but are unused. |
| Territorial water extends 200 nm | It extends 12 nm; 200 nm is the EEZ limit. |
| All non-renewable resources can be recycled | Metals can; fossil fuels cannot. |
| Rich resources always mean development | Technology and institutions are also needed. |
| Agenda 21 was signed in 1987 | It was adopted at Rio in 1992. |
