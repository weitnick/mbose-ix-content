## 1. Complete Lesson

### Introduction

*A Letter to God* is a short story by the Mexican writer **G.L. Fuentes**. It is about **Lencho**, a poor but hard-working farmer whose crop of corn is completely destroyed by a **hailstorm**. With nothing left, Lencho turns to the one hope he has: **God**. He writes a letter to God asking for **a hundred pesos**, and posts it. The letter reaches the **postmaster**, a kind man who is moved by Lencho's faith. He collects money from his staff and friends and sends it to Lencho, signed "God". But the story ends with a sharp twist of **irony**: Lencho, instead of being grateful, believes that the **post office employees have stolen** part of the money.

The story asks a simple but deep question: **what should we put our faith in?** It shows the power of faith, the kindness of ordinary people, and how easily good deeds can be misunderstood.

> **Background:** The story is set in a farming valley in Latin America. The money used there is the **peso**. In the story, money is sent through the post office, which was once the most common way of sending money to people far away. That is why the textbook begins with an activity on filling in a **money order form**.

#### Foundation first

Three ideas from earlier classes help you read this story well.

- **What a short story is.** A short story has a few characters and one main chain of events. It moves through a **setting** (where and when), a **conflict** (a problem), a **climax** (the high point) and an **ending** that leaves us with a point to think about. Here the setting is the lonely house in the valley, the conflict is the hailstorm and its result, the climax is Lencho's second letter, and the point is about faith.
- **Irony.** Irony is a gap between what we expect and what really happens. A fire station that catches fire is ironic. In **dramatic irony**, we readers know something a character does not. We know the postmaster sent the money; Lencho does not. This one idea explains the whole ending.
- **Faith.** Faith is firm trust without proof. Lencho needs no proof that God will help him. That is his strength, and also the reason he cannot see the real helpers.

**Why the post office matters.** The whole plot depends on the post office. Lencho can reach "God" only by posting a letter. The postmaster can answer only because letters pass through his hands. And because money also travelled by post (the money order), Lencho can believe that God, too, would send cash by mail. Remove the post office and there is no story.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (short answer, 2 marks).** Who is the author of *A Letter to God*, and what is the story about?

*Model answer.* *A Letter to God* is by the Mexican writer G.L. Fuentes. Lencho, a poor farmer, loses his corn crop to a hailstorm and writes to God for a hundred pesos. The kind postmaster sends money, but Lencho calls the post office staff crooks.

*Why this scores:* it names the author and his country (1 mark), and gives the problem, the help and the ironic ending in one tight summary (1 mark). It stays within the 30–40 word limit for a 2-mark answer.

**Example 2 (short answer, 2 marks).** What is the main irony in *A Letter to God*?

*Model answer.* The postmaster and his staff give their own money so that Lencho's faith in God is not shaken. Yet Lencho believes that these very people have stolen part of the money and calls them "a bunch of crooks". The helpers are treated as thieves.

*Why this scores:* it states what the helpers did (1 mark) and the opposite way Lencho sees them (1 mark). The short quotation shows the examiner that you know the text.

**Example 3 (a twist the board likes, MCQ).** The reader enjoys the ending of the story mainly because
(a) Lencho finally learns the truth (b) the reader knows something that Lencho does not (c) the postmaster is punished (d) God really sends the money.

*Solution.* **(b).** This is dramatic irony. Lencho never learns the truth, so (a) is wrong; nobody is punished, so (c) is wrong; and the money came from people, not from God, so (d) is wrong.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is this a true story?**
No. It is a piece of fiction by G.L. Fuentes, translated from Spanish. But the feelings in it, a farmer's fear of losing his crop and a stranger's quiet kindness, are true to life.

**Q. Why does the textbook start with a money order form?**
Because money in the story travels through the post. Filling in the form shows how people once sent money to someone far away, and it prepares you for an envelope with money reaching Lencho.

**Q. Is the story sad or funny?**
Both. The hailstorm and the family's fear are sad. The ending makes us smile, because Lencho's complaint against his helpers is so innocent and so wrong.

**Q. What is a peso?**
The peso is the money of Mexico and several other Latin American countries. Write "pesos", never "rupees" or "dollars", in your answers.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Spelling the author's name wrongly or calling him Spanish. He is **G.L. Fuentes**, a Mexican writer.
- Writing that God sent the money. The money came from the **postmaster, his employees and his friends**.
- Retelling the whole story in a 2-mark answer. Give only what the question asks.

1. Name the main character and say what destroyed his crop.
2. What is dramatic irony? Give the example from this story.
3. Why was the post office important to the plot?

**Answers:** 1. Lencho, a farmer; a hailstorm destroyed his corn. 2. When readers know something a character does not; we know the postmaster sent the money, but Lencho thinks God did. 3. Lencho could reach "God" only by posting a letter, and the postmaster could answer only because the letter passed through his office.

### The story

#### Foundation first: the language of the story

The writer uses a few figures of speech again and again. Know them before you read, because extract questions often ask about them.

| Device | What it means | Example in the story |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | compares two things using *like* or *as* | the field looked white, *as if covered with salt* |
| **Metaphor** | says one thing *is* another, without *like* or *as* | raindrops are *new coins*; hailstones are *frozen pearls* |
| **Hyperbole** | deliberate exaggeration | "a plague of locusts would have left more than this" |
| **Irony** | the opposite of what we expect | the helpers are called *a bunch of crooks* |

**Quick check.** Is "an ox of a man" a simile or a metaphor? **Answer:** a metaphor. Lencho is called an ox, with no *like* or *as*: strong and hard-working like an ox.

**A house on a hill.** In a wide valley there was only one house, standing on the top of a low hill. From there one could see the river and Lencho's field of ripe corn, dotted with flowers that always promised a good harvest. Only one thing was needed: rain, a heavy downpour or at least a shower. Lencho knew his fields closely, and all morning he did nothing but watch the sky towards the north-east.

**The rain comes.** He told his wife that they were really going to get some water now. She was cooking supper and replied, "Yes, God willing." The older boys were working in the field and the younger ones were playing near the house. When the family sat down to eat, the big drops of rain began to fall, just as Lencho had predicted. Huge, mountain-like clouds rolled in from the north-east, and the air felt fresh and sweet.

**"New coins."** Lencho went outside only for the joy of feeling the rain on his body. When he came back, he said happily that these were not raindrops but **new coins**: the big drops were ten-cent pieces and the small ones were fives. He looked with great satisfaction at his ripe corn and its flowers, covered by a curtain of rain.

**The hailstorm.** Suddenly a strong wind began to blow, and along with the rain came very large **hailstones**. They really did look like new silver coins. The boys ran out into the rain to collect these "frozen pearls". Lencho was worried and said he hoped it would pass quickly. It did not. For a whole hour the hail fell on the house, the garden, the hillside, the cornfield and the whole valley. The field turned **white, as if covered with salt**. Not a single leaf was left on the trees, the flowers were gone, and the corn was **totally destroyed**.

**Nothing left.** Lencho's heart was full of sadness. When the storm was over, he stood in the middle of his field and told his sons that even a **plague of locusts** would have left more than this. The hail had left nothing, and they would have no corn that year. That night the family was full of sorrow. They felt that all their work had been for nothing, that nobody could help them, and that they would all go hungry.

**One hope.** Yet in all their hearts there was one hope left: **help from God**. Someone reminded the family that no one dies of hunger. All night Lencho thought only of God, who, as he had been taught, sees everything, even what lies deep in a person's **conscience**. Lencho was "an ox of a man", who worked like an animal in the fields, but he knew how to write.

**The letter.** On the following Sunday, at daybreak, Lencho wrote a letter to God. He wrote that if God did not help him, his family would go hungry that year, and that he needed **a hundred pesos** to sow his field again and to live until the new crop came, because of the hailstorm. He wrote "**To God**" on the envelope, went to town, put a stamp on it and dropped it into the mailbox.

**At the post office.** A postman, who also helped at the post office, found the letter. He had never seen such an address in his whole career. Laughing heartily, he took it to his boss, the **postmaster**, a fat and friendly man. The postmaster laughed too, but almost at once became serious. Tapping the letter on his desk, he said that he wished he had the faith of the man who had written it, a man who had started writing letters to God!

**The postmaster's plan.** So that Lencho's faith in God would not be shaken, the postmaster decided to **answer the letter**. When he opened it, he saw that answering it would need more than goodwill, ink and paper: it would need money. He did not give up. He asked his employees for money, gave part of his own salary, and made several friends give something "for an act of charity". Even so, he could not collect a hundred pesos. He was able to send only **a little more than half**. He put the money in an envelope addressed to Lencho, with a letter that was signed with just one word: **God**.

**Lencho receives the money.** The next Sunday, Lencho came a little earlier than usual to ask if there was a letter for him. The postman himself handed it to him, while the postmaster watched from his office with the quiet happiness of a man who has done a good deed. Lencho was **not at all surprised** to see the money, because he was so sure God would help. But when he counted it, he became **angry**. In his mind, God could not have made a mistake, and God could not have refused what Lencho had asked for.

**The second letter.** At once Lencho asked for paper and ink at the window. At the public writing-table he wrote a second letter, wrinkling his forehead with the effort of putting his ideas into words. He bought a stamp, licked it, and fixed it to the envelope with a thump of his fist. As soon as the letter dropped into the mailbox, the postmaster opened it. Lencho had written to God that only **seventy pesos** had reached him. He asked God to send the rest, as he needed it badly, but **not through the mail**, because the post office employees were "**a bunch of crooks**".

**Reading between the lines.** The key images and actions, and why they matter:

- **"New coins."** For a farmer, rain means a crop, and a crop means money. So Lencho sees money falling from the sky. The image shows how completely his family's life depends on this one field.
- **Frozen pearls and worry.** The boys run out to collect the hailstones; Lencho worries. Children see only the beauty of the hail, while the father knows what it will do to the corn. The contrast makes the coming loss sharper.
- **"As if covered with salt."** Salt on soil kills plants, so a field that looks salted looks dead. The simile shows total ruin, not just damage.
- **The locusts.** Lencho's comparison says that the hail was worse than the worst pest a farmer knows. It is exaggeration born of grief.
- **"An ox of a man."** Lencho is strong and works like an animal, yet he can write. This prepares us for the letter: a simple man who can still put his faith on paper.
- **The postmaster's laugh, then seriousness.** He laughs at the strange address, but then he sees the faith behind it and wishes he had that faith himself. His kindness begins in respect, not pity.
- **Signing "God".** The postmaster wants Lencho's faith to stay whole. If the letter were signed by a man, Lencho would learn that God had not answered. So the kind act has to stay secret.
- **No surprise, then anger.** Lencho is not surprised to get money, because he never doubted God. For the same reason he cannot believe God would send less. Someone between God and Lencho must have taken it, and the only people in between are the post office staff. His anger comes from his faith, not from greed.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (extract-based, 4 × 1 mark).** Read the extract (retold from the story) and answer.

> Suddenly a strong wind began to blow, and very large hailstones fell with the rain. The boys ran out to collect the "frozen pearls". For an hour the hail fell on the house, the garden and the cornfield, until the field was white, as if covered with salt.

(i) What does the writer call the hailstones that the boys ran out to collect?
(ii) Why did the boys run out while their father worried?
(iii) What does "as if covered with salt" tell us about the field?
(iv) Find a word in the extract that means "abruptly, without warning".

*Model answers.* (i) "Frozen pearls". (ii) The boys saw only how pretty the hail was; Lencho knew it would destroy the corn. (iii) The crop was completely destroyed; the field looked dead. (iv) Suddenly.

*Why this scores:* each answer is one exact line. In (ii) the answer gives **both** halves of the contrast, and in (iii) it explains the meaning instead of repeating the words.

**Example 2 (short answer, 2 marks).** Why does Lencho call the post office employees "a bunch of crooks"?

*Model answer.* Lencho had asked God for a hundred pesos but received only seventy. He believed God could neither make a mistake nor refuse him, so he concluded that the post office employees had stolen the rest. In fact they had given that money themselves.

*Why this scores:* it gives the reason in Lencho's mind (God cannot err, 1 mark) and the ironic truth (they were his helpers, 1 mark).

**Example 3 (short answer, 2 marks).** How did the postmaster collect the money for Lencho?

*Model answer.* He asked his employees to give money, gave part of his own salary, and persuaded several friends to give something "for an act of charity". Even then he could collect only a little more than half of the hundred pesos.

*Why this scores:* all three sources of money are named (1½ marks) and the shortfall, which matters for the ending, is mentioned (½ mark).

**Example 4 (long answer, 5 marks).** Give a character sketch of the postmaster.

*Model answer.* The postmaster is a fat, amiable man, and the story's true hero. He has a **sense of humour**: he laughs heartily at a letter addressed to God. But he is also **sensitive and thoughtful**. He quickly turns serious, admires Lencho's faith and even wishes he had such faith himself. He is **kind and generous**: to keep a stranger's faith alive, he gives part of his own salary and collects money from his staff and friends. He is **modest**, because he signs the letter "God" and seeks no thanks for his good deed. Finally, he is **determined**: when he sees that the reply needs money, not just ink and paper, he does not give up. The irony is that this good man is the one Lencho calls a crook. He shows that real goodness does not wait for praise.

*Why this scores:* five qualities, each in bold and each proved by an event from the story; a closing line that links him to the irony; about 150 words. Examiners look for "quality + evidence", not a retelling.

**Example 5 (a twist the board likes, 2 marks).** "Lencho showed not the slightest surprise on seeing the money." Why, and what does this tell us about him?

*Model answer.* He was not surprised because he was completely sure that God would answer him. It shows that his faith in God was total and unquestioning, almost childlike.

*Why this scores:* reason (1 mark) plus what it reveals about his character (1 mark). Many students give only the reason and lose a mark.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Why did Lencho call the raindrops "new coins"?**
Because rain would give him a good harvest, and the harvest would bring money. The big drops were ten-cent pieces and the small ones fives.

**Q. Why does Lencho call the post office employees crooks when they helped him?**
He does not know they helped him. In his mind God sent a hundred pesos and God cannot make a mistake, so the missing thirty pesos must have been stolen on the way. That is the irony of the story.

**Q. Were thirty pesos really stolen?**
No. The postmaster could collect only a little more than half the amount, and that is all he sent. Nobody took anything.

**Q. Why didn't the postmaster sign his own name?**
He wanted Lencho to believe that God had answered. A letter signed by a man would have shown Lencho that his prayer had gone unanswered, and his faith might have been shaken.

**Q. Why did Lencho not try to find out who sent the money?**
For him there was nothing to find out. God had sent it. The idea that a human being had helped never entered his mind.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Writing that the postmaster sent a hundred pesos. He sent **a little more than half**; Lencho counted **seventy**.
- Writing "snow" or "ice" for the hail, or "wheat" for the crop. It was **hail**, and the crop was **corn**.
- Saying Lencho was surprised, or grateful. He was **not surprised**, and he became **angry**.
- In extract answers, copying the line back instead of explaining it.

#### Check yourself

1. What did Lencho say would have left more than the hail?
2. What three things did the postmaster realise were not enough to answer the letter, and what else was needed?
3. Why did Lencho ask God not to send the money through the mail?

**Answers:** 1. A plague of locusts. 2. Goodwill, ink and paper were not enough; money was needed. 3. He believed the post office employees had stolen part of the money and called them "a bunch of crooks".

### Conclusion: what the story teaches

The story is full of **irony**. The postmaster and his staff gave their own money to protect a stranger's faith, yet the very people who helped Lencho are the ones he accuses of being thieves. Lencho's faith in God is complete and unquestioning, but he has no faith at all in his fellow human beings. The story shows that **faith is powerful**, that **kindness often goes unseen**, and that we should think carefully before judging others. It also makes us smile at Lencho's innocence, while respecting the goodness of the postmaster.

#### Foundation first: theme and message

The **theme** is the big idea a story keeps returning to (here, faith). The **message** is what the writer wants us to learn from it (here, that faith in God and faith in people should go together). A long answer on "what the story teaches" must name both, and prove them with events from the story.

**Why the irony works.** The story builds three layers of irony on one event:
1. **Situational irony:** the helpers are accused of theft.
2. **Dramatic irony:** we know the truth; Lencho does not, and never will.
3. **The postmaster's irony:** he wished he had Lencho's faith, and his own good act is "rewarded" with an insult.

Lencho is not a bad man. He is honest, hard-working and asks only for what he needs. His fault is that his faith has room for God but none for people. The postmaster's quiet goodness is the answer to that fault.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (short answer, 2 marks).** What two kinds of faith do we see in the story?

*Model answer.* Lencho has total faith in God; he writes to God and is sure of an answer. The postmaster shows faith in goodness; he acts to protect a stranger's belief, expecting nothing in return. Ironically, Lencho has no faith in people.

*Why this scores:* one kind of faith per mark, each with an action from the story; the last line adds the irony for a strong finish.

**Example 2 (long answer, value-based, 5 marks).** Lencho had complete faith in God but none in his fellow human beings. What does the story teach us about faith?

*Model answer.* *A Letter to God* shows that faith can give a person strength in the worst of times. After the hailstorm ruins his crop, Lencho does not lose hope; he writes to God and waits calmly for help. His faith is so strong that it moves the postmaster to act. Yet the story also shows the limits of a faith that does not include people. Lencho cannot imagine that a human being has helped him, so he insults the very men who gave him their own money. The postmaster, on the other hand, has faith in goodness: he quietly helps a stranger and wants no thanks. The lesson is that God often helps us through other people. True faith should make us grateful to them, not suspicious of them, and we should think carefully before judging anyone.

*Why this scores:* it gives the positive side of faith with evidence (2 marks), the limit of Lencho's faith with evidence (2 marks), and a clear personal message at the end (1 mark). About 150 words, in paragraph form.

**Example 3 (a twist the board likes, 5 marks).** Is "A Letter to God" a suitable title for the story? Give reasons.

*Model answer.* Yes, the title is very suitable. The whole story turns on Lencho's letter to God. It shows his simple, complete faith: he addresses the envelope "To God" and posts it like any other letter. The letter is what moves the postmaster, who is so touched by it that he collects money and replies in God's name. Lencho's second letter to God brings the story to its ironic climax, where he calls the helpers crooks. So the letter starts the action, keeps it going and ends it. The title also hints at the theme, faith, and makes the reader curious from the start: who would write to God, and who would answer?

*Why this scores:* a clear "yes" in the first line, three reasons tied to the plot (first letter, postmaster's reply, second letter), and one on theme. Examiners reward a clear stand with reasons.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is Lencho ungrateful?**
Not knowingly. He would have thanked God gladly. He insults the staff only because he cannot see that they were his helpers. His ingratitude comes from innocence, not from a bad heart.

**Q. Does the story have a happy ending?**
Partly. Lencho receives help (seventy pesos by his count, though not the full hundred) and his faith stays strong, so the postmaster's plan works. But the helpers are insulted, so the ending is bittersweet and ironic.

**Q. Will Lencho ever learn the truth?**
The story does not say. It ends with his second letter, so the irony is left hanging. That open ending is what makes us think.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Calling Lencho greedy. He asked only for what he needed to sow and to live until the next crop.
- Writing a long answer on the theme without any event from the story. Every point needs proof.
- Forgetting to give your own view in a value-based question. The last mark is usually for it.

#### Check yourself

1. Name the three layers of irony in the story.
2. What is the difference between the theme and the message of a story?
3. Give one reason why the postmaster is called the real hero of the story.

**Answers:** 1. The helpers are called crooks (situational); we know the truth but Lencho does not (dramatic); the postmaster who envied Lencho's faith is insulted by him. 2. The theme is the big idea (faith); the message is the lesson we draw (faith in God should include trust in people). 3. He gave his own money to protect a stranger's faith and sought no thanks.

## 3. Detailed Section Explanations

### Section 1 — The house in the valley and the hope of rain (Paragraphs 1–2)
- **What happens:** Lencho's house is the only one in the valley, on the top of a low hill. He can see the river and his field of ripe corn, full of flowers. The crop only needs rain. All morning Lencho watches the north-east sky. He tells his wife they will get water now, and she replies, "Yes, God willing." During the meal, big drops of rain begin to fall, and huge clouds approach.
- **Explanation:** The opening shows a **lonely, simple farming family** that depends completely on nature. The flowers on the corn "promised a good harvest", so hope is high. Lencho is an experienced farmer who "knew his fields intimately"; his prediction comes true. His wife's words "God willing" show the family's **faith in God**, which becomes the centre of the story.
- **Key points:**
  - The house: **the only one in the entire valley**, on the **crest** (top) of a low hill.
  - The field of **ripe corn** dotted with **flowers**, a sign of a **good harvest**.
  - The earth needed a **downpour** or at least a **shower**.
  - Lencho watched the sky towards the **north-east** all morning.
  - The **older boys** worked in the field; the **smaller ones** played near the house.
  - "Huge **mountains of clouds**" (a metaphor) came from the north-east.

### Section 2 — Raindrops like new coins (Paragraph 3)
- **What happens:** Lencho goes out just to feel the rain on his body. He comes back delighted and says the raindrops are **new coins**: big drops are **ten-cent pieces** and little ones are **fives**. He looks at his field, covered by a curtain of rain, with satisfaction.
- **Explanation:** For a farmer, rain means a good crop, and a good crop means money. So Lencho sees each raindrop as **money falling from the sky**. This metaphor shows his joy and how much his family's life depends on the harvest. The phrase "draped in a curtain of rain" makes the rain look like a soft cloth covering the field.
- **Key points:**
  - Lencho went out "for no other reason than" to feel the rain (a negative used for emphasis).
  - **Raindrops = new coins**: big drops = **ten cents**, little drops = **fives**.
  - **Draped** = covered (as with cloth).

### Section 3 — The hailstorm destroys the crop (Paragraphs 4–6)
- **What happens:** A strong wind blows and very large hailstones fall with the rain. The boys run out to collect them. Lencho hopes the storm will pass quickly, but the hail falls for **an hour** on the whole valley. The field turns white "as if covered with salt". Not a leaf remains on the trees, the flowers are gone and the corn is totally destroyed. Lencho tells his sons that a plague of locusts would have left more. The family spends a sorrowful night.
- **Explanation:** In a moment, joy turns to disaster. The hailstones look like **new silver coins** (a simile) and **frozen pearls** (a metaphor), but these "coins" bring ruin, not riches. The boys' excitement contrasts with Lencho's worry, because children do not understand the danger. The comparison with **locusts** shows that the hail was worse than the worst pest a farmer can imagine. This is the **conflict between humans and nature**.
- **Key points:**
  - Hailstones **resembled new silver coins**; the boys collected the **frozen pearls**.
  - Hail fell **for an hour** on the house, garden, hillside, cornfield and valley.
  - The field was **white, as if covered with salt**.
  - **Not a leaf** remained; the **corn was totally destroyed**; the flowers were gone.
  - "A **plague of locusts** would have left more than this."
  - "This year we will have **no corn**."
  - That night: "All our work, for nothing", "no one who can help us", "We'll all go hungry".

### Section 4 — Faith in God and the letter (Paragraphs 7–9)
- **What happens:** The family's only hope is help from God. Someone reminds them that "no one dies of hunger". All night Lencho thinks of God, whose eyes see everything, even what is deep in one's conscience. The next Sunday, at daybreak, Lencho, "an ox of a man" who can still write, writes a letter to God asking for a hundred pesos. He addresses it "To God", takes it to town, puts a stamp on it and posts it.
- **Explanation:** This section shows Lencho's **simple, complete faith**. He does not doubt for a moment that God will read his letter. He even explains exactly why he needs the money: to **sow his field again** and to **live until the crop comes**. The phrase "**an ox of a man**" is a metaphor: like an ox, he is strong and works very hard, yet he can write, which was unusual for a poor farmer.
- **Key points:**
  - The **single hope**: help from God.
  - God's eyes "see everything, even what is deep in one's **conscience**".
  - Lencho: "**an ox of a man**", working like an animal in the fields, but he **knew how to write**.
  - Wrote the letter the **following Sunday at daybreak**.
  - Asked for **a hundred pesos** to **sow his field again** and **live until the crop comes**.
  - Wrote "**To God**" on the envelope; put a **stamp** on it; dropped it in the **mailbox**.

### Section 5 — The postmaster decides to help (Paragraphs 10–11)
- **What happens:** A postman finds the letter and, laughing, shows it to the postmaster. The postmaster, a **fat, amiable** man, laughs too but soon turns serious. He admires Lencho's faith and decides to answer the letter so that Lencho's faith is not shaken. He collects money from his employees, gives part of his salary and asks friends to give "for an act of charity". He can collect only a little more than half of a hundred pesos. He sends it with a letter signed "God".
- **Explanation:** The postmaster is the **hero** of the story. His first laugh is natural, since the address is strange. But he quickly sees the **deep faith** behind the letter and respects it. His good deed is **selfless**: he gives his own money and asks nothing in return. He signs the letter "God" because he wants Lencho to believe that **God himself** has answered, so that his faith remains strong.
- **Key points:**
  - The postman had **never known that address** in his career.
  - Postmaster: **fat, amiable** (friendly and pleasant).
  - "What faith! I wish I had the faith of the man who wrote this letter."
  - Purpose: **not to shake the writer's faith in God**.
  - Needed more than **goodwill, ink and paper**: needed money.
  - Collected from **employees**, his **own salary** and **friends** ("for an act of charity").
  - Sent **a little more than half**; letter signed with one word: **God**.

### Section 6 — Lencho's anger and second letter (Paragraphs 12–15)
- **What happens:** Lencho comes early the next Sunday. The postman hands him the letter while the postmaster watches with **contentment**. Lencho shows **not the slightest surprise** at the money, but he becomes angry when he counts it. He believes God cannot make a mistake. He writes a second letter at the public writing-table, sticks the stamp with a blow of his fist and posts it. The postmaster opens it. Lencho says only **seventy pesos** reached him, asks God to send the rest, but **not by mail**, because the post office employees are **a bunch of crooks**.
- **Explanation:** This is the **climax** and the **ironic ending**. Lencho's faith is so complete that the money does not surprise him, but the same faith makes him sure that God sent the full amount. So, he thinks, someone must have stolen the rest, and he blames the **very people who helped him**. The blow of his fist shows his anger. This is the **conflict between humans** (Lencho against the post office staff, though they did nothing wrong).
- **Key points:**
  - Lencho came "**a bit earlier than usual**" the next Sunday.
  - The postmaster felt the **contentment of a man who has performed a good deed**.
  - Lencho showed **not the slightest surprise**, "such was his confidence".
  - He became **angry** when he **counted** the money.
  - Wrote with "much **wrinkling of his brow**" (it was hard for him to express his ideas).
  - Fixed the stamp **with a blow of his fist**.
  - "**Only seventy pesos** reached me … don't send it through the mail … the post office employees are **a bunch of crooks**."

## 5. Important Facts

### 5.1 About the story
- **Title:** *A Letter to God*; **author:** **G.L. Fuentes** (a Mexican writer).
- **Unit 1** of *First Flight*; a **short story** about **faith**.
- **Setting:** a lonely house on the **crest of a low hill** in a valley; a nearby **town** with a **post office**.
- **Main characters:** **Lencho** (farmer) and the **postmaster**; also Lencho's **wife**, his **sons**, the **postman** and the post office **employees**.

3. A strong wind brings **large hailstones**; the hail lasts **an hour**.

7. The **postman** shows the letter to the **postmaster**, who decides to answer it.
8. The postmaster collects money from **employees, his salary and friends**; sends **a little more than half**, signed "**God**".
9. Next Sunday Lencho gets the money, shows **no surprise**, but is **angry** after counting it.
10. He writes a second letter: only **70 pesos** reached him; the rest should not come by mail because the employees are **crooks**.

## 7. Themes / Messages

1. **Faith.** Lencho's faith in God is complete and unquestioning. The postmaster admires it and works to protect it.
2. **Kindness and charity.** Ordinary people, the postmaster and his staff, give their own money to help a stranger.
3. **Irony of life.** Those who help Lencho are called thieves; the good deed is misunderstood.
4. **Human against nature.** The hailstorm destroys a year's work in an hour.
5. **Human against human.** Lencho distrusts the post office employees.
6. **Hope in hard times.** Even after losing everything, the family keeps hope alive.
7. **Dependence of farmers on nature.** A farmer's whole year depends on the weather.

### 7.1 Irony in the story
**Irony** is when something happens that is the opposite of what we expect.
- The **postmaster and his staff** collect money out of kindness, but Lencho calls them **"a bunch of crooks"**.
- The postmaster signs the letter "God" to **keep Lencho's faith** in God, but Lencho's faith makes him **lose faith in people**.
- Lencho is **not surprised** to receive money from God, but he is **angry** that the amount is less.
- The **rain** that Lencho calls "new coins" brings **hailstones** that look like "new silver coins" and ruin him.
- The people Lencho trusts least (the employees) are the only ones who actually helped him.

### 7.2 Two kinds of conflict

| Conflict | How it is shown |
|---|---|
| **Human vs nature** | The hailstorm destroys Lencho's corn; the farmer is helpless before nature. |
| **Human vs human** | Lencho accuses the post office employees of stealing part of the money. |

### 7.3 Metaphors, similes and other devices

| Device | Example | What is compared / its effect |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphor | "huge **mountains of clouds**" | Clouds compared to mountains: their great mass and size. |
| Metaphor | Raindrops are "**new coins**" | Rain compared to money: rain means a good harvest and income. |
| Metaphor | Hailstones are "**frozen pearls**" | Hail compared to pearls: their shape, colour and shine. |
| Simile | Hailstones "resembled **new silver coins**" | Their size, shape and shine. |
| Simile | The field was white "**as if covered with salt**" | Shows how completely the hail covered the field. |
| Metaphor | "a **plague** of locusts" | Locusts compared to an epidemic that spreads quickly and kills; shows total destruction. |
| Metaphor | "**an ox of a man**" | Lencho compared to an ox: strong and very hard-working. |
| Negatives for emphasis | "Not a leaf remained", "not the slightest surprise" | Make the ideas stronger. |
| Irony | The helpers are called crooks | Surprises the reader; makes the reader think. |

### 7.4 Significance of the title
The title *A Letter to God* names the **central event** of the story: Lencho's letter. It also hints at his **simple, complete faith**, since only a person with such faith would write to God and post the letter. The title makes the reader curious: will God answer? The answer comes from the postmaster, which shows that God's help often comes **through kind human beings**.

### 8.1 Oral Comprehension Check

**Set 1 (after the hailstorm)**

**1. What did Lencho hope for?**
**Answer:** Lencho hoped for rain, a heavy downpour or at least a shower, because his field of ripe corn needed water for a good harvest.

**2. Why did Lencho say the raindrops were like "new coins"?**
**Answer:** For Lencho, rain meant a good harvest, and a good harvest meant money. So he compared the raindrops to new coins: the big drops were ten-cent pieces and the little ones were fives.

**3. How did the rain change? What happened to Lencho's fields?**
**Answer:** A strong wind began to blow, and very large hailstones began to fall along with the rain. The hail fell for an hour. Lencho's field turned white, as if covered with salt. The corn was totally destroyed, the flowers were gone, and not a leaf remained on the trees.

**4. What were Lencho's feelings when the hail stopped?**
**Answer:** Lencho was filled with sadness. He felt that a plague of locusts would have left more than the hail. He was worried that his family would go hungry because they would have no corn that year.

**Set 2 (after the postmaster's plan)**

**1. Who or what did Lencho have faith in? What did he do?**
**Answer:** Lencho had faith in God. He wrote a letter to God asking for a hundred pesos to sow his field again and to live until the next crop came, and he posted it.

**2. Who read the letter?**
**Answer:** A postman who also helped at the post office found it and showed it to the postmaster, who opened and read it.

**3. What did the postmaster do then?**
**Answer:** To keep Lencho's faith in God alive, the postmaster decided to answer the letter. He collected money from his employees, gave part of his own salary and asked his friends to contribute. He sent a little more than half of the amount to Lencho with a letter signed "God".

**Set 3 (at the end)**

**1. Was Lencho surprised to find a letter for him with money in it?**
**Answer:** No. He was not at all surprised, because he was so confident that God would help him.

**2. What made him angry?**
**Answer:** When he counted the money, he found it was less than the hundred pesos he had asked for (only seventy pesos). He believed God could not make a mistake, so he thought the post office employees had stolen the rest. This made him angry.

### 8.2 Thinking about the Text

**1. Who does Lencho have complete faith in? Which sentences in the story tell you this?**
**Answer:** Lencho has complete faith in God. These sentences show it: "All through the night, Lencho thought only of his one hope: the help of God, whose eyes … see everything, even what is deep in one's conscience." Also: "Lencho showed not the slightest surprise on seeing the money; such was his confidence." And: "God could not have made a mistake, nor could he have denied Lencho what he had requested."

**2. Why does the postmaster send money to Lencho? Why does he sign the letter "God"?**
**Answer:** The postmaster is moved by Lencho's deep faith. He does not want that faith to be shaken, so he collects money and sends it. He signs the letter "God" so that Lencho will believe God himself has answered his prayer, and his faith will remain strong.

**3. Did Lencho try to find out who had sent the money to him? Why/Why not?**
**Answer:** No. Lencho was completely sure that God had sent the money. It never occurred to him that a human being might have sent it, so he had no reason to find out.

**4. Who does Lencho think has taken the rest of the money? What is the irony in the situation?**
**Answer:** Lencho thinks the post office employees have taken the rest of the money. The irony is that these are the very people who collected the money and sent it to him out of kindness. Instead of thanking them, Lencho calls them "a bunch of crooks". The helpers are treated as thieves.

**5. Are there people like Lencho in the real world? What kind of a person would you say he is?**
**Answer:** Yes, there are people like Lencho, simple people with a strong faith in God who accept everything as God's will. Lencho is **naive** and **unquestioning**: he believes God will send money by post and never asks who really sent it. He is also **ungrateful**, though without knowing it, because he insults his helpers. He is not greedy or selfish, as he asks only for what he needs, but his innocence makes the ending **comical**.

**6. There are two kinds of conflict in the story: between humans and nature, and between humans themselves. How are these conflicts illustrated?**
**Answer:** The conflict between humans and nature is shown when the hailstorm destroys Lencho's crop. His year's hard work is ruined in an hour, and he is helpless. The conflict between humans is shown at the end, when Lencho suspects the post office employees of stealing his money and calls them "a bunch of crooks", though in fact they helped him.

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 marks each, 30–40 words)

**Q1.** Describe the location of Lencho's house.
**Answer:** Lencho's house was the only one in the entire valley. It stood on the crest (top) of a low hill, from where one could see the river and the field of ripe corn dotted with flowers.

**Q2.** What did the earth need, and why was Lencho watching the sky?
**Answer:** The earth needed a downpour or at least a shower for the ripe corn. Lencho was watching the sky towards the north-east all morning because he expected rain clouds to come from there.

**Q3.** Why did Lencho go out in the rain?
**Answer:** He went out for no other reason than to have the pleasure of feeling the rain on his body. He was delighted because the rain promised a good harvest.

**Q4.** Why did Lencho compare the raindrops to new coins?
**Answer:** Rain meant a good harvest, and a good harvest meant money for his family. So he called the big drops ten-cent pieces and the little ones fives.

**Q5.** How did the boys react to the hailstorm, and why?
**Answer:** The boys ran out in the rain to collect the hailstones, the "frozen pearls". They were children and did not understand that the hail would destroy the crop.

**Q6.** Why was Lencho's soul filled with sorrow?
**Answer:** The hailstorm, which lasted an hour, totally destroyed his corn. Not a leaf remained on the trees, and the flowers were gone. His whole year's work was lost, and his family would go hungry.

**Q7.** What did Lencho mean when he said "A plague of locusts would have left more than this"?
**Answer:** He meant that the hailstorm was worse than even a swarm of locusts, which destroys crops. Locusts would have left something, but the hail left nothing at all.

**Q8.** What was the family's single hope after the storm?
**Answer:** Their single hope was help from God. Lencho believed that God sees everything, even what is deep in one's conscience, and would not let them go hungry.

**Q9.** Why is Lencho called "an ox of a man"?
**Answer:** He is called "an ox of a man" because he was strong and worked like an animal in the fields. The metaphor shows his hard work and strength, though he also knew how to write.

**Q10.** What did Lencho write in his first letter to God?
**Answer:** He wrote that if God did not help him, his family would go hungry that year. He asked for a hundred pesos to sow his field again and to live until the crop came, because of the hailstorm.

**Q11.** How did the postman react when he saw Lencho's letter?
**Answer:** He laughed heartily and took the letter to his boss, the postmaster. Never in his career as a postman had he seen a letter addressed to God.

**Q12.** How did the postmaster's reaction change when he saw the letter?
**Answer:** At first the postmaster laughed, but almost at once he became serious. He admired the writer's faith and said he wished he had the faith of the man who wrote it.

**Q13.** Why did the postmaster decide to answer Lencho's letter?
**Answer:** He did not want to shake Lencho's faith in God. He felt that such strong faith deserved an answer, so he decided to reply and send money.

**Q14.** How did the postmaster collect the money for Lencho?
**Answer:** He asked his employees for money, gave part of his own salary, and made several friends give something "for an act of charity".

**Q15.** Why could the postmaster not send the full amount?
**Answer:** Even after collecting from his employees, his friends and his own salary, it was impossible to gather a hundred pesos. So he sent only a little more than half.

**Q16.** Why did the postmaster sign the letter "God"?
**Answer:** He wanted Lencho to believe that God himself had answered his prayer, so that his faith would stay strong. He did not want any credit for the good deed.

**Q17.** Why was Lencho not surprised to see the money?
**Answer:** He had complete confidence that God would answer his prayer. For him, receiving money from God was natural, so he showed not the slightest surprise.

**Q18.** Why did Lencho become angry when he counted the money?
**Answer:** The money was less than the hundred pesos he had asked for. He believed God could not make a mistake or refuse him, so he thought someone had stolen part of it.

**Q19.** Did Lencho try to find out who had sent him the money? Why?
**Answer:** No. He was certain that God had sent it, so it never occurred to him that a person might have sent it. He had no reason to enquire.

**Q20.** What did Lencho say in his second letter?
**Answer:** He told God that only seventy pesos had reached him and asked God to send the rest, as he needed it badly. He asked God not to send it by mail, because the post office employees were crooks.

**Q21.** Whom did Lencho suspect of taking the rest of the money, and why is this ironic?
**Answer:** He suspected the post office employees. It is ironic because they were the very people who had kindly collected and sent the money to him.

**Q22.** What does the way Lencho fixed the stamp on the second letter show?
**Answer:** He fixed the stamp with a blow of his fist. This shows how angry he was, because he believed the post office employees had stolen part of God's money.

**Q23.** What are the two kinds of conflict in the story?
**Answer:** The first is between humans and nature: the hailstorm destroys Lencho's crop. The second is between humans: Lencho accuses the post office employees of stealing his money.

**Q24.** What is the significance of the title *A Letter to God*?
**Answer:** The title names the central event, Lencho's letter, and shows his simple, complete faith. It also makes the reader curious about how a letter to God could be answered.

**Q25.** What do the words of Lencho's wife, "Yes, God willing", tell us about her?
**Answer:** They show that she, like Lencho, had faith in God. She believed that the rain, and everything else, depended on God's will.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (5 marks each, 100–150 words)

**Q1.** Describe how the rain turned into a disaster for Lencho.
**Answer:** Lencho's field of ripe corn needed only rain. He watched the north-east sky all morning, and during the meal big drops of rain began to fall. Lencho was overjoyed. He went out to feel the rain and called the raindrops "new coins": ten-cent pieces and fives. He looked at his field with satisfaction.

Suddenly a strong wind began to blow, and very large hailstones fell with the rain. The boys ran out to collect these "frozen pearls", but Lencho grew worried. The hail did not stop quickly. For an hour it fell on the house, garden, hillside, cornfield and the whole valley. The field turned white, as if covered with salt. Not a leaf remained on the trees, the flowers were gone and the corn was totally destroyed. The rain that Lencho had welcomed as money had ruined him completely.

**Q2.** Draw a character sketch of Lencho.
**Answer:** Lencho is a poor farmer who lives with his family in the only house in a valley. He is extremely **hard-working**: the writer calls him "an ox of a man", working like an animal in the fields. He is also an **experienced** farmer who knows his fields intimately and correctly predicts the rain. Though poor, he **knows how to write**.

His most striking quality is his **complete, unquestioning faith in God**. After the hailstorm destroys his crop, he writes a letter to God asking for a hundred pesos and posts it. He shows no surprise when the money arrives. However, he is also **naive** and **quick to suspect** people. When he finds only seventy pesos, he believes the post office employees have stolen the rest and calls them "a bunch of crooks". Lencho is not greedy, since he asks only for what he needs, but his innocence makes him unknowingly **ungrateful** to his helpers.

**Q3.** Draw a character sketch of the postmaster.
**Answer:** The postmaster is described as "a fat, amiable fellow", friendly and good-humoured. When the postman shows him Lencho's letter, he laughs at first, which is natural. But he quickly becomes serious and **respects** the writer's faith, saying, "I wish I had the faith of the man who wrote this letter."

He is **kind** and **generous**. To keep Lencho's faith in God alive, he decides to answer the letter. He collects money from his employees, gives part of his own salary and persuades his friends to give for "an act of charity". He is **determined**: he sticks to his resolution even though he cannot collect the full hundred pesos. He is also **humble** and **thoughtful**: he signs the letter "God" and wants no credit. He feels the quiet contentment of a man who has done a good deed. Sadly, he and his staff are later called "crooks" by Lencho. The postmaster is the true hero of the story.

**Q4.** What is the irony in the story *A Letter to God*?
**Answer:** Irony is a situation in which the result is the opposite of what is expected. The main irony of the story lies in its ending. The postmaster and his employees, moved by Lencho's faith, collected money from their own pockets to help him. The postmaster even signed the letter "God" so that Lencho's faith would not be shaken.

We expect Lencho to be grateful. Instead, he counts the money, finds only seventy pesos, and becomes angry. He writes to God asking for the rest, but not by mail, because the post office employees are "a bunch of crooks". So the very people who helped him are accused of stealing from him.

There is further irony: the rain Lencho welcomed as "new coins" turns into hailstones that look like "new silver coins" and destroy him. And Lencho's perfect faith in God makes him lose faith in the human beings through whom God's help actually came.

**Q5.** "There are two kinds of conflict in the story." Explain with examples.
**Answer:** The first conflict is between **humans and nature**. Lencho depends entirely on nature for his living. Rain, which he longs for, comes at first as a blessing, but a strong wind and a hailstorm follow. In one hour the hail destroys his entire crop of corn, leaving the field white as if covered with salt. Lencho is helpless against the power of nature, and his family faces hunger.

The second conflict is between **humans themselves**. The postmaster and his staff help Lencho by collecting money and sending it in God's name. But Lencho, not knowing this, counts the money and suspects that the post office employees have stolen part of it. He calls them "a bunch of crooks" in his second letter. This conflict exists only in Lencho's mind, since the employees are innocent, which makes the ending ironic. Together, the two conflicts show how hard a poor farmer's life is, and how easily goodness can be misunderstood.

**Q6.** Lencho had faith in God but not in human beings. Discuss.
**Answer:** Lencho's faith in God is complete. After the hailstorm, he believes God sees everything and will not let his family starve. He writes a letter to God, posts it like any ordinary letter, and returns the next Sunday expecting a reply. When he receives the money, he shows "not the slightest surprise".

But Lencho has very little faith in people. When he counts the money and finds only seventy pesos, he cannot imagine that God made a mistake. Instead, he immediately concludes that the post office employees are thieves and calls them "a bunch of crooks". He never thinks that humans might have helped him.

This is the central irony of the story. God's help actually came through human kindness: the postmaster, his employees and his friends. Lencho's blind faith in God and his distrust of people make him insult the very persons who saved his family. The story suggests that faith in God should also include faith in the goodness of people.

**Q7.** Narrate the role of the post office and its staff in the story.
**Answer:** The post office links the two halves of the story. Lencho posts his letter "To God" there. A postman, who had never seen such an address in his career, laughs and shows it to the postmaster. The postmaster, a fat, friendly man, laughs too but soon admires Lencho's faith.

To protect that faith, the postmaster decides to answer the letter. He collects money from his employees, gives part of his own salary and asks friends to contribute "for an act of charity". He manages to send a little more than half of a hundred pesos, signed "God". The next Sunday, the postman hands Lencho the letter while the postmaster watches with contentment.

Ironically, Lencho believes the post office staff have stolen part of the money. In his second letter, which the postmaster opens at once, he calls them "a bunch of crooks". The staff are the true helpers in the story, yet they receive only blame.

**Q8.** "Are there people like Lencho in the real world?" Give your opinion with reasons.
**Answer:** Yes, there are people like Lencho in the real world. In villages across India, including Meghalaya, many farmers depend completely on the rain and on their faith in God. When a hailstorm, flood or drought destroys their crops, they pray and hope for help, just as Lencho did.

Such people are often **simple, honest and hard-working**. Like Lencho, they may be **naive**: they trust completely in God and do not think about how help actually arrives. Sometimes, like him, they may also be quick to **suspect** others, especially officials, when things go wrong.

Lencho is not greedy or selfish, as he asks only for what he needs to survive. But his innocence makes him unknowingly **ungrateful** to the postmaster and his staff. The story reminds us to respect such simple faith, and also to recognise and thank the people through whom help reaches us.

**Q9.** Imagine you are the postmaster. Write a diary entry on the day you read Lencho's second letter.
**Answer:**
*Sunday, evening*
What a strange day! This morning I watched the farmer, Lencho, receive the money we had collected. I felt so happy, as if I had done the best deed of my life. He did not look surprised at all; such is his faith.

Then he wrote another letter and stuck the stamp on with his fist. I opened it at once, and I could not believe my eyes. He has asked God for the rest of the money, but not through the mail, because, he says, we post office employees are "a bunch of crooks"!

At first I felt hurt. My employees gave from their small salaries, and my friends gave out of kindness. But then I smiled. His faith in God is still unshaken, and that was all I wanted. Perhaps I should not be thanked; the good deed was meant for God's account, not mine.

### Case 3 — Seventy pesos

> Lencho was not the least bit surprised to find money in the envelope, so sure was he of God's help. But when he counted it, his face darkened with anger. God could not have made a mistake, he thought, and God could not have refused him. He asked for paper and ink and, frowning hard, wrote a reply. He bought a stamp, licked it, and thumped it onto the envelope with his fist.

**(i)** Why was Lencho not surprised to find the money?
**Answer:** He was completely confident that God would help him.

**(ii)** What made Lencho angry?
**Answer:** The money was less than the hundred pesos he had asked for.

**(iii)** What did Lencho believe God could not do?
**Answer:** Make a mistake, or refuse what Lencho had asked for.

**(iv)** What does the way Lencho fixed the stamp show?
**Answer:** That he was very angry.

### Case 4 — Who gave what? (data-based)

| Person or group | What they did for Lencho | What Lencho thought of them |
|---|---|---|
| God (in Lencho's mind) | sent the money | trusted completely |
| The postman | found the letter; handed Lencho the reply | did not know his role |
| The postmaster | planned the reply; gave part of his salary; signed "God" | did not know his role |
| The employees | gave money | "a bunch of crooks" |
| The postmaster's friends | gave "for an act of charity" | did not know their role |

**(i)** Who actually sent the money to Lencho?
**Answer:** The postmaster, with money from his employees, his friends and his own salary.

**(ii)** Which group did Lencho call "a bunch of crooks"?
**Answer:** The post office employees.

**(iii)** Why did Lencho not know the real role of the postmaster?
**Answer:** The postmaster signed the letter "God", so Lencho believed God had sent the money.

**(iv)** What does the table show about the story's irony?
**Answer:** The people who really helped Lencho are the ones he blamed, while he gave all the credit to God.

## 15. Revision Notes

### Story at a glance
- **Author:** **G.L. Fuentes**. **Unit 1**, *First Flight*. **Theme:** faith; kindness; irony.
- **Lencho** watches the **north-east** sky; rain falls during the meal; raindrops = **new coins** (big = **ten cents**, little = **fives**).
- **Strong wind** + **large hailstones** (like **new silver coins**, "**frozen pearls**") for **an hour**; field **white as if covered with salt**; corn **totally destroyed**; "a **plague of locusts** would have left more".
- **Single hope: help from God.** Lencho, "**an ox of a man**", **knew how to write**.
- **Sunday, daybreak:** letter asking for **100 pesos** to **sow again** and **live until the crop comes**; envelope "**To God**".
- **Postman** laughs; **postmaster** (fat, amiable) admires the faith; answers so as **not to shake his faith**; money from **employees, salary, friends**; sends **a little more than half**; signs "**God**".
- **Next Sunday:** Lencho shows **not the slightest surprise**; **angry** on counting; second letter: **only 70 pesos** reached; send the rest **not through the mail**; employees are "**a bunch of crooks**".

### Key words

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| crest | top of a hill |
| draped | covered (as with cloth) |
| locusts | insects that fly in big swarms and destroy crops |
| conscience | an inner sense of right and wrong |
| peso | currency of several Latin American countries |
| amiable | friendly and pleasant |
| contentment | satisfaction |
| crooks | dishonest people |

### Common traps

| Don't confuse… | Correct fact |
|---|---|
| Money asked for vs received | Asked for **100 pesos**; Lencho says **70** reached him; the postmaster sent "a little more than half". |
| Why the field was white | It was covered with **hailstones**; "as if covered with salt" is only a comparison. |
| Who found the letter | The **postman** found it; the **postmaster** answered it. |
| Who handed Lencho the money | The **postman**; the postmaster watched from his office. |
| Signature on the reply | One word: **God**. |
| Lencho's reaction | **No surprise** at the money; **anger** at the amount. |
| Raindrops vs hailstones | Raindrops = **new coins**; hailstones = **new silver coins / frozen pearls**. |
| Direction of the clouds | **North-east**. |

### Key themes in one line each
- **Faith:** Lencho's faith in God is total and unquestioning.
- **Kindness:** the postmaster helps a stranger quietly.
- **Irony:** the helpers are called crooks.
- **Conflict:** human vs nature (hailstorm); human vs human (Lencho vs employees).
