## 1. Complete Lesson

### How do we know a chemical reaction has taken place?

Milk turning sour, an iron nail rusting, grapes fermenting, food being cooked or digested and the respiration in our cells all change the **nature and identity** of the substances involved. Each is a **chemical change**, and the process is a **chemical reaction**.

**The idea in plain words.** In a **physical change** the substance stays the same substance: ice that melts is still water, and sugar dissolved in tea can be got back by evaporating the water. In a **chemical change** the atoms are rearranged and a **new substance** with new properties forms: once paper has burnt, you cannot get the paper back from the ash and smoke. So the real test is always: *has a new substance been made?* The signs below are the clues that tell us it has.

Three simple activities show the signs:

- **Activity 1.1 — burning magnesium.** A cleaned magnesium ribbon burns with a **dazzling white flame** and leaves a **white powder**, magnesium oxide, formed from magnesium and the oxygen of the air. The ribbon is first rubbed with sandpaper to remove the dull layer of basic magnesium carbonate that would stop it burning.
- **Activity 1.2 — lead nitrate and potassium iodide.** Mixing the two colourless solutions gives a **yellow precipitate** of lead iodide.
- **Activity 1.3 — zinc and dilute acid.** Zinc granules in dilute hydrochloric or sulphuric acid give off **bubbles of hydrogen**, and the flask becomes **warm**.

> **Signs of a chemical reaction:** a change in **state**, a change in **colour**, the **evolution of a gas**, or a change in **temperature**.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (basic).** Sort these into physical and chemical changes: (a) melting of wax, (b) curdling of milk, (c) dissolving common salt in water, (d) cooking rice, (e) a puddle drying in the sun.

*Solution.* Ask of each: is a new substance formed?
- (a) Physical — molten wax sets back into the same wax.
- (b) Chemical — curd has a different taste and texture, and cannot be turned back into milk.
- (c) Physical — boiling off the water gives the salt back.
- (d) Chemical — cooked rice cannot become raw rice again; the starch has changed.
- (e) Physical — the water has only become vapour.

**Example 2 (board level).** Name the sign of a chemical reaction seen in each: (a) a cut apple turns brown; (b) baking soda added to lemon juice fizzes; (c) quick lime added to water makes the beaker hot; (d) a candle's wax burns away.

*Solution.* (a) change in colour; (b) evolution of a gas (carbon dioxide); (c) change in temperature; (d) change in state — the solid wax burns to gases (carbon dioxide and water vapour), with heat and light given out.

**Example 3 (a twist).** "A change of state always means a chemical reaction." Is this true?

*Solution.* No. Ice melting and water boiling are changes of state, but no new substance forms — steam is still H₂O. A change of state points to a chemical reaction only when the product is a **different substance**, as when solid candle wax burns to form gases (carbon dioxide and water vapour). Always check for a new substance, not just one sign.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Why must the magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning?**
Magnesium left in air slowly gets a dull coating (basic magnesium carbonate). This layer does not burn and keeps oxygen away from the metal underneath, so the ribbon is hard to light. Sandpaper exposes the shiny metal.

**Q. Why does the flask feel warm when zinc reacts with acid?**
The reaction gives out heat. Reactions that release heat are called **exothermic** — you will meet the term later in this chapter.

**Q. If a gas is given off, is it always a chemical reaction?**
No. Boiling water gives off steam, and opening a soda bottle releases dissolved carbon dioxide; neither makes a new substance. In Activity 1.3 the gas is **hydrogen**, which was not there before — that is why it counts.

**Q. Is dissolving sugar in water a chemical change?**
No. The sugar is only spread out among the water molecules. Evaporate the water and the same sugar is left behind.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Calling every change of colour or state a chemical reaction. Look for a new substance.
- Writing "white smoke" for the product of burning magnesium. It is a white **powder**, magnesium oxide.
- Forgetting that the precipitate in Activity 1.2 is **yellow** (lead iodide), while the two solutions mixed are **colourless**.

#### Check yourself

1. Is the rusting of an iron gate a physical or a chemical change? Why?
2. Which two signs of a chemical reaction are seen when zinc is put into dilute sulphuric acid?
3. Why is the burning of a candle called both a physical and a chemical change?

**Answers:** 1. Chemical — rust (hydrated iron oxide) is a new substance that cannot be turned back into iron by a simple method. 2. Evolution of a gas (hydrogen) and a change in temperature (the flask gets warm). 3. The wax first melts and evaporates (physical change); the wax vapour then burns, forming carbon dioxide and water (chemical change).

### Chemical equations

#### Foundation first (from Class IX)

Writing equations needs the correct formula for every substance. Here is the Class IX background you need.

- **Atom and molecule.** An atom is the smallest particle of an element. A molecule is two or more atoms joined together: O₂ is a molecule of the element oxygen, and H₂O is a molecule of the compound water.
- **Reading a formula.** The small number after a symbol counts the atoms just before it. H₂SO₄ has 2 H, 1 S and 4 O. A number after a bracket multiplies everything inside: Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H.
- **Valency and ions.** The valency of an element or group is its combining capacity. In compounds such as salts, it appears as the charge on the ion. The ions you will use in this chapter and the next three:

| Valency | Positive ions | Negative ions |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Na⁺ (sodium), K⁺ (potassium), Ag⁺ (silver), H⁺ (hydrogen), NH₄⁺ (ammonium) | Cl⁻ (chloride), Br⁻ (bromide), I⁻ (iodide), OH⁻ (hydroxide), NO₃⁻ (nitrate) |
| 2 | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺ (copper(II)), Fe²⁺ (iron(II) or ferrous) | O²⁻ (oxide), S²⁻ (sulphide), SO₄²⁻ (sulphate), CO₃²⁻ (carbonate) |
| 3 | Al³⁺ (aluminium), Fe³⁺ (iron(III) or ferric) | — |

- **Writing a formula — the criss-cross rule.** Write the two ions side by side, then give each one the other's valency as its subscript. Cancel any common factor, and drop the 1s. Put brackets around a group of atoms when it needs a subscript.

**Example.** Aluminium oxide: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ → Al₂O₃. Calcium hydroxide: Ca²⁺ and OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂. Calcium oxide: Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ → Ca₂O₂, and cancelling 2 gives CaO. Ammonium sulphate: NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻ → (NH₄)₂SO₄.

**Quick check.** Write the formulas of (a) iron(III) chloride, (b) sodium sulphate, (c) lead nitrate.
**Answers:** (a) FeCl₃ (b) Na₂SO₄ (c) Pb(NO₃)₂

A sentence such as "magnesium burns in oxygen to form magnesium oxide" can be shortened to a **word equation**:

Magnesium + Oxygen → Magnesium oxide

The substances on the left (LHS) are the **reactants**; those on the right (RHS) are the **products**. The arrow points towards the products.

Using formulas gives a **chemical equation**: Mg + O₂ → MgO. Here the oxygen atoms are not equal on the two sides, so this is an unbalanced or **skeletal chemical equation**.

**Why use formulas at all?** A word equation tells us *what* reacts; a chemical equation also tells us *how many* atoms and molecules take part. That is what lets a chemist calculate how much of each substance is needed — and it is why an equation must be balanced before it is complete.

**Why O₂ and not O?** Oxygen in the air exists as molecules of two atoms, O₂. The same is true of hydrogen (H₂), nitrogen (N₂) and chlorine (Cl₂). Whenever these gases appear as elements in an equation, write them as two-atom molecules.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (basic).** Write the word equation and the skeletal equation for: "Hydrogen gas combines with nitrogen to form ammonia."

*Solution.*
Word equation: Hydrogen + Nitrogen → Ammonia
Skeletal equation: H₂ + N₂ → NH₃
(It is not yet balanced: there are 2 N on the left but only 1 on the right. We balance it in the next lesson.)

**Example 2 (board level).** Translate into a chemical equation: "Hydrogen sulphide gas burns in air to give water and sulphur dioxide."

*Solution.* "Burns in air" means it reacts with **oxygen** of the air, so oxygen is a reactant even though the sentence does not name it.
Reactants: H₂S, O₂. Products: H₂O, SO₂.
H₂S + O₂ → H₂O + SO₂ (skeletal)

**Example 3 (a twist).** Identify the reactants and products: "Barium chloride and potassium sulphate in water react to give insoluble barium sulphate and a solution of potassium chloride."

*Solution.* Reactants: barium chloride (BaCl₂) and potassium sulphate (K₂SO₄). Products: barium sulphate (BaSO₄) and potassium chloride (KCl).
BaCl₂ + K₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + KCl (skeletal)
The word "insoluble" will later tell us to write BaSO₄(s), and "solution" tells us KCl(aq).

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Why do we use an arrow and not an equals sign?**
The arrow shows the **direction of change**: reactants turn into products. An equals sign would suggest both sides are the same thing, which they are not.

**Q. Is a skeletal equation wrong?**
It is incomplete rather than wrong. It shows the correct substances but not the correct numbers. A balanced equation is the complete statement.

**Q. Where do I write a reactant that is "in the air"?**
On the left, like any other reactant. Burning, rusting and tarnishing all use oxygen from the air.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Writing O, H or N for the free gases instead of O₂, H₂, N₂.
- Putting a reactant on the right because it is named last in the sentence. Ask: is it used up, or made?
- Using "+" between a substance and its condition. Heat, light and electricity go on the arrow, not in the list of reactants.

#### Check yourself

1. Write the skeletal equation: "Calcium oxide reacts with water to form calcium hydroxide."
2. In Mg + O₂ → MgO, how many oxygen atoms are on each side?
3. Name the reactants in: "Natural gas (methane) burns in air."

**Answers:** 1. CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (it happens to be balanced already). 2. Two on the left, one on the right — so it is skeletal. 3. Methane (CH₄) and oxygen (O₂).

### Balancing a chemical equation

By the **law of conservation of mass**, mass is neither created nor destroyed in a reaction, so **the number of atoms of each element must be the same on both sides**. Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂ is already balanced (Zn 1, H 2, S 1, O 4 on each side).

**Why balancing follows from the law.** Every atom on the left must still be there on the right — only joined up differently. If one side had an extra oxygen atom, that atom would have appeared from nowhere, and the products would weigh more than the reactants. Balancing simply makes the equation agree with what really happens.

**Example — balance Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ (the hit-and-trial method).**

1. Treat each formula as fixed: we may change only the **coefficients** in front, never the subscripts (4H₂O, not H₂O₄).
2. List the atoms: Fe 1 | 3, H 2 | 2, O 1 | 4 (LHS | RHS).
3. Start with the compound with the most atoms, Fe₃O₄, and its most numerous element, oxygen: write **4**H₂O. Now Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂.
4. Balance hydrogen: 8 on the left, so write **4**H₂. Now Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂.
5. Balance iron: write **3**Fe.
6. Check every element:

3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (balanced)

**State symbols** make an equation more informative: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) aqueous. Here water reacts as steam:

3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g)

**Conditions** such as temperature, pressure or a catalyst are written above or below the arrow:

CO(g) + 2H₂(g) $\xrightarrow{\text{340 atm}}$ CH₃OH(l)

6CO₂(aq) + 12H₂O(l) $\xrightarrow{\text{sunlight, chlorophyll}}$ C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l)

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (basic).** Balance: H₂ + Cl₂ → HCl

*Solution.* Count: H 2 | 1, Cl 2 | 1. Both are short by one on the right, so write 2HCl: H 2 | 2, Cl 2 | 2.

H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (balanced)

**Example 2 (board level).** Balance, with state symbols: sodium + water → sodium hydroxide + hydrogen.

*Solution.*
1. Skeletal: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂
2. Count: Na 1 | 1, O 1 | 1, H 2 | 3 (one in NaOH, two in H₂).
3. Hydrogen is odd on the right. Make the H₂O even first: 2H₂O gives H 4, O 2 on the left.
4. Oxygen on the right must now be 2: write 2NaOH. Right-side hydrogen = 2 + 2 = 4. ✓
5. Sodium: 2 on the right, so write 2Na.

2Na(s) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H₂(g)

Check: Na 2 | 2, H 4 | 4, O 2 | 2.

**Example 3 (board level — a textbook question).** Balance: BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → BaSO₄ + AlCl₃

*Solution.* A group of atoms that stays together on both sides, like SO₄, can be counted as **one unit**. That makes the work shorter.
1. SO₄: 3 on the left, so write 3BaSO₄.
2. Ba: now 3 on the right, so write 3BaCl₂.
3. Cl: 6 on the left, so write 2AlCl₃ (6 Cl).
4. Al: 2 | 2. ✓

3BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → 3BaSO₄ + 2AlCl₃

**Example 4 (a twist — the odd–even trick).** Balance: C₂H₆ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O (the burning of ethane)

*Solution.*
1. C: write 2CO₂. H: 6 on the left, so write 3H₂O.
2. Oxygen on the right = 2 × 2 + 3 × 1 = 7. Seven is odd, but O₂ always brings oxygen in twos.
3. Double **everything**: 2C₂H₆ + O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O. Now right-side oxygen = 8 + 6 = 14, so write 7O₂.

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O

Check: C 4 | 4, H 12 | 12, O 14 | 14.

**Example 5 (a twist — use the LCM).** Balance: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃

*Solution.* Oxygen is 2 on the left and 3 on the right. The LCM of 2 and 3 is 6, so write 3O₂ and 2Al₂O₃ (6 O each side). That makes 4 Al on the right, so write 4Al.

4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

> **In the exam, write:** the skeletal equation, a short atom-count table (element | LHS | RHS), the balanced equation, and the state symbols if the question asks for them. In the SSLC papers a balancing item usually carries 1 mark for the final balanced equation, so recount every element before you move on.

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Why can't I just change H₂O to H₂O₂ to get more oxygen?**
Because H₂O₂ is a different substance — hydrogen peroxide, a bleach — not water. Changing a subscript changes **what** the substance is; changing a coefficient changes only **how much** of it there is.

**Q. Does a coefficient multiply everything inside the formula?**
Yes, including what is in brackets. 3Ca(OH)₂ has 3 Ca, 6 O and 6 H.

**Q. Must the coefficients be the smallest whole numbers?**
Yes. 4Na + 4H₂O → 4NaOH + 2H₂ has equal atoms, but every number can be halved, so the expected answer is 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.

**Q. Is "heat" written above the arrow a reactant?**
No. It is a **condition**. Heat, light, electricity, pressure and catalysts are written on the arrow, never added to the atom count.

**Q. Which element should I balance first?**
Start with the compound that has the most atoms and balance its most numerous element first, as with oxygen in Fe₃O₄. Leave free elements such as Fe, O₂ and H₂ for last. A free element can be fixed without disturbing anything else.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Changing subscripts instead of coefficients.
- Fixing one element and forgetting to recount the others; a new coefficient changes every atom in that formula.
- Leaving fractions such as 7/2 O₂ in the final answer — multiply through to whole numbers.
- Writing H₂O(g) when the water is a liquid, or forgetting that water reacts as steam with hot iron.

#### Check yourself

1. Balance: Mg + O₂ → MgO
2. Balance: Fe + Cl₂ → FeCl₃
3. Balance: KClO₃ → KCl + O₂

**Answers:** 1. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. 2. 2Fe + 3Cl₂ → 2FeCl₃ (Cl: LCM of 2 and 3 is 6). 3. 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ (O: LCM of 3 and 2 is 6).

### Types of chemical reactions

![Fig. 1.1 The types of chemical reactions](figures/sci_ch01_fig04_types_map.png)

In a chemical reaction, bonds between atoms break and new bonds form. Atoms are never created, destroyed or changed into atoms of another element.

**How to name the type — four questions.** Look at the equation, not just the story:
1. Is there **one product** made from two or more reactants? → combination.
2. Is there **one reactant** breaking into two or more products? → decomposition.
3. Is a **single element** reacting with a **compound** and taking its place? → displacement.
4. Are **two compounds** swapping partners? → double displacement.
Then ask separately: is **oxygen or hydrogen** being gained or lost? → redox. Is heat given out or taken in? → exothermic or endothermic. One reaction can belong to more than one group.

#### 1. Combination reaction

**Two or more substances combine to form a single product.**

**Activity 1.4.** Water added slowly to calcium oxide (quick lime) reacts vigorously and makes the beaker hot:

CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat
(quick lime → slaked lime)

> **Do you know?** A solution of slaked lime is used for whitewashing. It reacts slowly with carbon dioxide in the air, forming a thin, shiny layer of calcium carbonate after two or three days: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l). Marble is also CaCO₃.

More examples: burning of coal, C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g); formation of water, 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l).

**Exothermic reactions** give out heat along with the products — for example, the reaction of quick lime with water, the burning of natural gas, CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(g), respiration, and the decay of vegetable matter into compost.

> **Respiration is exothermic.** Carbohydrates in food are broken down to glucose, which combines with oxygen in our cells and releases energy: C₆H₁₂O₆(aq) + 6O₂(aq) → 6CO₂(aq) + 6H₂O(l) + energy.

**Doubt cleared — is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O a combination even though the "2" makes it look like two products?** Yes. The 2 counts molecules of the **same** substance. There is only one product, water.

**Why must water be added to quick lime slowly?** The reaction gives out so much heat that the water can boil and spit hot, caustic lime. Masons slaking lime for whitewash add the water a little at a time.

#### 2. Decomposition reaction

**A single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products.** It is the opposite of a combination reaction and needs energy as heat, light or electricity.

**Thermal decomposition (by heat).**

![Fig. 1.2 Heating ferrous sulphate crystals (Activity 1.5)](figures/sci_ch01_fig02_heating_ferrous_sulphate.png)

**Activity 1.5.** Green ferrous sulphate crystals (FeSO₄·7H₂O) lose their water on heating and then decompose, leaving brown ferric oxide and giving off gases that smell of burning sulphur:

2FeSO₄(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g)

Limestone decomposes to quick lime, which is used in making cement:

CaCO₃(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

**Activity 1.6.** Heating lead nitrate gives off brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide:

2Pb(NO₃)₂(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ 2PbO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)

**Electrolytic decomposition (by electricity).**

![Fig. 1.3 Electrolysis of water (Activity 1.7)](figures/sci_ch01_fig01_electrolysis.png)

**Activity 1.7.** When a current is passed through water containing a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid, bubbles form at both carbon electrodes. The gas at the cathode (−) is **hydrogen**: it burns with a "pop" when a lighted candle is brought near. The gas at the anode (+) is **oxygen**: it makes the candle burn more brightly. **The volume of hydrogen is twice that of oxygen**, because each water molecule contains two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom:

2H₂O(l) $\xrightarrow{\text{electric current}}$ 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)

**Photolytic decomposition (by light).** **Activity 1.8.** White silver chloride turns grey in sunlight as silver forms:

2AgCl(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{sunlight}}$ 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g)

Silver bromide behaves in the same way: 2AgBr(s) → 2Ag(s) + Br₂(g). These reactions are used in black-and-white photography.

> **Endothermic reactions** absorb energy (heat, light or electricity). Most decomposition reactions are endothermic. Mixing barium hydroxide with ammonium chloride in a test tube makes it feel cold — also an endothermic reaction.

**Doubts cleared on decomposition.**

**Q. Why do the green crystals first turn white in Activity 1.5?** The green colour belongs to the hydrated crystals. Heating drives off the water of crystallisation, leaving white anhydrous ferrous sulphate. Only on stronger heating does it decompose to the brown oxide and the gases.

**Q. Why add dilute sulphuric acid to the water for electrolysis?** Pure water conducts electricity very poorly. A few drops of acid supply ions so that a current can flow.

**Q. Why is the hydrogen exactly twice the oxygen?** The equation 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ gives two molecules of hydrogen for every one of oxygen. At the same temperature and pressure, equal numbers of gas molecules take up equal volumes, so hydrogen fills twice the volume.

**Q. Why are silver chloride and silver bromide kept in dark bottles?** Light decomposes them. A dark bottle keeps the light out.

#### 3. Displacement reaction

**A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound.**

![Fig. 1.4 Iron nails in copper sulphate solution (Activity 1.9)](figures/sci_ch01_fig03_iron_nails.png)

**Activity 1.9.** Iron nails kept in copper sulphate solution for 20 minutes become coated with brown copper, and the blue colour of the solution fades as pale green iron sulphate forms:

Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s)

Other examples: Zn(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu(s); Pb(s) + CuCl₂(aq) → PbCl₂(aq) + Cu(s). Zinc, lead and iron are more reactive than copper. The reverse reactions do not happen: copper cannot displace iron.

**How to predict a displacement.** Use the order of reactivity of metals (you will study it in detail in Chapter 3). The common ones, most reactive first:

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au

A metal displaces any metal **to its right** from a solution of that metal's salt. It cannot displace a metal to its left.

**Worked example (SSLC sample paper).** What happens when a strip of zinc is placed in iron(II) sulphate solution? Write the equation.

*Solution.* Zinc is to the left of iron, so zinc is more reactive and displaces iron. The pale green solution slowly becomes colourless (zinc sulphate), and a grey-black deposit of iron forms on the strip.

Zn(s) + FeSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Fe(s)

**Worked example.** Will a reaction take place? (a) Copper in iron(II) sulphate solution. (b) Silver in copper sulphate solution. (c) Magnesium in zinc sulphate solution.

*Solution.* (a) No — copper is less reactive than iron. (b) No — silver is less reactive than copper. (c) Yes — magnesium is above zinc: Mg(s) + ZnSO₄(aq) → MgSO₄(aq) + Zn(s).

**Q. Why does the blue colour fade in Activity 1.9?** The blue colour comes from copper ions in solution. As iron takes the copper's place, the copper ions leave the solution as brown copper metal, and the iron ions that replace them give only a pale green colour.

#### 4. Double displacement reaction

**Two compounds exchange their ions.**

**Activity 1.10.** Sodium sulphate and barium chloride solutions give a **white precipitate** of barium sulphate, formed from Ba²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions, while sodium chloride stays in solution:

Na₂SO₄(aq) + BaCl₂(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)

An insoluble solid formed in a reaction is a **precipitate**, and a reaction that forms one is a **precipitation reaction**. The yellow precipitate of Activity 1.2 is another double displacement reaction:

Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s)↓ + 2KNO₃(aq)

**Picture it as partners changing places.** Write each compound as two parts: Na₂ | SO₄ and Ba | Cl₂. The first parts swap partners: Ba joins SO₄, and Na joins Cl. That is the whole trick for writing the products.

**Doubt cleared — displacement or double displacement?** Count what is on the left. *One element + one compound* → displacement (Fe + CuSO₄). *Two compounds* → double displacement (Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂). The reaction of an acid with a base, NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O, is also a double displacement; you will meet it as **neutralisation** in Chapter 2.

#### 5. Oxidation and reduction (redox reactions)

**Activity 1.11.** Copper powder heated in a china dish turns black, because copper gains oxygen and forms copper(II) oxide: 2Cu + O₂ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ 2CuO. Passing hydrogen over the hot black oxide turns it brown again: CuO + H₂ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ Cu + H₂O.

> **Oxidation:** gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. **Reduction:** loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen. In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, copper oxide is **reduced** and hydrogen is **oxidised**. When one substance is oxidised and another reduced in the same reaction, it is a **redox reaction**.

More redox reactions: ZnO + C → Zn + CO (C oxidised, ZnO reduced); MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂ (HCl oxidised to Cl₂, MnO₂ reduced to MnCl₂). In Activity 1.1, magnesium gains oxygen, so it is **oxidised**.

**Why oxidation and reduction always come together.** Oxygen does not appear from nowhere. If one substance gains oxygen, another substance must have given it up. In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, the oxygen simply moves from the copper to the hydrogen — so one change cannot happen without the other.

**A method for redox questions.** Follow the oxygen (or hydrogen) across the arrow:
1. Find the substance that **gains O** or **loses H** → it is **oxidised**.
2. Find the substance that **loses O** or **gains H** → it is **reduced**.

**Worked example (a textbook question).** Identify the substances oxidised and reduced: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe

*Solution.* Aluminium becomes Al₂O₃ — it **gains oxygen**, so Al is **oxidised**. Fe₂O₃ becomes Fe — it **loses oxygen**, so Fe₂O₃ is **reduced**. (This reaction gives out so much heat that the iron melts; it is used to join railway tracks. It is also a displacement reaction — aluminium displaces iron.)

**Worked example (a twist with hydrogen).** Identify the substances oxidised and reduced: H₂S + Cl₂ → 2HCl + S

*Solution.* No oxygen here, so follow the hydrogen. H₂S becomes S — it **loses hydrogen**, so H₂S is **oxidised**. Cl₂ becomes HCl — it **gains hydrogen**, so Cl₂ is **reduced**.

#### Worked examples — naming the type

Name the type of each reaction. More than one name may fit.

| Equation | Type | How we know |
|---|---|---|
| 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | Combination (and oxidation of Mg) | One product; Mg gains oxygen |
| CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (heat) | Thermal decomposition | One reactant, two products, heat on the arrow |
| Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu | Displacement | Element + compound; Zn takes copper's place |
| AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ | Double displacement (precipitation) | Two compounds swap partners; white AgCl precipitate |
| CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O | Redox (also displacement) | CuO loses oxygen, H₂ gains it |
| 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ (sunlight) | Photolytic decomposition | One reactant broken by light |
| CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O | Exothermic combustion (oxidation) | Heat given out; carbon gains oxygen |

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is burning (combustion) a separate type?**
In this chapter, combustion is treated as an oxidation that gives out heat. Burning coal (C + O₂ → CO₂) is also a combination, because there is one product. Burning methane is not a combination — it gives two products — but it is still an exothermic oxidation.

**Q. Are all decomposition reactions endothermic?**
Most are, because energy is needed to break the reactant apart. That is why each one needs heat, light or electricity.

**Q. Can a reaction be both displacement and redox?**
Yes. Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe is both: aluminium displaces iron, and aluminium gains the oxygen that the iron oxide loses.

**Q. What does the ↓ arrow mean?**
It marks a **precipitate** — an insoluble solid that settles. An ↑ arrow after a product marks a gas that escapes.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Calling 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ a combination. One reactant breaking up is **decomposition**.
- Saying "copper displaces iron". The **more reactive** metal does the displacing.
- Naming the wrong substance as oxidised: it is the one that **gains oxygen**, not the one that loses it.
- Writing "white precipitate" for lead iodide. It is **yellow**; barium sulphate and silver chloride are white.

#### Check yourself

1. Name the type: 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ (heat).
2. Name the type: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃. What colour is the precipitate?
3. In ZnO + C → Zn + CO, which substance is oxidised?

**Answers:** 1. Thermal decomposition (one reactant, heat on the arrow). 2. Double displacement (precipitation); yellow lead iodide. 3. Carbon — it gains oxygen to become CO.

### Effects of oxidation in everyday life

- **Corrosion.** A metal attacked by moisture, air or acids slowly wears away. Examples: **rusting of iron** (a reddish-brown coating), the **black coating on silver** and the **green coating on copper**. Corrosion damages car bodies, bridges, iron railings and ships. Painting, oiling and galvanising slow it down (Chapter 3).
- **Rancidity.** When fats and oils in food are oxidised, their smell and taste change; the food has gone **rancid**. It is prevented by adding **antioxidants**, storing food in **air-tight containers**, and flushing packets of chips with **nitrogen**, an unreactive gas.

**Why iron rusts.** Rusting needs **both air (oxygen) and water** together. An iron nail in dry air, or in boiled water sealed under a layer of oil, does not rust; one left in ordinary tap water exposed to air soon does (you will test this in Chapter 3). Rust is a hydrated iron oxide — iron, oxygen and water joined together — which is flaky and falls off, exposing fresh iron underneath. That is why rusting keeps going until the metal is eaten through.

**Rust where you live.** Tin roofs, gates and bridges in Meghalaya rust quickly, because the air is moist for much of the year and places such as Sohra and Mawsynram get some of the heaviest rain on Earth. Near the sea, salt in the air makes rusting faster still.

**How each prevention method works.** Every method keeps air and water away from the iron:
- **Painting, oiling and greasing** put a coat between the metal and the air. Paint suits gates and roofs; oil and grease suit moving parts such as bicycle chains.
- **Galvanising** coats iron with zinc. Zinc is more reactive, so even if the coat is scratched, the zinc corrodes first and the iron is spared.
- **Chrome plating** gives a shiny, protective layer to taps and bicycle handlebars.
- **Alloying** — stainless steel, iron mixed with chromium and nickel, does not rust easily.

**How rancidity is slowed.** Oxidation goes faster in warm air and light, so food keeps longer in a refrigerator and in dark, air-tight containers. Chips are packed in **nitrogen**: it pushes the oxygen out of the packet, and it does not react with the oil. That is also why the packet is puffed up.

#### Worked examples

**Example 1 (board level).** Why are iron gates and window grills painted? (2 marks)

*Model answer.* Iron rusts when it comes in contact with both air (oxygen) and moisture. A coat of paint keeps air and moisture away from the iron surface, so rusting (corrosion) is prevented.
*Why this scores:* it names both conditions for rusting (1 mark) and says how paint stops them (1 mark).

**Example 2 (board level).** What is rancidity? Write two ways of preventing it. (3 marks)

*Model answer.* When fats and oils in food are oxidised, their smell and taste change. Such food is said to have become rancid, and the process is rancidity. It can be prevented by (i) flushing food packets, such as chips, with nitrogen, and (ii) keeping food in air-tight containers or adding antioxidants to it.
*Why this scores:* the definition (1 mark) and two clearly different methods (1 mark each).

**Example 3 (a twist).** Aluminium is more reactive than iron. Why, then, do aluminium utensils not get eaten away like iron?

*Solution.* Aluminium reacts with air to form a thin, tough layer of aluminium oxide that sticks firmly to the metal and stops further attack. Rust on iron is flaky and falls off, so it gives no such protection. (This is studied in Chapter 3.)

#### Doubts cleared

**Q. Is rusting the same as corrosion?**
Rusting is one kind of corrosion — the corrosion of **iron**. Corrosion is the general word for any metal being eaten away, such as silver turning black or copper turning green.

**Q. Why does gold not corrode?**
Gold is one of the least reactive metals. Air and water do not attack it, which is why gold ornaments keep their shine for centuries.

**Q. Does a refrigerator stop rancidity completely?**
No. It only slows the oxidation, because reactions are slower at low temperature. Food kept long enough will still go rancid.

**Q. Why nitrogen in chip packets, and not just air?**
Air contains oxygen, which would oxidise the oil. Nitrogen is unreactive, so it protects the chips — and the extra gas cushions them so they do not break.

#### Mistakes to avoid

- Writing that rusting needs "air **or** water". It needs **both**.
- Calling rancidity "rotting by bacteria". Rancidity is **oxidation** of fats and oils.
- Saying chips are packed in "oxygen" or "carbon dioxide". The gas is **nitrogen**.

#### Check yourself

1. Name the coating that forms on (a) silver, (b) copper, when left in air.
2. Why does galvanised iron not rust even when its zinc coat is scratched?
3. Give one reason why food kept in an open plate goes rancid faster than food in an air-tight box.

**Answers:** 1. (a) A black coating; (b) a green coating. 2. Zinc is more reactive than iron, so the zinc corrodes first and protects the iron. 3. The open food is in contact with more oxygen of the air, so its fats are oxidised faster.

### Summary of the chapter

1. A complete chemical equation shows the reactants, the products and their physical states.
2. Equations must be balanced, so that the atoms of each element are the same on both sides.
3. Combination: two or more substances form one product. Decomposition: one substance gives two or more products.
4. Displacement: one element displaces another from its compound. Double displacement: ions are exchanged; a precipitate often forms.
5. Exothermic reactions give out heat; endothermic reactions absorb energy.
6. Oxidation is gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen; reduction is loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
7. Corrosion and rancidity are harmful effects of oxidation.

## 7. Key Ideas and Real-Life Connections

### 7.1 Big ideas

- **Atoms are rearranged, never lost.** That is why every equation must balance.

### 8.1 Textbook questions — solutions

#### In-text questions (page 6)

**Q1.** Why should a magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning in air?
**Answer:** Magnesium reacts slowly with air to form a dull layer of **basic magnesium carbonate (and oxide)** on its surface. This layer stops the ribbon from burning easily, so it is rubbed off with sandpaper first.

**Q2.** Write the balanced equations for: (i) Hydrogen + Chlorine → Hydrogen chloride; (ii) Barium chloride + Aluminium sulphate → Barium sulphate + Aluminium chloride; (iii) Sodium + Water → Sodium hydroxide + Hydrogen.
**Answer:** (i) **H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl**. (ii) **3BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → 3BaSO₄ + 2AlCl₃**. (iii) **2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂**.

**Q3.** Write balanced equations with state symbols: (i) solutions of barium chloride and sodium sulphate react to give insoluble barium sulphate and a solution of sodium chloride; (ii) sodium hydroxide solution reacts with hydrochloric acid solution to give sodium chloride solution and water.
**Answer:** (i) **BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq)**. (ii) **NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)**.

#### In-text questions (page 10)

**Q1.** A solution of a substance X is used for whitewashing. (i) Name X and write its formula. (ii) Write its reaction with water.
**Answer:** (i) X is **calcium oxide (quick lime), CaO**. (ii) **CaO(s) + H₂O(l) → Ca(OH)₂(aq) + heat**; the slaked lime formed is used for whitewashing.

**Q2.** Why is the amount of gas collected in one test tube in Activity 1.7 double that in the other? Name this gas.
**Answer:** Water (H₂O) contains two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom, so electrolysis gives **two volumes of hydrogen for one volume of oxygen**: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. The gas with double the volume is **hydrogen**.

#### In-text questions (page 13)

**Q1.** Why does the colour of copper sulphate solution change when an iron nail is dipped in it?
**Answer:** Iron is more reactive than copper, so it **displaces copper** from copper sulphate: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. The blue copper sulphate is used up and pale green iron sulphate forms, so the blue colour fades.

**Q2.** Give an example of a double displacement reaction other than the one in Activity 1.10.
**Answer:** **AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)** (a white precipitate of silver chloride). Another: Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃.

**Q3.** Identify the substances oxidised and reduced: (i) 4Na(s) + O₂(g) → 2Na₂O(s); (ii) CuO(s) + H₂(g) → Cu(s) + H₂O(l).
**Answer:** (i) **Sodium is oxidised** (it gains oxygen); oxygen is reduced. (ii) **Hydrogen is oxidised** (it gains oxygen) and **copper(II) oxide is reduced** (it loses oxygen).

#### Activity questions

**Activity 1.2.** What is the colour of the precipitate formed with lead nitrate and potassium iodide? Name it and write the equation. Is it a double displacement reaction?
**Answer:** A **yellow** precipitate of **lead iodide**: Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2KI(aq) → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃(aq). Yes — the ions are exchanged, so it is a double displacement (precipitation) reaction.

**Activity 1.8.** What form of energy causes the decomposition of silver chloride and silver bromide? **Answer:** **Light** (sunlight).

**Barium hydroxide and ammonium chloride.** Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic? **Answer:** The test tube feels cold, so heat is absorbed: the reaction is **endothermic**.

**Group activity.** Potassium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, anhydrous copper sulphate and iron filings are added to beakers A, B, C (water) and D (copper sulphate solution). **Answer:** The temperature **falls** in A and B (endothermic dissolving) and **rises** in C and D (exothermic: copper sulphate taking up water, and iron displacing copper).

#### Exercises

**Q1.** Which statements about 2PbO(s) + C(s) → 2Pb(s) + CO₂(g) are incorrect? (a) Lead is getting reduced. (b) Carbon dioxide is getting oxidised. (c) Carbon is getting oxidised. (d) Lead oxide is getting reduced. (i) (a) and (b) (ii) (a) and (c) (iii) (a), (b) and (c) (iv) all
**Answer:** **(i) (a) and (b)**. It is lead **oxide** that is reduced, not lead, and CO₂ is a product, not something being oxidised. Statements (c) and (d) are correct.

**Q2.** Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe is an example of a (a) combination reaction (b) double displacement reaction (c) decomposition reaction (d) displacement reaction.
**Answer:** **(d) displacement reaction** — aluminium displaces iron from iron oxide. (It is also a redox reaction.)

**Q3.** What happens when dilute hydrochloric acid is added to iron filings? (a) Hydrogen gas and iron chloride are produced. (b) Chlorine gas and iron hydroxide are produced. (c) No reaction takes place. (d) Iron salt and water are produced.
**Answer:** **(a)** — Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂.

**Q4.** What is a balanced chemical equation? Why should chemical equations be balanced?
**Answer:** An equation in which **the number of atoms of each element is the same on both sides**. It must be balanced because of the **law of conservation of mass**: atoms are neither created nor destroyed, so the total mass of the products equals that of the reactants.

**Q5.** Translate into balanced equations: (a) hydrogen combines with nitrogen to form ammonia; (b) hydrogen sulphide burns in air to give water and sulphur dioxide; (c) barium chloride reacts with aluminium sulphate to give aluminium chloride and a precipitate of barium sulphate; (d) potassium reacts with water to give potassium hydroxide and hydrogen.
**Answer:** (a) **3H₂ + N₂ → 2NH₃**. (b) **2H₂S + 3O₂ → 2H₂O + 2SO₂**. (c) **3BaCl₂ + Al₂(SO₄)₃ → 2AlCl₃ + 3BaSO₄↓**. (d) **2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂↑**.

**Q6.** Balance: (a) HNO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + H₂O; (b) NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O; (c) NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl + NaNO₃; (d) BaCl₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + HCl.
**Answer:** (a) **2HNO₃ + Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O**. (b) **2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O**. (c) **NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl + NaNO₃** (already balanced). (d) **BaCl₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2HCl**.

**Q7.** Write balanced equations: (a) calcium hydroxide + carbon dioxide → calcium carbonate + water; (b) zinc + silver nitrate → zinc nitrate + silver; (c) aluminium + copper chloride → aluminium chloride + copper; (d) barium chloride + potassium sulphate → barium sulphate + potassium chloride.
**Answer:** (a) **Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O**. (b) **Zn + 2AgNO₃ → Zn(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag**. (c) **2Al + 3CuCl₂ → 2AlCl₃ + 3Cu**. (d) **BaCl₂ + K₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + 2KCl**.

**Q8.** Write balanced equations and identify the type: (a) potassium bromide(aq) + barium iodide(aq) → potassium iodide(aq) + barium bromide(s); (b) zinc carbonate(s) → zinc oxide(s) + carbon dioxide(g); (c) hydrogen(g) + chlorine(g) → hydrogen chloride(g); (d) magnesium(s) + hydrochloric acid(aq) → magnesium chloride(aq) + hydrogen(g).
**Answer:** (a) **2KBr(aq) + BaI₂(aq) → 2KI(aq) + BaBr₂(s)** — double displacement. (b) **ZnCO₃(s) → ZnO(s) + CO₂(g)** — decomposition. (c) **H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g)** — combination. (d) **Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)** — displacement.

**Q9.** What are exothermic and endothermic reactions? Give examples.
**Answer:** **Exothermic** reactions give out heat: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat; CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. **Endothermic** reactions absorb energy: CaCO₃ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ CaO + CO₂; 2AgCl $\xrightarrow{\text{sunlight}}$ 2Ag + Cl₂.

**Q10.** Why is respiration considered an exothermic reaction?
**Answer:** In respiration, glucose from food combines with oxygen in our cells to form carbon dioxide and water, **releasing energy**: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Because energy is given out, it is exothermic.

**Q11.** Why are decomposition reactions called the opposite of combination reactions? Write equations.
**Answer:** In combination, two or more reactants form **one** product; in decomposition, **one** reactant gives two or more products. Combination: CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃; decomposition: CaCO₃ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ CaO + CO₂. Likewise 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and 2H₂O $\xrightarrow{\text{electricity}}$ 2H₂ + O₂.

**Q12.** Write one decomposition reaction each where energy is supplied as heat, light and electricity.
**Answer:** Heat: **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**. Light: **2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂**. Electricity: **2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂**.

**Q13.** What is the difference between displacement and double displacement reactions? Write equations.
**Answer:** In **displacement**, a more reactive element replaces a less reactive one from its compound: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. In **double displacement**, two compounds **exchange ions**: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl.

**Q14.** In refining silver, silver is recovered from silver nitrate solution by displacement with copper. Write the reaction.
**Answer:** **Cu(s) + 2AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag(s)**.

**Q15.** What is a precipitation reaction? Give examples.
**Answer:** A reaction in which an **insoluble solid (precipitate)** forms when two solutions are mixed. Examples: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ (white) + 2NaCl; Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂↓ (yellow) + 2KNO₃.

**Q16.** Explain (a) oxidation and (b) reduction in terms of oxygen, with two examples each.
**Answer:** (a) **Oxidation** is the gain of oxygen: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO; C + O₂ → CO₂. (b) **Reduction** is the loss of oxygen: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (CuO reduced); ZnO + C → Zn + CO (ZnO reduced).

**Q17.** A shiny brown element X turns black on heating in air. Name X and the black compound.
**Answer:** X is **copper**; the black compound is **copper(II) oxide, CuO**: 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO.

**Q18.** Why do we apply paint on iron articles?
**Answer:** Paint keeps **air and moisture** away from the iron, which prevents **rusting (corrosion)**.

**Q19.** Oil and fat containing food items are flushed with nitrogen. Why?
**Answer:** Nitrogen is unreactive and pushes out the oxygen, so the fats and oils are **not oxidised** and the food does not become **rancid**.

**Q20.** Explain (a) corrosion and (b) rancidity, with one example each.
**Answer:** (a) **Corrosion** is the slow eating away of a metal by air, moisture or acids around it — for example, the rusting of iron. (b) **Rancidity** is the oxidation of fats and oils in food, which spoils its smell and taste — for example, stale chips or old butter.

- (b) a complete equation

- (a) definite proportions
- (b) conservation of mass
- (c) multiple proportions

## 10. Short-Answer Questions (2 and 3 marks)

### Very short answers (2 marks each)

**Q1.** What is a decomposition reaction? Give one example.
**Answer:** A reaction in which a single reactant breaks down into two or more simpler products. Example: CaCO₃ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ CaO + CO₂.

**Q2.** What is a combination reaction? Give one example.
**Answer:** A reaction in which two or more substances combine to form a single product. Example: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.

**Q3.** Why should a magnesium ribbon be cleaned before burning?
**Answer:** To remove the dull layer of basic magnesium carbonate on its surface, which would stop it from burning easily.

**Q4.** What is a balanced chemical equation? Why must equations be balanced?
**Answer:** An equation with equal numbers of each kind of atom on both sides. It must balance because mass is conserved: atoms are neither created nor destroyed.

**Q5.** Balance: Na + H₂O → NaOH + H₂.
**Answer:** 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. Check: Na 2, H 4, O 2 on each side.

**Q6.** Why is the volume of gas in one test tube double that in the other during the electrolysis of water?
**Answer:** Water has two hydrogen atoms for each oxygen atom, so 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ gives two volumes of hydrogen for one of oxygen.

**Q7.** What is a precipitation reaction? Give one example.
**Answer:** A reaction that forms an insoluble solid in solution. Example: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ (white) + 2NaCl.

**Q8.** Give one industrial application of a combination reaction.
**Answer:** Quick lime and water form slaked lime, used for whitewash and mortar: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂. Ammonia for fertilisers is made from N₂ + 3H₂.

**Q9.** State the rule for a displacement reaction.
**Answer:** A more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound in solution. Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu happens; the reverse does not.

**Q10.** Define oxidation and reduction in terms of oxygen and hydrogen.
**Answer:** Oxidation: gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. Reduction: loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.

**Q11.** Identify the substance oxidised and reduced in CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O.
**Answer:** Hydrogen is oxidised (gains oxygen); copper(II) oxide is reduced (loses oxygen).

**Q12.** What are exothermic and endothermic reactions?
**Answer:** Exothermic reactions give out heat (burning of methane); endothermic reactions absorb energy (decomposition of calcium carbonate by heat).

**Q13.** Why is respiration an exothermic reaction?
**Answer:** Glucose combines with oxygen in our cells to give carbon dioxide and water, releasing energy: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy.

**Q14.** Why do we paint iron articles?
**Answer:** Paint keeps air and moisture away from the iron, so it does not rust (corrode).

**Q15.** Why are packets of chips flushed with nitrogen?
**Answer:** Nitrogen is unreactive and replaces oxygen, so the oil in the chips is not oxidised and does not turn rancid.

**Q16.** Write the reaction of silver chloride in sunlight. What type of reaction is it?
**Answer:** 2AgCl(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{sunlight}}$ 2Ag(s) + Cl₂(g) — photolytic decomposition; white silver chloride turns grey.

**Q17.** What do you observe when lead nitrate is heated? Write the equation.
**Answer:** Brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide are given off. 2Pb(NO₃)₂ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂.

**Q18.** A shiny brown element X turns black on heating in air. Name X and the black compound.
**Answer:** X is copper; the black compound is copper(II) oxide, CuO (2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO).

### Short answers (3 marks each)

**Q19.** What would you observe when zinc is added to iron(II) sulphate solution? What type of reaction is this? Write the equation.
**Answer:** The pale green solution fades and a greyish-black deposit of iron forms on the zinc.
Zinc is more reactive than iron, so it is a displacement reaction.
Zn(s) + FeSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Fe(s).

**Q20.** Why does the colour of copper sulphate solution change when an iron nail is dipped in it? Write the equation.
**Answer:** Iron is more reactive than copper and displaces it.
Blue copper sulphate is used up and pale green iron sulphate forms; brown copper coats the nail.
Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).

**Q21.** Write one equation each for decomposition by heat, light and electricity.
**Answer:** Heat: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.
Light: 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂.
Electricity: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

**Q22.** Distinguish between displacement and double displacement reactions with one equation each.
**Answer:** Displacement: a more reactive element replaces a less reactive one from its compound — Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
Double displacement: two compounds exchange ions — Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄ + 2NaCl.

**Q23.** Write balanced equations with state symbols: (i) barium chloride and sodium sulphate solutions react; (ii) sodium hydroxide solution reacts with hydrochloric acid.
**Answer:** (i) BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s) + 2NaCl(aq).
(ii) NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l).
Both are double displacement reactions.

**Q24.** A solution of substance X is used for whitewashing. Name X, write its reaction with water, and explain why the wall becomes shiny.
**Answer:** X is calcium oxide (quick lime), CaO.
CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat.
The slaked lime slowly reacts with CO₂ in the air to form a shiny layer of calcium carbonate: Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O.

**Q25.** What happens when ferrous sulphate crystals are heated? Write the equation and name the type of reaction.
**Answer:** The green crystals lose water, then turn brown, and a smell of burning sulphur is given off.
2FeSO₄ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ Fe₂O₃ + SO₂ + SO₃.
It is a thermal decomposition reaction.

**Q26.** Explain corrosion and rancidity with one example each.
**Answer:** Corrosion: a metal is slowly eaten away by air, moisture or acids — the rusting of iron.
Rancidity: fats and oils in food are oxidised, spoiling the smell and taste — stale chips.
Both are caused by oxidation.

**Q27.** Translate into balanced equations: (a) hydrogen + nitrogen → ammonia; (b) hydrogen sulphide burns in air to give water and sulphur dioxide; (c) potassium + water → potassium hydroxide + hydrogen.
**Answer:** (a) 3H₂ + N₂ → 2NH₃.
(b) 2H₂S + 3O₂ → 2H₂O + 2SO₂.
(c) 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂.

**Q28.** Write the balanced equation and type for: (a) zinc carbonate → zinc oxide + carbon dioxide; (b) magnesium + hydrochloric acid → magnesium chloride + hydrogen; (c) hydrogen + chlorine → hydrogen chloride.
**Answer:** (a) ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂ — decomposition.
(b) Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ — displacement.
(c) H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl — combination.

**Q29.** Explain oxidation and reduction with two examples each.
**Answer:** Oxidation (gain of oxygen): 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO; C + O₂ → CO₂.
Reduction (loss of oxygen): CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O; ZnO + C → Zn + CO.
In each redox reaction one substance is oxidised while another is reduced.

**Q30.** Why are decomposition reactions called the opposite of combination reactions? Give a pair of equations.
**Answer:** Combination joins several reactants into one product; decomposition splits one reactant into several products.
Combination: CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃.
Decomposition: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.

**Q31.** What is observed when silver nitrate solution is added to sodium chloride solution? Write the equation and name the reaction.
**Answer:** A white precipitate of silver chloride forms.
AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq).
It is a double displacement (precipitation) reaction.

## 11. Long-Answer Questions (4 marks each)

### Long answers (4 marks each)

**Q1.** Describe an activity to show the electrolysis of water, with a labelled diagram. Name the gases formed and give their ratio.
**Answer:** Fit two carbon electrodes through the base of a plastic mug, fill it with water containing a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid, and invert a water-filled test tube over each electrode.
Connect the electrodes to a 6 V battery and pass the current.
Bubbles form at both electrodes and displace water from the tubes.
Hydrogen collects at the cathode (−); it burns with a pop.
Oxygen collects at the anode (+); it makes a candle burn brighter.
The volume of hydrogen is twice that of oxygen: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (ratio 2 : 1).
(Draw Fig. 1.3, labelling the electrodes, the two gases, the water and the battery.)

**Q2.** Describe an activity to show thermal decomposition using ferrous sulphate. Write the equation and state the observations.
**Answer:** Take about 2 g of ferrous sulphate crystals in a dry boiling tube.
Heat it over a burner, holding the tube with a holder and pointing its mouth away from people.
The green crystals first lose their water of crystallisation.
They then turn into a brown residue, ferric oxide.
A smell of burning sulphur is noticed, from SO₂ and SO₃.
2FeSO₄(s) $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ Fe₂O₃(s) + SO₂(g) + SO₃(g).
A single reactant breaks into simpler products, so it is a thermal decomposition reaction.

**Q3.** Describe an activity to show that iron is more reactive than copper. Write the equation and name the type of reaction.
**Answer:** Clean three iron nails with sandpaper.
Take copper sulphate solution in two test tubes, A and B.
Dip two nails, tied with thread, in B for 20 minutes; keep A and one nail for comparison.
The nails in B become coated with brown copper.
The blue colour of B fades to pale green, while A stays blue.
Iron displaces copper: Fe(s) + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu(s).
This is a displacement reaction, so iron is more reactive than copper.

**Q4.** Explain the following types of reactions with one balanced equation each: (a) combination, (b) decomposition, (c) displacement, (d) double displacement.
**Answer:** (a) Combination: two or more substances form one product.
CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.
(b) Decomposition: one substance breaks into simpler products.
2Pb(NO₃)₂ → 2PbO + 4NO₂ + O₂.
(c) Displacement: a more reactive element displaces a less reactive one.
Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu.
(d) Double displacement: two compounds exchange ions.
Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃ (yellow precipitate).

**Q5.** What are redox reactions? Explain with the reaction of copper oxide and hydrogen, and identify the substances oxidised and reduced in ZnO + C → Zn + CO and MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂.
**Answer:** A redox reaction is one in which one substance is oxidised and another is reduced at the same time.
Heated copper oxide with hydrogen gives copper and water: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O.
CuO loses oxygen, so it is reduced.
H₂ gains oxygen, so it is oxidised.
In ZnO + C → Zn + CO, carbon is oxidised and ZnO is reduced.
In MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + 2H₂O + Cl₂, HCl is oxidised to Cl₂ and MnO₂ is reduced to MnCl₂.

**Q6.** Balance the equation Fe + H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + H₂ step by step, and then write it with state symbols.
**Answer:** Count atoms: Fe 1 and 3; H 2 and 2; O 1 and 4.
Balance oxygen first, using the largest compound Fe₃O₄: write 4H₂O.
Now hydrogen is 8 on the left, so write 4H₂.
Iron is 3 on the right, so write 3Fe.
Check: Fe 3, H 8, O 4 on both sides.
3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂.
With states (water as steam): 3Fe(s) + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄(s) + 4H₂(g).

**Q7.** What are exothermic and endothermic reactions? Give two examples of each, and explain why respiration is exothermic.
**Answer:** Exothermic reactions release heat.
Examples: CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat; CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
Endothermic reactions absorb energy.
Examples: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (heat); 2AgCl → 2Ag + Cl₂ (light).
Respiration is exothermic because glucose combines with oxygen in our cells and releases energy.
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy.

**Q8.** What is corrosion? Give three examples. Why is it harmful, and how can iron be protected? What is rancidity and how is it prevented?
**Answer:** Corrosion is the slow destruction of a metal by air, moisture or acids around it.
Examples: rusting of iron, the black coating on silver, the green coating on copper.
It damages car bodies, bridges, railings and ships, and replacing them costs a great deal.
Iron is protected by painting, oiling or galvanising, which keep air and moisture away.
Rancidity is the oxidation of fats and oils in food, which spoils its taste and smell.
It is prevented with antioxidants, air-tight containers, and by filling packets with nitrogen.

## 12. Competency / Application Questions

**Q1. (Whitewashing a house in Shillong)** A painter mixes *chuna* (quick lime) with water and the bucket becomes very hot. Name the reaction and explain why the wall looks shiny a few days later.
**Model answer:** CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat is an exothermic combination reaction. The slaked lime on the wall slowly reacts with CO₂ in the air to form a thin, shiny layer of calcium carbonate.

**Q2. (Cement from Meghalaya's limestone)** Why must limestone be heated strongly in a cement kiln? Write the equation.
**Model answer:** Heat is needed to decompose limestone into quick lime: CaCO₃ $\xrightarrow{\text{heat}}$ CaO + CO₂. It is an endothermic, thermal decomposition reaction.

**Q3. (Rusting tin roofs)** Iron sheets on houses in Sohra rust faster than in drier places. Explain, and suggest one way to protect them.
**Model answer:** Rusting needs both air and moisture; Sohra's very high rainfall keeps the iron wet. Painting or galvanising (a zinc coating) keeps air and water away.

**Q4. (Choosing a self-heating pack)** Which would you use to make a food pouch warm itself — ammonium nitrate and water, or calcium oxide and water? Why?
**Model answer:** Calcium oxide and water, because their reaction is strongly exothermic. Ammonium nitrate dissolving in water is endothermic and would make the pouch cold.

**Q5. (Spot the error)** A student balances H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by writing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂. What is wrong?
**Model answer:** The student changed a formula (a subscript), which changes the substance into hydrogen peroxide. Only coefficients may change: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

## 13. Assertion–Reason Questions

**Directions:** Read the Assertion (A) and the Reason (R). Choose the correct option:
- (a) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A.
- (b) Both A and R are true, but R is **not** the correct explanation of A.
- (c) A is true, but R is false.
- (d) A is false, but R is true.

**Q1.**
**A:** Chemical equations must be balanced.
**R:** Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** Conservation of mass requires equal atoms on both sides.

**Q2.**
**A:** In the electrolysis of water, the volume of hydrogen is twice that of oxygen.
**R:** Water contains two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

**Q3.**
**A:** Copper displaces iron from iron(II) sulphate solution.
**R:** Copper is less reactive than iron.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** A less reactive metal cannot displace a more reactive one.

**Q4.**
**A:** The reaction of quick lime with water is exothermic.
**R:** Calcium hydroxide is used for whitewashing.
**Answer:** (b)
**Explanation:** Both true, but R does not explain the heat released.

**Q5.**
**A:** Decomposition reactions are generally endothermic.
**R:** Energy is needed to break the reactant into simpler products.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** Heat, light or electricity is absorbed.

**Q6.**
**A:** In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, hydrogen is reduced.
**R:** Hydrogen gains oxygen in this reaction.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** Gaining oxygen means hydrogen is oxidised.

**Q7.**
**A:** Chips packets are flushed with nitrogen.
**R:** Nitrogen prevents the oxidation of fats and oils.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** It keeps oxygen out.

**Q8.**
**A:** The reaction of sodium sulphate with barium chloride is a precipitation reaction.
**R:** An insoluble white solid, barium sulphate, is formed.
**Answer:** (a)
**Explanation:** A precipitate forms.

**Q9.**
**A:** Burning of LPG is a physical change.
**R:** New substances, carbon dioxide and water, are formed when LPG burns.
**Answer:** (d)
**Explanation:** Burning is a chemical change.

**Q10.**
**A:** White silver chloride turns grey in sunlight.
**R:** Silver bromide is used in photography.
**Answer:** (b)
**Explanation:** Both true; the reason for A is the decomposition of AgCl into silver.

## 14. Case-Based Questions

*Read each situation and answer the four questions, 1 mark each (4 × 1 = 4).*

### Case 1 — Electrolysis in the school laboratory

> In a school laboratory in Tura, a teacher passes current through acidified water using carbon electrodes. Gas collects in two inverted test tubes.

**(i)** Name the gas collected at the cathode.
**Answer:** Hydrogen.

**(ii)** What is the ratio of the volumes of the two gases?
**Answer:** 2 : 1 (hydrogen : oxygen).

**(iii)** How would you test the gas at the anode?
**Answer:** It makes a burning candle or glowing splinter burn brighter (oxygen).

**(iv)** What type of reaction is this?
**Answer:** Decomposition by electricity (electrolytic decomposition).

### Case 2 — A blue solution that fades

> Ibalari dips two clean iron nails in copper sulphate solution and leaves them for 20 minutes.

**(i)** What happens to the colour of the solution?
**Answer:** It changes from blue to pale green.

**(ii)** What forms on the nails?
**Answer:** A brown coating of copper.

**(iii)** Write the equation.
**Answer:** Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu.

**(iv)** Name the type of reaction.
**Answer:** Displacement reaction.

### Case 3 — Whitewash and cement

> Limestone from the Jaintia Hills is heated in a kiln to make quick lime. Quick lime mixed with water gives slaked lime for whitewash.

**(i)** Write the equation for heating limestone.
**Answer:** CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.

**(ii)** Is this reaction exothermic or endothermic?
**Answer:** Endothermic.

**(iii)** Write the reaction of quick lime with water.
**Answer:** CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat.

**(iv)** Name the shiny substance formed on the wall later.
**Answer:** Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).

### Case 4 — Spoiled snacks and rusty gates

> A shopkeeper finds that an opened packet of chips smells bad after a week and that the iron gate of the shop has turned reddish-brown.

**(i)** What has happened to the chips?
**Answer:** They have become rancid (the oil is oxidised).

**(ii)** Why are fresh packets filled with nitrogen?
**Answer:** To keep out oxygen and prevent oxidation.

**(iii)** What is the reddish-brown coating on the gate?
**Answer:** Rust (hydrated iron(III) oxide).

**(iv)** Suggest one way to protect the gate.
**Answer:** Painting (or galvanising).

## 15. Revision Notes

### Chapter at a glance
- Signs of a reaction: change of state, colour, temperature, or a gas given off.
- Balance by changing coefficients only; add state symbols (s), (l), (g), (aq).
- Combination (A + B → AB), decomposition (AB → A + B, by heat, light or electricity), displacement (A + BC → AC + B), double displacement (AB + CD → AD + CB).
- Exothermic: heat out. Endothermic: energy in.
- Oxidation: + O or − H. Reduction: − O or + H. Redox: both together.
- Corrosion (rust, black silver, green copper) and rancidity are oxidations.

### Common mistakes

| Mistake | Correct idea |
|---|---|
| Changing subscripts to balance (H₂O₂ for water) | Change only coefficients: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. |
| Saying lead is reduced in 2PbO + C → 2Pb + CO₂ | Lead **oxide** is reduced; carbon is oxidised. |
| Oxygen at the cathode in electrolysis | Hydrogen at the cathode (−), oxygen at the anode (+). |
| Ratio of H₂ to O₂ as 1 : 2 | It is 2 : 1. |
| Writing Cu + FeSO₄ → CuSO₄ + Fe | No reaction: copper is less reactive. |
| Calling photosynthesis a decomposition | It builds glucose from CO₂ and water. |
| Forgetting that X for whitewash is CaO | The solution used is Ca(OH)₂; X itself is quick lime, CaO. |
